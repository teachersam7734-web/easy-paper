# Brainstorm bank: what to write about in a CC

Use this file in BRAINSTORM mode (the user wants ideas for strengths and weaknesses) and in WRITE mode before drafting (pipeline Steps 7 to 9). It generates candidate judgments; it does not license them. Every candidate must then pass the fairness filter (section 0) and be anchored to a page, section, figure, table, number or short quotation before it enters the CC.

How to use it, in order:
1. Name the genre and its job (pipeline Step 1). Pick the genre block in section 2.
2. Run the angle generators (section 1) over the paper's skeleton: purpose, promises, claims, choices, scope statements, figures, appendices.
3. Pull aspect prompts (section 3) for the four to six aspects the genre weighs most, and the field angles (section 4) that apply.
4. Write each hit as a one-line judgment with a location, marked + or -. Aim for six or more of each; cut to three or four strengths and three to five weaknesses.
5. Calibrate tone with section 5.

When presenting brainstorm output to the user, give a ranked shortlist, not the whole bank: for each candidate, the judgment, its anchor (or "verify: where to look"), why it matters for this paper's aim, and for weaknesses the better alternative.

## 0. Fairness filter (run on every candidate weakness)

From pipeline Step 8. In the three model CCs, eight weakness claims were contradicted or partly answered by the papers themselves.

1. **Is it really absent?** Search the item and two synonyms (the "one shot" design appears as "one-group posttest only"). If present, drop the point or change it to "treated only briefly (p. n)".
2. **Was it excluded on purpose?** Look for "for the purpose of this paper", "beyond the scope", "without being comprehensive", "subsumed under". If declared, acknowledge the scope; question it only by showing the paper's own aims need what was left out.
3. **Does the genre require it?** A forum piece owes an argument, not a literature review. A methods chapter has no participants.
4. **Would the intended readers miss it?**
5. **Is it about this paper, in this field?** Criticism of experiments in general, or of hospitals, is not criticism of an L2 methods paper.
6. **Can I name the alternative?** If the "could have" cell is empty, the weakness is not ready.

## 1. Angle generators: thinking moves a professional uses

Each move: the cue that triggers it, what to check, and a sentence frame for a strength (+) and a weakness (-). Frames are starting points in plain academic English; adapt them to the writer's voice and anchor every slot.

| # | Move | Trigger cue in the paper | What to check | Sentence frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Promise vs delivery | Title, abstract aim, "the goal of this paper is", "our goal is achieved if", "it is not our intention to" | Does the body deliver what the title and aims promise, by the authors' own success test? | (+) "The article meets its own test: readers come away able to X (Section n)." (-) "The authors state that they aim to X (p. n), yet the paper Y, so the promise is only partly kept." |
| 2 | Both-sides feature | A figure, worked example, exemplar study or tool that carries much of the argument | Is the same feature a strength in one respect and a limit in another? | (+) "Figure n shows rather than tells: it X." (-) "At the same time, the comparison in Figure n is not like for like, since X, so it shows Y more clearly than Z." |
| 3 | Internal-consistency gap | The paper raises a concern, threat or criterion, then later sections omit the tool or step that addresses it | Does the paper's own logic call for something it leaves out? Strongest form of critique. | (-) "The authors identify X as a threat (p. n), yet the design built to detect it, Y, is not discussed." |
| 4 | Should-without-how | Recommendations with "should", "researchers need to", "it is essential" | Is every "should" followed by a "how" (procedure, example, tool, threshold)? | (+) "Each recommendation comes with a concrete procedure, for example X (p. n)." (-) "The advice that researchers should X (p. n) is sound, but the paper does not show how, for instance through Y." |
| 5 | Choice and its cost | "We selected", "was chosen because", "we adopt", "we focus on" | Find the stated reason first. Then ask what the choice makes visible and what it hides. | (-) "The choice of X is justified (p. n), but that choice limits Y: readers see Z, not W. A second, contrasting X would show more." |
| 6 | Stake and disclosure | Authors recommend their own tool, test, framework, programme or earlier work; superlatives near it | Is the interest disclosed? Are rival options treated like for like? Are downsides given space? | (-) "Since X was developed by one of the authors, a short disclosure and a fairer account of when other tools suffice would make the case more credible." |
| 7 | Claim-evidence alignment | Causal verbs (improve, enhance, effect, impact, lead to), general claims ("learners benefit"), strong evaluatives ("superior", "the preferred") | Does the claim type (causal, comparative, evaluative) match the design and evidence? Does it upgrade between results, discussion, abstract and title? | (+) "The authors keep their claims associative, as the correlational design requires (p. n)." (-) "The results show X; the conclusion that Y causes Z (p. n) needs a design that rules out W." |
| 8 | Audience fit | Statements of intended reader ("novice researchers", "teachers", "reviewers"), density, undefined terms | Is the level, length and terminology right for the readers the paper says it serves? | (-) "The paper addresses novice researchers (p. n), but X is introduced without definition and Y in a single dense page; a summary table would help those readers." |
| 9 | Scope fairness | Declared omissions; a short genre (forum, chapter) | Is the declared scope reasonable for the genre and length? Which undeclared omission matters to the stated reader? | (+) "The selective treatment of X is declared (p. n) and reasonable for a chapter-length overview." (-) "Within that scope, one omission weakens the paper's own logic: X." |
| 10 | Currency of sources | Reference years, rules of thumb, statistics without citations, claims of "recent" work | Are classic and current sources both present? Is any rule of thumb unsourced? Is "recent" true? | (+) "The discussion draws on both foundational work (X, year) and recent syntheses (Y, year)." (-) "The rule that X (p. n) is given without a source; Y (year) would support or qualify it." |
| 11 | Practical transfer | Checklists, appendices, worked examples, code, templates, "implications for teachers" | Could a teacher or researcher act on this tomorrow? Is the practical output mentioned in your Summary? | (+) "Appendix n turns the argument into a checklist that researchers can apply when planning or reviewing a study." (-) "The implications for X remain general; one worked classroom example would show how Y works with intact classes." |
| 12 | What a sceptic would ask | Any central claim or recommendation | What is the strongest rival explanation or position, stated as its proponent would? What evidence would make the authors' reading less plausible? | (-) "A reader committed to X would ask whether Y; the paper does not address this, and a brief response would strengthen the case." |
| 13 | What is absent | "Empty rooms": no limitations section, no effect sizes, no reliability, no keywords, no worked example, no counterargument | Verify the absence (section 0). Rank it by consequence: missing effect sizes matter; missing keywords barely do. | (-) "The paper reports p values but no effect sizes or confidence intervals (Table n), so readers cannot judge the size of the differences." |
| 14 | Genre conventions | Abstract moves, keywords, headings, reporting standards (APA JARS, PRISMA), repeated passages | Does the paper follow its venue's conventions? Rank last; one sentence at most. | (-) "Finally, on a minor point, the abstract omits X, and a paragraph on p. n repeats the abstract." |
| 15 | Centre of gravity and proportion | Section lengths, where the thesis sits, notes on the paper's origin | Is space allocated by importance? Does the central section get the most care? | (-) "The central section on X receives one page, while Y takes four; the balance reverses the paper's own priorities." |

## 2. By paper genre

One-line prompts. Strengths to look for (+) and weaknesses to look for (-). Weigh the genre's job: evidence for empirical work, guidance for methodological work, argument for position pieces, synthesis for reviews, theory for theoretical work, usefulness and coherence for books.

### 2.1 Empirical quantitative (experimental, quasi-experimental, correlational, survey)
+ RQs and hypotheses stated clearly and answered one by one in Results.
+ Design matches the question (causal question, experimental design with comparison group).
+ Random assignment, or a careful account of intact-class equivalence (pretest, covariates).
+ A priori power analysis or a justified sample size.
+ Reliability reported for this sample, with the right index for the scoring type.
+ Effect sizes and confidence intervals reported with p values.
+ Delayed post-test, so retention is tested, not only immediate gain.
+ Treatment described well enough to replicate; materials or data shared.
+ Assumptions checked; multiple comparisons controlled.
+ Claims kept within the sample and the design.
+ Practical significance discussed, not only statistical significance.
- Small N or no power rationale (median power in L2 interaction research about .56 against the .80 convention [Plonsky]).
- Intact classes with teacher confounded with condition; clustering ignored in the analysis.
- "No significant pretest difference" read as "equivalent groups".
- No delayed post-test; immediate uptake reported as acquisition.
- Researcher-made test with no validity or reliability evidence; ceiling or floor effects.
- Treatment too short, or not described; control condition undefined.
- p values only; no effect sizes or CIs (few L2 studies report CIs [Larson-Hall and Plonsky 2015]).
- Causal language from correlational data; general claims from one context.
- Many outcome measures or subgroup tests with no correction; only significant results reported.
- Attrition and missing data not reported.
- Self-report as the only measure of a behaviour.

### 2.2 Qualitative (case study, interview, narrative, ethnography, discourse analysis)
+ Clear paradigm and a method that fits the question.
+ Purposeful sampling with a stated rationale; maximum variation where relevant.
+ Rich, contextualised data extracts that do evidential work, spread across participants.
+ Analytic procedure described well enough to follow (variant of thematic analysis, unit of coding, how codes became themes).
+ Reflexivity and researcher positionality addressed (essential for teacher-researchers).
+ Trustworthiness procedures: triangulation, member checking, audit trail, thick description.
+ Participant voice preserved; translation of data explained.
+ Transferability argued through description of context, not claimed as generalisability.
+ Theory used in the analysis, not only in the introduction.
- Thin extracts, or most quotations from one or two participants.
- Analysis that is only thematic summary; themes as topics, not interpretations.
- Coding process, coders and disagreement handling not described.
- Researcher role and power relations with participants unexamined.
- Saturation asserted without explanation.
- Claims of generalisability the paradigm does not support.
- Limitations written in positivist terms (sample size) rather than paradigm terms (transferability) [MLJ 2026 limitations study].
- Translated data with no account of who translated and how.
- Theory introduced and then dropped.

### 2.3 Mixed methods
+ Explicit rationale for mixing (complementarity, explanation, development).
+ Design named (convergent, explanatory sequential, exploratory sequential) with timing and priority.
+ Integration shown: joint displays, meta-inferences, one strand explaining or challenging the other.
+ Sampling linked across strands (follow-up interviewees chosen from the survey sample for a stated reason).
+ Each strand meets its own quality standards.
- Strands reported side by side with no integration (few high-integration studies in AL [Hashemi and Babaii 2013]).
- Qualitative data used only as illustration of numbers.
- Timing, priority or purpose of mixing unclear.
- Sampling misaligned across strands.
- Divergent findings ignored rather than discussed.
- Quantitative strand underpowered, qualitative strand thin, so neither carries the claims.
- "Mixed methods" claimed for a survey with open-ended items only.

### 2.4 Methodological or tutorial article (including methods chapters)
+ Accurate, current advice; the advice itself is correct.
+ Worked examples with real numbers that make abstract points tangible.
+ Field-specific illustrations (L2 scenarios, not generic ones).
+ A framework made operational through figures, tables or templates readers can reuse.
+ Practical outputs: checklists, decision trees, code, appendices.
+ Clear distinction between what is required and what is optional.
+ Common mistakes paired with remedies.
+ Cross-references to companion papers in the same collection.
+ Accessible language for the stated audience.
- Examples drawn from one subfield or one exemplary study only.
- Principles shown on exemplary practice, not on the weak practice the paper criticises.
- Recommendations without procedures ("should" without "how").
- Concerns raised but the tools that address them omitted (internal-consistency gap).
- No guidance on when not to use the method, or on its costs.
- Framework chosen without comparison with its main rival.
- Density beyond the stated novice audience; terms undefined.
- Rules of thumb without sources.
- No code, data or materials for the worked example.

### 2.5 Position paper, forum piece, commentary or response
+ Clear thesis stated early.
+ Fair, recognisable account of opposing views.
+ Claims supported by evidence or citations, not only assertion.
+ Productive reframing of a debate or a concrete proposal.
+ Concessions made where due (learning curve, costs, limits).
+ Demonstration rather than assertion (a figure, a worked case).
+ Readable, economical argument suited to the short format.
- Straw man of the opposing position, or quotations out of context.
- Absolutist stance; "crisis" or "the only way" framing.
- Superlatives without comparison ("superior", "the preferred package").
- Undisclosed stake in what is advocated.
- Counterarguments not addressed; no situations in which the alternative suffices.
- Evidence anecdotal or from one context.
- Circular argument; conclusion assumed in the premises.
- Promise of neutrality broken by the conclusion.
- Practical demonstration missing (claims about a tool with no example of its use).

### 2.6 Review, systematic review or meta-analysis
+ Search reproducible: databases, strings, dates, counts.
+ Transparent inclusion and exclusion criteria; PRISMA or equivalent flow.
+ Coding scheme with reliability for coders.
+ Study quality or risk of bias appraised, and tested as a moderator.
+ Effect sizes with CIs; heterogeneity examined; moderators chosen on theoretical grounds.
+ Publication bias assessed.
+ Synthesis that interprets patterns, not a list of studies.
+ Clear agenda for future research based on gaps found.
- Search not reproducible or outdated.
- Heterogeneous studies pooled without justification.
- Vote counting of significant results.
- Quality of primary studies not scrutinised.
- Publication bias unaddressed; grey literature excluded without reason.
- Moderators chosen after seeing the data.
- Same construct label covering different measures.
- Narrative review presented as systematic.
- Dependence among effect sizes from the same sample ignored.

### 2.7 Theoretical or conceptual article
+ Constructs defined and distinguished from neighbours (complexity vs difficulty).
+ Integration of separate literatures into a coherent account.
+ Testable predictions or clear empirical implications.
+ Boundary conditions stated.
+ Engagement with the strongest rival theory.
+ Assumptions made explicit.
+ A theory that explains a mechanism, not a list of famous names (imf2-discipline-tefl-sla).
- Constructs under-specified or not operationalisable.
- No falsifiability: no evidence that would count against the account.
- Selective literature favouring the proposal.
- Terminological proliferation (new labels for known ideas).
- Rival accounts collapsed or caricatured.
- Unstated assumption doing the main work.
- No route from theory to classroom or research design.

### 2.8 Book or edited volume
+ Breadth suited to the stated audience; coherent organisation.
+ Pedagogical apparatus: tasks, glossaries, summaries, further reading.
+ Authoritative and varied contributors; consistent chapter structure (edited volumes).
+ Accessibility for students alongside depth for researchers.
+ Recent research integrated; international scope.
+ Clear editorial introduction linking chapters.
+ Practical value for teachers or researchers.
- Uneven chapters in depth, length or quality.
- Mixed audience: too advanced for beginners, too basic for experts.
- Thin coverage of key topics (for example coding qualitative data, advanced statistics).
- Little new material relative to earlier editions or existing textbooks.
- Missing glossary, index or author information.
- One paradigm dominates despite claims of diversity.
- Geographically narrow focus presented as general.
- Recent research missing.
- Chapters that do not speak to each other.

## 3. By aspect

Three to five prompts each. Use the aspects your genre weighs most (pipeline_B 6.3: empirical weighs design, sampling, instruments, analysis, claims; methodological weighs purpose, framework, coverage, practice and accuracy; position weighs argument, claims, coverage and stake).

**Rationale and gap**
- Is the gap real and specific, or only "little research has examined X"?
- Is the gap supported with evidence (syntheses, data), not only asserted?
- Does the literature review build towards the RQs or only list studies?
- Is the context (country, level, language) justified as worth studying?

**Theory or framework**
- Is the framework named, defined and actually used in analysis and discussion?
- Why this framework rather than its main rival, and is the reason stated?
- Does the framework fit the object it is applied to (a testing framework applied to research instruments)?
- Are key constructs defined both theoretically and operationally?

**Research questions**
- Are the RQs answerable with this design and these data?
- Are hypotheses directional and justified by the literature?
- Does each RQ get an answer in Results, in order?
- Are there too many RQs for the data (proliferation dilutes interpretation)?

**Design**
- Does the design match the question type (causal, relational, descriptive, exploratory)?
- Is there a comparison group; random assignment or intact classes?
- Are threats to internal validity (history, maturation, testing, attrition, selection) addressed?
- Is the treatment long and intense enough to expect an effect?

**Sampling and participants**
- Who took part, how were they recruited, and to whom can results extend?
- Is the sample size justified (a priori power, saturation)?
- Are proficiency, L1, age, learning history and setting reported?
- For qualitative work: is the purposeful sampling logic stated?

**Instruments, validity and reliability**
- Is reliability reported for this sample, with the right index?
- Is there validity evidence beyond face or content validity?
- Does the instrument measure the construct the claims are about?
- Are there ceiling or floor effects?
- Are self-report data the only source?

**Procedure**
- Are timing, setting, instructions and prompts described?
- Are tasks and materials available (appendix, IRIS)?
- Are raters trained, and is agreement reported?
- Could another researcher repeat the study from this description?

**Analysis**
- Are assumptions checked; are tests appropriate to the data (nested, non-normal, ordinal)?
- Are effect sizes and CIs reported; is multiple testing handled?
- Is the unit of analysis right (texts nested in learners, learners in classes)?
- Qualitative: is the coding process transparent, with an example of a theme's development?
- Mixed: where does integration happen?

**Reporting**
- Are descriptive statistics complete (M, SD, n per group)?
- Do tables and text agree?
- Are non-significant and unexpected results reported?
- Are data, code or materials shared?

**Interpretation and claims**
- Do claims go beyond the data (causal from correlational, general from local)?
- Are alternative explanations considered?
- Does the claim type stay the same from Results to Discussion to Abstract to Title?
- Is practical significance discussed?

**Limitations acknowledged**
- Are limitations specific and consequential, or ritual?
- Are they in the paradigm's own terms?
- Does any earlier claim change because of a stated limitation?
- Is the most serious limitation the one acknowledged?

**Implications**
- Are pedagogical implications supported by these findings?
- Are they concrete enough to act on (what a teacher would do differently)?
- Are future-research suggestions specific (design, sample, measure)?
- Do implications fit realistic contexts (intact classes, small budgets, exam pressure)?

**Structure**
- Is there a clear roadmap; does the order build understanding?
- Is space allocated in proportion to importance?
- Are sections linked; is anything duplicated?
- Does the conclusion match the promise of the introduction?

**Style**
- Are terms defined at first use for the intended reader?
- Is the density right for the audience?
- Do figures and tables carry the argument or decorate it?
- (Style criticism is rare in real reviews, about 8% of criticisms; keep it short.)

**References**
- Are classic and current sources both present?
- Is the literature international, not only local or only Anglophone?
- Is any statistic or rule of thumb unsourced?
- Is self-citation proportionate?

**Ethics**
- Consent, anonymity and data protection addressed?
- Withholding a beneficial treatment from a control group discussed?
- Teacher-researcher power over student participants addressed?
- Ethics approval reported?

**Open science and transparency**
- Preregistration, or a clear line between confirmatory and exploratory analyses?
- Data, materials, code shared or shareable?
- Reporting guidelines followed (APA JARS, PRISMA, SMART for syntheses)?
- Is reproducibility claimed but not demonstrated?

## 4. Field-specific Applied Linguistics angles

Prompts that mark the reviewer as someone inside the field. Each: what to check, and a frame.

| Angle | What to check | Frame |
|---|---|---|
| L2 proficiency reporting | Is proficiency measured with a named instrument (standardised test, placement test, C-test) or only labelled ("intermediate")? Are levels comparable across groups? | "Proficiency is described only as 'intermediate' (p. n); a standard measure would let readers judge whether the effect depends on level." |
| Intact classes and quasi-experiments | Teacher, timetable and class culture confounded with condition; clustering ignored; baseline differences | "Because each condition was a different intact class, teacher effects cannot be separated from the treatment; a multilevel analysis or more classes per condition would help." |
| Ecological validity of lab tasks | Lab or online tasks vs classroom conditions; time on task; stakes; generalisation to real use | "The treatment ran in a single 30-minute lab session (illustrative); how far the gains transfer to classroom conditions remains open." |
| Rater reliability and task variability | Inter-rater agreement reported, but tasks, prompts or occasions also vary; a reliability figure for raters does not license task generalisation | "Rater agreement is high (p. n), but with one task per learner the scores cannot be generalised across tasks." |
| Learner populations and WEIRD samples | University students only; one L1; one country; adult learners as default; young learners or heritage speakers absent | "All participants were Persian-speaking university students (illustrative), so the claim about 'L2 learners' (p. n) should be scoped to that population." |
| Pedagogical transfer | Do implications follow from these findings, in these settings? Are they actionable for teachers? | "The recommendation for teachers (p. n) follows from a lab finding; one classroom example would show how it survives real constraints." |
| Construct definitions of L2 skills | What do "speaking proficiency", "fluency", "complexity", "vocabulary knowledge" mean here? Receptive vs productive, explicit vs implicit, task difficulty vs linguistic complexity (Bulte, Housen and Pallotti) | "Complexity is measured as mean length of T-unit only (illustrative), which captures one dimension of a multidimensional construct." |
| Uptake vs acquisition | Immediate acceptance of feedback or correction during a task reported as learning; no delayed, unassisted task (imf2-discipline-tefl-sla) | "Accepting 80% of the suggestions shows immediate uptake (illustrative); a delayed task without feedback would be needed to show retention." |
| Nested data | Several texts or items per learner, learners in classes, analysed as independent | "Each learner contributed several essays, but the analysis treats essays as independent, which inflates the effective sample." |
| CALL and technology currency | Tool versions, platforms and AI systems date fast; claims about "new" tools; access and equity | "The study uses a 2019 version of the platform (illustrative); since generative AI tools now perform the same task, the findings speak mainly to that earlier generation." |
| Feedback and instruction conditions | Type, timing and amount of feedback; who gave it; whether the comparison condition received equal exposure | "The experimental group received both feedback and extra practice time, so the two are confounded." |
| Assessment and washback | Exam-driven settings; score use; whether a test validated for one purpose is used for another | "The placement test is used as an outcome measure (p. n), a use for which the paper gives no validity evidence." |
| Multilingual perspectives | Monolingual native-speaker norm as the yardstick; translanguaging; L1 use in class treated only as interference | "The native-speaker benchmark (p. n) sits uneasily with the multilingual learners studied; an alternative criterion would be X." |
| Teacher-researcher role | The researcher teaches the participants; grading power; expectancy effects | "The author taught both groups (p. n), which raises the possibility of expectancy effects that a second teacher or blind rating would reduce." |
| Statistical conventions in L2 research | Power, effect-size benchmarks for L2 (field-specific rather than Cohen's general ones), CIs, assumption checks | "Effect sizes are interpreted with Cohen's general benchmarks; L2-specific benchmarks (Plonsky and Oswald 2014) would give a different reading." (Verify the citation before use.) |

Items marked "illustrative" contain example numbers or settings; replace them with the paper's own facts.

## 5. Evidence from the review literature: calibrating tone and proportion

These findings set the default shape and tone of a CC. Evidence status is given as research_R3 and research_R4 record it.

| Finding | Source | Status | Consequence for the CC |
|---|---|---|---|
| About 76% of criticisms in academic book reviews are mitigated; hedges are about 40% of mitigation devices, praise-criticism pairs about 23%; other devices include personal responsibility ("in my view"), other attribution, indirectness | Zou and Hyland 2020 (mitigation of criticism in book reviews) | research_R3 read this at abstract or excerpt level and did not re-verify title and venue; research_R4 lists the accepted manuscript as read in full. Verify before citing in anything published. | Mitigate most criticisms, mostly with one well-placed hedge or a praise-criticism pair. Do not mitigate every sentence. |
| About 80% of criticism targets content, about 8% style, about 4% presentation | Zou and Hyland 2020 | Same as above (numbers from abstract or excerpt in R3) | Lead Weaknesses with substance (argument, framework, design, coverage); style one sentence at most. |
| Criticism is more frequent in social sciences than in physical sciences; reviewers manage it with hedges, conditionals, questions and positive framing | Zou and Hyland 2020, summarising Hyland 2004, 2006 | Read in R4 | A CC in AL is expected to criticise; calm, specific criticism is the norm, not rudeness. |
| Book reviews move through: introduce, outline contents, evaluate parts, close with a recommendation that often concedes shortcomings; about 66% of RELC reviews are chapter-led | Motta-Roth 1998; Siripukdi 2026 | Abstract or excerpt level; Motta-Roth known through secondary descriptions | Follow the paper's order in the Summary; end with a recommendation that names readers and concedes limits. |
| Peer review reports: summarise, outline, criticise, recommend; most comments target methods reporting (990 comments across 23 moves in one 2022 study) | Fortanet 2008; 2022 genre study; Pearson and Mirhosseini 2026 | Abstract or excerpt level; citation details to verify | Methods reporting (reliability, effect sizes, procedures) is a safe, high-value target in empirical papers. |
| Sampling is the limitation authors most often name (43.7%); many qualitative authors describe limitations in positivist terms | MLJ 2026 study of limitations in qualitative language education research | Passage read | Expect sampling to be discussed; credit paradigm-appropriate limitations; criticise positivist limitation talk in qualitative work. |
| Median statistical power in L2 interaction research about .56 against the .80 convention | Plonsky 2013, 2015 | Passage read | Power and sample size are fair, field-standard targets in quantitative critique. |
| Few primary studies report confidence intervals (0 to 5%) | Larson-Hall and Plonsky 2015 | Passage read | Missing CIs and effect sizes are legitimate, consequential weaknesses. |
| Few high-integration mixed methods studies in AL | Hashemi and Babaii 2013 | Passage read | Integration is the first thing to check in mixed methods papers. |
| Experts hedge more than students; L2 students boost more and use more attitude markers | Qiu et al. 2024 (2.64M words), via imf2-academic-2 | Reported in the IMF bundle, not re-verified | Fewer boosters ("outstanding", "clearly"); one calibrated hedge per interpretive claim. |
| "Would have been" counterfactuals and critical questions are the main polite, unhedged criticism devices in L2 reviews | research_R3 Part 1, from attested review passages | Attested sentences | Pair each weakness with a "could have" or "would have benefited from" alternative; convert questions into claims. |
| In the three course model CCs: only about one in four strengths concerned substance; eight weakness claims were answered by the papers; zero contrast and zero consequence connectors | pipeline_A, pipeline_B measurements | Own measurement on the course materials | Push at least one strength to substance; verify every absence; build Weaknesses on concession and consequence. |
| Fully AI-written reviews carry more claims (about 32 vs 23) and converge on the same points; human reviewers raise more unique points | Sem-Detect (Duarte et al. 2026) | Passages read [A] | Fewer, text-specific points. |

**Tone calibration rules derived from the above**
1. Strengths before weaknesses, and weaknesses longer than strengths (about 35% vs 20 to 25% of the CC).
2. Content first: substance, then structure, then style.
3. Mitigate with one device per criticism: a single hedge tied to a reason, or a concession that credits what the authors did ("Although the authors note X (p. n), ..."). Never stack devices.
4. One or two major weaknesses developed fully, the rest short. Each with an alternative.
5. Close with a recommendation that names who should read the paper and for what, conceding the main limit.
6. Specific praise in plain words. Praise is information about what works, not cushioning.
