A Critical Commentary on "R as a Lingua Franca: Advantages of Using R for Quantitative Research in Applied Linguistics"

Authors: Atsushi Mizumoto and Luke Plonsky (2016)

Bibliographic information: Mizumoto, A., & Plonsky, L. (2016). R as a lingua franca: Advantages of using R for quantitative research in applied linguistics. Applied Linguistics, 37(2), 284-291. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amv025

## Summary

More than two decades ago, applied linguists were told that statistics was a foreign language to them. Survey evidence suggests that their statistical literacy has changed little in twenty years, even as the field has begun to adopt effect sizes, confidence intervals, bootstrapping and mixed-effects models. Against this background, this forum article argues that R, a free and open-source environment for statistical computing, would serve the field well as a shared language for quantitative analysis. The authors are careful to frame their aim modestly: they do not intend to "sell" the software, and they will count the piece a success if readers make more informed choices about statistical tools.

They begin by noting the gap between R's low uptake in applied linguistics, where a recent survey found that only 15% of researchers used it, fewer than the 17% who calculated by hand, and its rapid growth in other disciplines. They then set out four advantages. Reproducibility comes first and receives the most attention: because R is driven by code, sharing scripts alongside data allows others to rerun an analysis exactly, which the authors link to the field's growing interest in replication, meta-analysis and open repositories of instruments. Second, thousands of packages written by statisticians and computer scientists make almost any analysis possible, and the authors list recent studies in and around the field that used R for bootstrapping, multilevel modelling, Bayesian analysis and quantile regression. Third, R produces richer graphics, which a figure illustrates by plotting the same data four times, from a bar chart of means to a box plot that also shows every data point. Finally, because R is a programming language, it supports online testing platforms and corpus tools such as readability and lexical diversity indices.

Their final section turns to the main drawback the authors acknowledge, the command-line interface, and introduces a web application built by the first author to ease the transition. The application accepts pasted spreadsheet data, reports effect sizes and confidence intervals, offers language testing analyses such as item analysis and generalizability theory, and makes its code available. The authors close by conceding that R's learning curve is steep but worth climbing, and they compare learning R to learning a new language.

## Strengths

Judged against its own aim of helping readers make informed decisions, this forum article largely succeeds, chiefly because it ties a software choice to a methodological argument rather than to convenience. Its case for reproducibility is the strongest part of the piece: by connecting scripted analysis to replication, meta-analysis and open data, the authors show why the choice of tool matters for the credibility of the field and not only for the individual analyst. It is also at its most persuasive where it shows rather than tells. Figure 1 plots identical data four ways, and the step from a bar chart of means to a box plot with individual scores lets readers see how much conventional graphs conceal. A further merit is practical. The web application lowers the cost of a first attempt, and the decision to report effect sizes and confidence intervals by default nudges users towards the reporting practices the field has been asked to adopt. Finally, the piece is short, clearly organised and easy to read, which suits a forum audience of researchers who may never have opened R. Taken together, these features make the article a useful starting point for applied linguists weighing a change of software.

## Weaknesses

Despite these merits, the article has three limitations, concerning the balance of its argument, the evidence behind its comparisons and its treatment of the barriers to adoption. The most serious is balance. The authors state that they do not intend to "sell" the software and that they recognise its weaknesses (p. 285). Apart from brief mentions of the command-line interface, the steep learning curve and the web application's own limits, however, the piece presents only advantages. Their own transition "So far we have highlighted only strengths" (p. 288) concedes as much, and the conclusion calls R "the preferred package of researchers in the 21st century" (p. 290). As a result, it reads more as advocacy than as the balanced guide its framing promises. The authors also say openly that the first author built the recommended application, yet they never weigh this as an interest bearing on the recommendation. Acknowledging that stake, and naming situations in which other tools are adequate, would have brought the piece closer to its own goal of informed choice.

Second, some comparisons rest on thin or uneven evidence. The claim that R "became the most widely used analytics software" in scholarly articles (p. 285) rests on a blog post rather than a peer-reviewed study. Figure 1, while effective, does not compare like with like: its panels differ in plot type as well as in software, so it demonstrates the value of richer plots more clearly than the superiority of one program. A matched comparison, or a sentence acknowledging that individual-point plots can also be drawn in other packages, would have closed this gap.

Third, the article reports that uptake in the field is very low (p. 285) but does not explore why. Beyond the interface and the learning curve, likely obstacles such as limited training in graduate programmes and institutional reliance on licensed software go undiscussed, although they are precisely what hesitant readers would need to weigh.

These limitations do not undermine the article's central point about reproducibility; rather, they suggest that it is best read as an invitation to try R, ideally alongside a more even-handed comparison of the tools available to applied linguists.
