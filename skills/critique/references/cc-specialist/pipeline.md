# CC Pipeline

### From Original Paper to Professional Critical Commentary: a step-by-step manual for Applied Linguistics

---

## How to read this manual

A Critical Commentary (CC) is a short evaluation of one paper, usually two pages long. It has a title block and three sections: a **Summary**, the **Strengths** and the **Weaknesses**. This manual shows you how an experienced reviewer gets from the original paper (A) to a finished commentary (B), and it breaks that journey into fourteen small steps you can follow on your first attempt.

The manual was built by working backwards from three paper and commentary pairs: what did the reviewer have to notice, decide and write at each moment? Those pairs are called **Paper A** (a methodology article on experimental design in L2 research), **Paper B** (an article on the validity of quantitative measures) and **Paper C** (a short forum article promoting a statistics package). Their commentaries showed the moves of the genre clearly, and their slips showed where novices go wrong. Both are used here.

Start with the pipeline on the next page. Then work through the steps. The last pages give you a two-page CC Template you can keep beside you while you write.

## Part 1. The pipeline at a glance

```
            PAPER (A)
               |
   +-----------v-----------+
   | PHASE 1  EXTRACT      |   What is this paper, really?
   |  1 Name the genre     |
   |  2 Build title block  |
   |  3 Skim the skeleton  |
   |  4 Hunt the purpose   |
   |  5 Annotate and log   |   -> Skeleton Card
   +-----------v-----------+
   | PHASE 2  EVALUATE     |   How good is it, and how do I know?
   |  6 Map and budget     |
   |  7 Interrogate        |
   |  8 Verify and filter  |  <- go back to the paper!
   |  9 Rank the ledger    |   -> Judgment Ledger
   +-----------v-----------+
   | PHASE 3  COMPOSE      |   Say it in the genre's moves
   | 10 Write the Summary  |
   | 11 Write Strengths    |
   | 12 Write Weaknesses   |   -> Draft CC
   +-----------v-----------+
   | PHASE 4  REFINE       |   Fair, precise, natural?
   | 13 Balance and verify |  <- go back to the paper!
   | 14 Polish the voice   |   -> Final CC
   +-----------v-----------+
               |
       CRITICAL COMMENTARY (B)
```

| Phase | Steps | What you walk away with | Novice time | Practised time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Extract | 1 to 5 | Genre label, title block, purpose line, coded margins (the **Skeleton Card**) | 90 min | 45 min |
| 2. Evaluate | 6 to 9 | Move map with word budget, ranked list of judgments with evidence (the **Judgment Ledger**) | 70 min | 35 min |
| 3. Compose | 10 to 12 | Full draft: Summary, Strengths, Weaknesses | 100 min | 60 min |
| 4. Refine | 13 to 14 | Balanced, verified, polished CC | 40 min | 30 min |

**The one number to remember.** About half your time (roughly 50%) goes into reading and judging before you write a single sentence of the CC. Novices do the reverse, and most of their problems come from that.

**The shape of a finished CC (about 900 to 1,000 words):** Summary about 40% (350 to 450 words, 4 to 6 moves), Strengths about 20 to 25% (180 to 230 words, 3 to 4 merits), Weaknesses about 35% (300 to 400 words, 3 to 5 flaws).

Weaknesses should be **longer** than strengths, because each flaw needs evidence and a better alternative. When the summary swells past half the page, the critique gets squeezed, and the CC turns into a report.

### The whole pipeline on one page

<!-- FIG f6 -->

- **1. Before reading:** name the genre and its job; set the yardstick (evidence, guidance, argument or synthesis); write the title block now.
- **2. While reading:** hunt the purpose ("we argue", "the goal of"); underline promises and scope notes; margin codes S + - 0 ? with page numbers.
- **3. Judging:** budget words by importance; ask the question bank for this genre; see key features from both sides.
- **4. Fairness filter:** really absent (search plus two synonyms)? promised or excluded on purpose? does the genre need it? would readers miss it? is it about this paper, in this field?
- **5. Summary (about 40%):** opener, purpose and genre, roadmap, walk-through in the paper's order, wrapper; no praise.
- **6. Strengths (about 22%):** overall judgment tied to purpose; FAE (Feature, Anchor, Effect); substance, illustration, presentation; closer.
- **7. Weaknesses (about 35%):** concessive pivot and preview; CECA (Claim, Evidence, Consequence, Alternative); substance first, style last; fair verdict.
- **8. Voice:** present tense and varied reporting verbs; two contrast and one consequence connector; one hedge per judgment; no "!" and no "to me".
- **Golden rule:** before you write "the authors ignored X", search the paper for X.

## Part 2. The fourteen steps

Each step gives you: **Do** (the action), **Look for** (where the expert's eyes go first), **You get** (the output), **Watch out** (the classic novice slip) and **Inside the reviewer's head** (how a professional thinks at that moment).

### PHASE 1. EXTRACT: build the paper's skeleton

#### Step 1. Name the genre and set the yardstick (10 min)

**Do.** Before reading closely, decide what kind of paper this is. Write one line: "This is a [genre] whose job is to [function]." Then list three to five things this genre must deliver.

**Look for.** The label on the first page (Research Article, Chapter, Review, Forum, Commentary), the length, whether there is a Method section, the range of reference years.

| Genre | Its job | Judge it mainly on |
|---|---|---|
| Empirical study | Produce evidence | Design, sample, instruments, analysis, claims that fit the data |
| Methodological review | Give guidance | Accuracy of the advice, coverage within its stated scope, usable examples |
| Position or forum paper | Persuade | Strength and balance of the argument, evidence for claims, disclosure |
| Meta-analysis or review | Synthesise | Search transparency, inclusion criteria, coding, effect sizes |
| Theoretical article | Build or refine theory | Clear constructs, coherence, engagement with rival theories |

**You get.** A genre line and an expectations list. Example for Paper C: "A short forum piece whose job is to persuade the field to adopt a tool. It should state the problem, give reasons with evidence, admit limitations, show the tool in use, and disclose any interest."

**Watch out.** Calling every paper "a research study". Two of the three model commentaries called non-empirical papers "this research paper". Judging a forum piece by the standards of an experiment produces a list of category errors.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "My judgment is only fair if my yardstick fits. A 3,000-word forum piece cannot cover everything; it must argue well. A methods chapter has no participants; it needs accurate, usable guidance. I fix the yardstick now so I am not tempted to move it later."

#### Step 2. Build the title block (5 min)

**Do.** Write it now, while you are fresh: "A Critical Commentary on "Exact Title"", the authors, and a full APA reference.

**Look for.** The first-page header and footer: year, volume, issue (including supplement numbers), page range, DOI.

**You get.** A finished title block.

**Watch out.** Missing year or page range, two citation styles mixed together, an ISSN instead of a DOI.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "A sloppy reference tells the reader I may be sloppy with the paper as well. Two careful minutes here buy credibility for the whole page."

#### Step 3. Skim the skeleton (15 min)

**Do.** Read only the abstract, the headings, every figure and table caption, the first and last paragraph of the introduction, the conclusion, notes and appendices. Label each abstract sentence with its move: Background, Gap, Purpose, Method, Findings, Implications. Sketch the paper as a numbered outline.

**Look for.** Roadmap sentences ("This paper begins with...", "The paper concludes with..."), a keywords line, notes about the paper's origin, appendices and checklists, and anything that is **absent** (no keywords, no limitations section, no worked example).

**You get.** An architecture sketch and an abstract move map.

**Watch out.** Reading line by line from page one, and skipping notes and appendices. Paper B ended with a practical checklist of mistakes and remedies, arguably its most useful output, and its commentary never mentioned it. A note in the same paper explained that it merged three conference talks, which accounted for its uneven density.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "I read the skeleton before the flesh. Once I know where the paper is going, every paragraph has a place to land. I also look for the empty rooms: a missing limitations section is information too."

#### Step 4. Hunt the purpose, the claims and the promises (15 min)

**Do.** Find the sentences where the authors say what they are doing and why. Write the thesis in one line of your own words. List the three to five claims the paper defends. Underline every **promise** the authors make, because promises become evaluation criteria.

**Look for.** *The purpose of, the goal of the current paper is, this article examines, we argue, we suggest, we propose, it is hoped that, our goal is achieved if, it is not our intention to.* Purpose statements cluster in three places: the abstract, the last paragraph of the introduction, and the first paragraph of the conclusion.

| Paper | What the authors wrote | Why the reviewer marked it |
|---|---|---|
| A | "It is hoped that this discussion will bring design issues to the forefront in experimental L2 research." | The thesis: everything else serves this aim. |
| B | "the goal of the current paper is to discuss these concerns so that researchers can have a better understanding of best practices in quantitative reasoning" | Defines the audience (researchers) and the yardstick (better practice). |
| C | "our goal is achieved if, after reading this article, readers make more informed decisions" | A success test written by the authors themselves. |
| C | "it is not our intention to 'sell' this particular software" | A promise the rest of the paper will keep or break. |

**You get.** A one-line thesis, a claim list, a list of promises.

**Watch out.** Taking the first sentence of the introduction as the thesis (it is usually background), and copying the purpose sentence instead of paraphrasing it.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "The thesis is my contract with the paper. Every strength I praise and every weakness I name must connect back to it. When the authors tell me how to judge them, I use their yardstick before I use mine."

#### Step 5. Annotate and keep an evidence log (40 to 60 min)

**Do.** Read the whole paper closely with a small set of margin codes. Every time you write a plus or a minus, copy the short phrase and its page number into an evidence log.

| Code | Meaning | Feeds |
|---|---|---|
| **S** | Summary-worthy: a main move, definition, list or claim | Summary |
| **+** | Possible strength: clear example, useful figure, worked number, practical tool, honest concession | Strengths |
| **-** | Possible weakness: gap, overclaim, imbalance, unclear part, missing link | Weaknesses |
| **0** | Something you expected but cannot find (provisional absence) | Step 8 |
| **?** | Check later: is this answered somewhere else? | Step 8 |

**Look for.** Slow down at definitions, numbered lists, worked examples, figures, concessions ("however", "we recognize", "we concede"), scope statements ("for the purpose of this paper", "beyond the scope", "without being comprehensive", "subsumed under"), boosters ("superior", "the preferred package"), and the authors' own stake in what they recommend.

**You get.** An annotated paper and an evidence log of 10 to 20 entries. Copy the essentials onto a one-page **Skeleton Card**:

```
SKELETON CARD
Genre: ____________________      Intended reader: ____________________
Purpose (one line, own words): _______________________________________
Main claims: 1. ______  2. ______  3. ______
Structure (% of space): Intro __% | Part 1 __% | Part 2 __% | Conclusion __%
Choices the authors made (framework, examples, figures) + an alternative: ____
Declared omissions: ________________   Possible gaps (to verify): ________
Authors' conclusion or recommendation: _______________________________
```

**Watch out.** Highlighting whole paragraphs (nothing has been decided), writing minuses without page numbers (they cannot be checked later), and forgetting the question mark, which is what protects you from unfair criticism.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "My margin is a courtroom file. Anything I later praise or blame needs an exhibit with a page number. The question mark is my conscience: before I write 'the authors ignored X', I check whether they dealt with X somewhere I skimmed."

### PHASE 2. EVALUATE: turn observations into defensible judgments

#### Step 6. Map the moves and budget the words (15 min)

**Do.** Turn your outline into a table: each part of the paper, the move it makes, a one-line gist in your own words, and how many summary words it deserves. Budget by **importance**, not by page count or by how easy the part is to summarise.

| Part of Paper B | Move | Gist (own words) | Words |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Establish problem | L2 claims rest on scores whose meaning is rarely justified | 40 |
| Aim and roadmap | State purpose | Discuss these concerns through a framework, a worked example and advice | 30 |
| History of validation | Background | From separate types of validity, to a unified view, to an argument-based chain | 70 |
| Framework applied to one study | Worked illustration (the centre) | Six inferences walked through on one oral task | 110 |
| Generalization in performance tests | Deep dive | Rater agreement is not enough; tasks vary too | 80 |
| Common mistakes | Recommendations | Ten mistakes in four groups, each with a remedy | 60 |
| Appendix | Practical output | A checklist for planning and reviewing studies | 20 |

**You get.** A move map with word budgets that add up to about 40% of your target length.

**Watch out.** Giving each list item a full sentence. The commentary on Paper B spent almost half its summary on the closing list of mistakes (the easiest part) and a single sentence on the inference chain at the heart of the paper.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "Before I write, I decide what the paper is mostly about. My summary should let the reader feel the paper's centre of gravity. If I put most words on the easiest section, I have mapped my comfort, not the paper."

#### Step 7. Interrogate the paper (20 min)

**Do.** Pick the four to six criteria that matter most for this genre (Step 1), then ask the matching questions from the bank below. Write each answer as a one-line judgment with a page number, marked plus or minus. Aim for at least six of each; you will cut later. Look at key features from **both sides**: one figure can be a strength and a weakness at once.

| Area | Questions the expert asks |
|---|---|
| Purpose and theory | Is the purpose explicit, and does the paper deliver it? Why this framework rather than its main rival, and is the reason stated? |
| Design, sample, instruments | Does the design match the question? Who took part and to whom can results extend? Are reliability and validity evidenced for this sample? |
| Analysis and claims | Are assumptions checked and effect sizes reported? Do the claims outrun the evidence (causal words from a correlational design, a rule from one example)? |
| Coverage and balance | What does the title promise that the body does not deliver? Do the authors have a stake? Are rival options compared like for like and limitations admitted? |
| Structure, style, sources | Are abstract moves, keywords and a limitations section present? Is space allocated by importance? Are classic and current sources both present, and every rule of thumb sourced? |
| Practice | Could a teacher or researcher act on this tomorrow? Is the advice itself correct? |

**You get.** A raw list of judgments, each with a location.

**Watch out.** Questioning only style and presentation. In the model commentaries, most strengths were about presentation; only about one in four concerned substance.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "A judgment without a page number is an opinion. A judgment with a page number is a finding. In a methods paper I also check whether the advice itself is right. Novices accept expert advice; professionals test it, politely."

#### Step 8. Verify every absence and run the fairness filter (20 min)

**Do.** This is the most valuable step in the pipeline. For every weakness that says "the authors do not...", search the paper for the item **and two synonyms**. Then run each surviving weakness through five tests. If it fails, delete it, soften it, or move it to the end.

1. **Is it really absent?** If it is present, drop the point or change it to "treated only briefly (p. x)".
2. **Did the paper promise it, or exclude it on purpose?** If the authors set it aside ("for the purpose of this paper", "beyond the scope"), you may question the choice of scope, but you must acknowledge it.
3. **Does the genre require it?** A forum piece owes you an argument, not a literature review.
4. **Would the intended readers miss it?**
5. **Is it about this paper, in this field?** "Experiments are hard to run in hospitals" is about experiments in general and about another field. It is not a weakness of an L2 methods paper.

**You get.** A verified, fair list of weaknesses.

**Watch out.** In the three model commentaries, **eight** weakness claims were contradicted or partly answered by the papers themselves. One commentary said a design type was left out when the paper presented it under another name; another asked "On what basis was it selected?" when the paper stated the basis in its introduction.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "My credibility is gone the moment an author can reply, 'We addressed that on page 10.' One unfair point makes the reader doubt all my fair ones. I would rather make four bulletproof criticisms than seven leaky ones."

#### Step 9. Rank the ledger and pair every flaw with a remedy (15 min)

**Do.** Keep three to four strengths and three to five weaknesses. Rank them by consequence: **substance** (argument, framework, accuracy, coverage) before **structure** (organisation, proportion, genre conventions) before **style** (wording, readability). For every weakness, write the alternative now.

```
JUDGMENT LEDGER
#  | + / - | Judgment (one line) | Evidence (page or quote) | Why it matters | Better alternative ("could have...")
1  |   +   |                     |                          |                |  n/a
2  |   -   |                     |                          |                |
```

**You get.** The Judgment Ledger, which is your CC in note form.

**Watch out.** Ranking by order of discovery. One model commentary opened its weaknesses with missing abstract moves and keywords and reached the author's undeclared bias only fourth, which made a strong critique look trivial.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "If I cannot fill the 'alternative' cell, the weakness is not ready. Criticism without a better option is only complaint."

### PHASE 3. COMPOSE: write in the genre's moves

#### Step 10. Write the Summary (40 min)

**Do.** Write two or three paragraphs that walk your move map in the paper's own order. Use this sequence of moves:

1. **Opener**: one or two sentences of context (why the topic matters), paraphrased from the paper's own framing.
2. **Purpose and genre**: "In this [methodological review / forum article], the authors argue that..."
3. **Roadmap** (optional): "To this end, they first..., then..., and finally..."
4. **Walk-through**: one or two sentences per major part, the centre of the paper getting the most.
5. **Wrapper**: how the paper closes: its conclusion, recommendation or practical output.

**Compression rules.**
- Aim sentences become reporting-verb paraphrases ("The authors open by defining...").
- Numbered lists become **category + count + two examples**.
- Worked examples shrink to one clause.
- Background shrinks hardest: one or two sentences.
- Keep technical terms (*internal validity, effect size*); change everything else into your own words.
- Use the present tense for what the paper says ("the authors argue") and keep it consistent.
- **No evaluation.** Words like *insightful, beautifully, fantastic* belong in the next section.

**You get.** A 350 to 450 word summary.

**Watch out.** Copying (patchwriting), evaluation leaking in, and meaning changing during compression (see Part 3, examples 3 and 4).

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "The summary is a promise of fairness. If the authors read it, they should nod and say, 'Yes, that is what we did.' Only after I have earned that nod do I have the right to judge."

#### Step 11. Write the Strengths (20 min)

**Do.** Open with one sentence giving your overall positive judgment, naming the genre correctly and tying the judgment to the paper's purpose. Then write each merit with the **FAE pattern**:

- **Feature**: what the paper does well, named precisely.
- **Anchor**: where it shows (a figure number, a section, a worked number, a short quote).
- **Effect**: what it does for the reader or the field.

Order: substance first (contribution, framework, argument), then illustration (examples, figures, worked calculations), then presentation (structure, readability), then scholarship (sources). Close with one synthesising sentence.

**You get.** 180 to 230 words, three to four anchored merits.

**Watch out.** Vague praise ("nice and neat", "really insightful", "did an outstanding job"), praise that is not true (calling a reference list "the most recent" when half of it is decades old), and missing the paper's best feature.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "Specific praise proves I read closely and makes my criticisms more believable. 'Clear and well written' fits a thousand papers. 'A worked calculation showing a 26% false-positive risk turns an abstract warning into something a reader can feel' fits only this one."

#### Step 12. Write the Weaknesses (40 min)

**Do.** Open with a concessive pivot and a preview: "Despite these merits, the paper has three limitations, concerning X, Y and Z." Then write each flaw as a four-part block, **CECA**:

- **Claim**: the limitation, stated precisely and calmly.
- **Evidence**: where it shows, acknowledging first what the authors *did* say ("Although the authors note... (p. x), they...").
- **Consequence**: why it matters for this paper's own aim ("As a result, readers...").
- **Alternative**: what would fix or reduce it ("A table crossing designs with threats would have...").

Put the most serious substance first, style last. Spend more words on the first two flaws. Close with a balanced verdict that tells readers how to use the paper.

**You get.** 300 to 400 words, three to five fair, constructive flaws.

**Watch out.** Rhetorical questions in place of claims ("Why not other studies? On what basis?"), personal reactions in place of properties of the text ("the article was confusing"), speculation ("Maybe there exist major problems with it"), and emotional punctuation ("!!").

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "A weakness is a claim about the paper that I must prove, and a gift to the authors if it comes with a remedy. I ask: if the authors read this paragraph, would they say 'fair point, we should have done that'? If they would say 'you did not read page 10', I delete it."

### PHASE 4. REFINE: make it fair, precise and natural

#### Step 13. Balance and verify (20 min)

**Do.** Count the words in each section and compare them with the shape table in Part 1. If you are over length, cut the summary, never the weaknesses. Then lay the draft beside the paper and tick every quotation, number, name and page reference. Check that every weakness concerns something your summary mentioned.

**You get.** A balanced, fact-checked draft.

**Watch out.** Factual slips under time pressure. One model commentary called a widely used, decades-old statistics package "brand new". Another misnamed a well-known threat to validity.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "Proportion is an argument. If my summary fills two thirds of the page, I am telling the reader I had little to judge. And one wrong detail costs the credibility of every correct one."

#### Step 14. Polish the voice (20 min)

**Do.** Read the CC aloud and fix anything you stumble over. Then run this checklist.

| Check | Target |
|---|---|
| Contrast and consequence connectors | At least two of *however, although, while* and one of *as a result, therefore* in the Weaknesses. The model commentaries used none at all, so their logic read like a shopping list. |
| Hedges | One per interpretive claim (*appears, largely, arguably, may*), none on verified facts |
| Stance | Little or no first person; no rhetorical questions; no exclamation marks; no clichés ("As we all know", "Last but not least") |
| Reporting verbs | Varied and accurate: *describe* for neutral content, *argue* for a position, *concede* for an admission |
| Sentences and quotations | Mostly 15 to 30 words and varied; two or three short quotations at most, each with a page number |

**You get.** The final CC. Score it with the rubric in Part 5.

> **Inside the reviewer's head.** "Tone is part of the argument. A calm sentence with a page number does more damage than three angry questions, and it is far more useful to the authors."

## Part 3. From paper to commentary: ten worked examples

Each example shows the **original** (verbatim, names replaced in brackets), what the **model CC** made of it, the **professional version** where it can be improved, and **why**.

### Summary examples

**Example 1. Compressing a statement of purpose (Paper A)**

- **Original:** "The purpose of research design is to ensure that the data collected can unambiguously address the question that is being asked."
- **Model CC:** "They argued that research designs are to ensure that the gleaned data is capable of addressing the research questions posed by the researcher."
- **Professional:** "The authors open by defining the purpose of research design: to ensure that the data collected can answer the research question without ambiguity."
- **Why:** The signal phrase "The purpose of" marks a thesis-level idea, so it enters the summary early. But the paper *defines* here; it does not *argue*, so the reporting verb should match the move. The key word *unambiguously* carries the paper's point and must survive the compression.

**Example 2. Turning a long list into one sentence (Paper A)**

- **Original:** six numbered threats to internal validity and five to external validity, each a paragraph with an L2 illustration (about 1,100 words), beginning "1. Attrition: In studies that take place over an extended period of time, those participants who drop out of the study are not necessarily a representative sample..."
- **Model CC:** "As for the internal validity threats, they mentioned; attrition (drop out), history, instability, maturation, selection, and testing effect."
- **Professional:** "They then catalogue six threats to internal validity, from attrition and history to selection and testing effects, and five to external validity, illustrating each with an L2 scenario and stressing that a threat to internal validity also threatens generalizability."
- **Why:** A numbered list is the spine of a methods paper, so its labels belong in the summary while the explanations go. The formula is **category + count + two examples + the authors' key point**. About 1,100 words become 40.

**Example 3. Copying is not summarising (Paper B)**

- **Original:** "These measured observations are used to provide evidence in support of meaningful claims about learners' L2 knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs)."
- **Model CC:** the identical sentence, word for word.
- **Professional (one sentence replacing a whole copied paragraph):** "Starting from the observation that most claims in applied linguistics rest on scores whose meaning is rarely justified, the authors argue that every research instrument, not only formal tests, needs validity evidence before its scores can support conclusions."
- **Why:** The opening of a dense paper feels hard to paraphrase, so novices copy it. The cure is to close the paper, write from your move map, and only then check. Copying more than four or five words in a row without quotation marks is patchwriting.

**Example 4. When compression flips the meaning (Paper A)**

- **Original:** "Studies that adopt a correlational design, therefore, should use appropriate language such as "more/less, tied, connected/related/associated, tend, and predict" and should avoid causal language such as "cause, increase/decrease, benefits, impacts, enhances/undermines, effect/affect, improves/boosts.""
- **Model CC:** "For instance, if the design is experimental, the researchers need to make causal claims not correlational ones."
- **Professional:** "Finally, the authors argue that the language of a report must match its design: correlational studies should describe associations (*related to, predicts*) rather than effects (*impacts, improves*), because only designs that rule out rival explanations license causal claims."
- **Why:** The warning runs one way: non-experimental designs must *not* use causal verbs. The model CC reversed it. Step 13 (verify against the paper) exists to catch exactly this.

### Strength examples

**Example 5. From "good examples" to an anchored merit (Paper A)**

- **Original:** "So, there would be a 26% chance that one of the contrasts would be a false positive. That is much larger than the conventional 5% level."
- **Model CC:** "Another good point in this article, to me, was the use of understandable examples when the authors wanted to introduce a concept or design."
- **Professional:** "A particular strength is the use of concrete L2 scenarios to make abstract threats tangible. A worked calculation, for instance, shows that six uncorrected comparisons raise the chance of a false positive to about 26%, which turns a general warning about multiple testing into a figure readers will remember."
- **Why:** "Understandable examples" is a label. The professional version names the feature, anchors it in one example, and states its effect. Once the evidence carries the judgment, "to me" is no longer needed.

**Example 6. From "insightful figures" to a reusable template (Paper B)**

- **Original:** "Figure 2 provides an illustrative example of an argument structure that could be used for [the exemplar study's] generalizability inference, hypothesizing that the two raters' judgments of the developmental scores in her oral description task are consistent."
- **Model CC:** "Last but not least, I found the figures and schematic representations of different frameworks along with tangible examples and clarifications really insightful."
- **Professional:** "The paper's most useful feature is the way it makes an abstract framework operational. Figure 2, for example, sets out a single inference (that two raters score consistently) as claim, warrant, rebuttal and backing, using real agreement figures. Readers can transfer this template directly to the instruments in their own studies."
- **Why:** Figures are a prime strength cue in methods papers because they turn theory into procedure. The professional version says what the figure *does* for a reader. "Last but not least" is a cliché, and the strongest merit should come first anyway.

**Example 7. One feature, two sides (Paper C)**

- **Original:** "The same data are used to plot four types of graphs with the amount of information increasing from (A) to (D)." and "Figure 1 shows an example of [the package]'s superiority in producing graphics even for simple purposes."
- **Model CC:** "The schematic representations showing how [the package] is superior in reporting data in comparison to other softwares were nice and neat."
- **Professional, as a strength:** "The argument is strongest where it shows rather than tells: Figure 1 plots the same data four times, from a bar chart of means to a box plot with individual data points, so that readers see how much information conventional graphs conceal."
- **Professional, as a weakness:** "At the same time, the comparison is not like for like, since the panels differ in plot type as well as software, so the figure demonstrates the value of richer plots more clearly than the superiority of one program."
- **Why:** Experts look at a key feature from both sides. "Nice and neat" praises appearance; the professional version praises function and then tests the claim the figure is meant to support.

### Weakness examples

**Example 8. Respecting a declared scope (Paper A)**

- **Original:** "for the purpose of this paper, ex post facto design concerns are subsumed under quasi-experimental issues (for more comprehensive discussions of all designs, see [classic design texts])". The paper's list of designs also includes the one-group posttest-only design, which is the one-shot design under another name.
- **Model CC:** "Firstly, speaking of different types of experimental research designs, the authors excluded some of the design types like expost facto, time series, one shot, ABAB, Solomon Four groups, and single subject designs..."
- **Professional:** "The selective treatment of designs is declared and reasonable for a chapter-length overview, but one omission weakens the paper's own logic. The authors identify testing effects as a threat, noting that a pretest may prime participants, yet the design built to detect this threat (the four-group design that crosses pretest and treatment) is not mentioned. Similarly, time-series and single-case designs, which suit the small intact classes the authors say dominate L2 research, would give readers alternatives to the quasi-experimental compromise."
- **Why:** Two of the six "excluded" designs were covered, and the paper declared its scope. The professional turns a coverage wish into an **internal-consistency** argument: the paper's own concerns call for the missing item. This is the strongest kind of critique.

**Example 9. From rhetorical questions to an argued limitation (Paper B)**

- **Original:** "This study was selected as it contains a wide range of simple and sophisticated analyses of the sort that could be considered evidence in a validity argument."
- **Model CC:** "...the authors had selected a single study as a criterion study of a sort with the selection of which I am not that much convinced. Why not other and more research studies? On what basis was it selected?"
- **Professional:** "The decision to illustrate the framework through a single study is justified, since it 'contains a wide range of simple and sophisticated analyses', but that very richness limits the demonstration. Readers see the framework confirming an unusually well-documented study, not diagnosing the thinner reporting the authors criticise elsewhere. A second, contrasting example would have shown the framework's diagnostic power."
- **Why:** The basis for the choice was in the paper. Before asking "why?", check; then criticise the **consequence** of the stated choice. Questions push the work onto the reader; claims do the work.

**Example 10. Testing a promise against the text (Paper C)**

- **Original:** "it is not our intention to 'sell' this particular software to the readers of [the journal] or to anyone, for that matter." Later: "So far we have highlighted only strengths of [the package]." And the conclusion calls it "the preferred package of researchers in the 21st century".
- **Model CC:** "...the authors argued that they were not advertising for their application but the whole text is biased toward their application. If not, why they were presenting [the package] as a cure-for-all diseases...? Has it been of less popularity? Why? Maybe there exist major problems with it."
- **Professional:** "The most serious limitation is the balance of the argument. The authors state that they do not intend to 'sell' the package and concede its steep learning curve, yet they close by calling it 'the preferred package of researchers in the 21st century'. The authors mention that the first author built the recommended web application, but they never treat this as an interest bearing on the recommendation. Acknowledging that stake, and giving a fairer account of situations in which other tools suffice, would have made the case more credible and closer to their stated aim of helping readers make 'more informed decisions'."
- **Why:** "The whole text is biased" is an accusation; the professional version is a demonstration. It places the promise beside the authors' own words, credits their concession, names the stake neutrally (the authors state it, but never weigh it), measures the paper against its own success test, and offers a remedy. Because it matters most, it comes first.

## Part 4. The professional mindset in ten principles

1. **Fit the yardstick to the genre.** Judge an experiment as evidence, a methods paper as guidance, and a forum piece as an argument.
2. **Critique the paper they wrote, not the one you wanted.** Respect declared scope. If you question the scope itself, say so openly and show why the paper's own aims need what was left out.
3. **Anchor everything.** No strength or weakness without a figure, section, number or short quote. "Nothing was said about X" is a factual claim: verify it first.
4. **Summarise before you judge, and summarise faithfully.** A distorted summary destroys the critique built on it.
5. **Proportion is part of the argument.** About 40% summary, 20 to 25% strengths, 35% weaknesses.
6. **Rank by consequence.** Substance first, structure next, style last. Missing keywords never open a critique.
7. **Pair every weakness with a way forward.** A limitation plus a remedy is scholarship; a limitation alone is a complaint.
8. **Turn wishes into internal-consistency arguments.** The strongest critique shows that the paper's own logic needs the missing piece.
9. **Credit concessions, then push.** "Although the authors acknowledge X, they..." is fairer and sharper than "they ignore X".
10. **Calm precision beats emphasis.** Measured verbs, one hedge where it is earned, no exclamation marks, no barrage of questions. Write for two readers at once: someone who has not read the paper, and the authors themselves.

## Part 5. Self-check rubric

Score each criterion out of 10 and add them up (out of 100). Below 70 means revise; 70 to 89 is strong; 90 and above is professional.

| # | Criterion | 9 to 10 looks like this |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title block | Exact title, all authors, complete APA reference with DOI |
| 2 | Summary fidelity | Purpose, parts in order and conclusion all accurate; the authors would recognise their paper |
| 3 | Summary compression and neutrality | About 40% of the CC, lists compressed, no evaluation, no copied strings |
| 4 | Strengths: anchoring | Three to four merits, each with feature, anchor and effect; at least one about substance |
| 5 | Weaknesses: fairness | Every absence verified; declared scope respected; genre-appropriate |
| 6 | Weaknesses: constructiveness and order | Ranked by consequence; each with evidence, consequence and alternative |
| 7 | Critical depth | Engages framework, claims and advice; sees key features from both sides |
| 8 | Structure and proportion | All moves present, including the closing sentences of each section |
| 9 | Stance and tone | Calibrated hedges, little or no first person, no questions or exclamations, respectful |
| 10 | Language and cohesion | Accurate, varied reporting verbs; contrast and consequence connectors; natural collocations |

Most first drafts score between 40 and 60. Steps 8, 12 and 14 alone usually lift a draft above 80.

## Part 6. The two-page CC Template

*Keep this beside you while you write. For each line: what to say, then the three most reliable fixed expressions. X, Y and Z are slots.*

### The Title Block

**What to write:** the exact title in quotation marks, the authors and year, a full APA reference.

```
A Critical Commentary on "Exact Title of the Paper"
Authors: Author A, Author B and Author C (Year)
Bibliographic information: Author, A. A., & Author, B. B. (Year). Title in sentence case.
Journal Name, Volume(Issue), pages. https://doi.org/...
```

### Section 1. Summary (about 40%, 350 to 450 words, 2 to 3 paragraphs)

| Line | What to say, in a few words | Top three fixed expressions |
|---|---|---|
| **Summary Opener** | Why the topic matters; the problem in the field (1 to 2 sentences, no praise) | 1. X has long been a central concern in Y, yet Z. 2. Research in X often depends on Y. 3. In response to growing interest in X, ... |
| **Purpose and Genre** | Genre label + what the authors set out to do | 1. In this [methodological review / forum article / empirical study], the authors set out to X. 2. The article argues that X. 3. The authors' stated aim is to X. |
| **Roadmap** (optional) | The order of the paper in one sentence | 1. To this end, they first X, then Y, and finally Z. 2. The article is organised into X main parts. 3. The discussion moves from X to Y. |
| **Walk-through** | One or two sentences per major part, in the paper's order; centre gets most words; lists as category + count + 2 examples | 1. The authors begin by X. 2. They then turn to X, distinguishing Y from Z. 3. In the next section, they illustrate X through Y. |
| **Summary Wrapper** | How the paper ends: conclusion, recommendation or practical output | 1. The authors conclude that X. 2. The article closes with practical recommendations for X. 3. In sum, the authors call for X. |

### Section 2. Strengths (about 20 to 25%, 180 to 230 words, 1 paragraph)

| Line | What to say, in a few words | Top three fixed expressions |
|---|---|---|
| **Strengths Opener** | Overall positive judgment tied to the paper's purpose | 1. Overall, the article makes a timely and valuable contribution to X. 2. Judged against its own aim of X, the article largely succeeds. 3. Chief among the article's merits is X. |
| **Merit 1** (substance) | Contribution, framework or argument + where + effect | 1. The main strength of the article lies in X. 2. The authors make a compelling case for X. 3. Particularly valuable is X, which Y. |
| **Merit 2** (illustration) | Example, figure, worked number + where + effect | 1. A further strength is the use of X to illustrate Y. 2. Figure X, for instance, shows how Y. 3. The worked example of X turns Y into Z. |
| **Merit 3 to 4** (presentation, sources) | Structure, clarity, references, practical tools | 1. The article is also clearly organised, moving from X to Y. 2. The appendix offers a practical checklist that readers can apply to X. 3. The discussion draws judiciously on both foundational and recent work. |
| **Strengths Closer** | One sentence of synthesis: who benefits | 1. Taken together, these features make the article a valuable resource for X. 2. These strengths make the article particularly useful for X. 3. In sum, the article succeeds in X. |

### Section 3. Weaknesses (about 35%, 300 to 400 words, 1 to 3 paragraphs)

| Line | What to say, in a few words | Top three fixed expressions |
|---|---|---|
| **Weaknesses Opener** | Concessive pivot + preview of the flaws | 1. Despite these merits, the article has some limitations, concerning X and Y. 2. Notwithstanding these strengths, a few shortcomings should be noted. 3. Valuable as the article is, it is less convincing on X. |
| **Flaw: Claim** | The most serious limitation, stated calmly | 1. First, the rationale for X remains largely implicit. 2. The treatment of X is rather brief. 3. A more serious concern is X. |
| **Flaw: Evidence** | Acknowledge what the authors did say, then what is missing (with page) | 1. Although the authors note that X (p. n), they do not Y. 2. While the article acknowledges X, it stops short of Y. 3. The authors concede X, yet Y. |
| **Flaw: Consequence** | Why it matters for the paper's own aim | 1. As a result, readers may find it difficult to X. 2. This leaves unclear whether X. 3. This may limit the usefulness of the article for X. |
| **Flaw: Alternative** | One concrete remedy | 1. It would have been helpful if the authors had X. 2. A brief comparison with X would have clarified Y. 3. The article would have benefited from X. |
| **Further flaws** | Shorter blocks, structure and style last | 1. A further limitation concerns X. 2. In addition, X is not always signalled. 3. Finally, on a more minor point, X. |
| **Final Verdict** | Weigh limitations against merits; how to use the paper | 1. These limitations do not undermine X; rather, they suggest that Y. 2. On balance, the article remains a useful starting point for X. 3. Read alongside X, the article offers Y. |

**Before you submit:** Could the authors recognise their paper in my Summary, and accept my Weaknesses as fair, even if they disagree?
