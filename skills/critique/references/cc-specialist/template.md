# CC template: tagged slots, fixed expressions and pro tips

The same content as the CC Expert HTML tool. Each slot lists its tag, whether it is FIXED (recurrent expression) or VARIABLE (content varies by paper), the expression options (X, Y, Z are slots) and the expert tip. Pick options that fit the paper; never paste more than one option per slot; adapt wording so no two commentaries read alike.

## Title Block (Title, authors, full reference)

### [The Title] FIXED
**What to write:** Copy the paper's title exactly, with its original capitalisation and punctuation, inside quotation marks.

**Options:**
- A Critical Commentary on "X"
- A Critical Review of "X"
- Critical Commentary: "X"
- A Critique of "X"
- Commentary on "X": Strengths and Limitations
- Reading "X" Critically: A Commentary

**How pros do it:** Professionals never shorten or paraphrase the title; a changed title signals careless reading. If your department uses a cover page (course, instructor, your name, date), keep it on a separate page above the commentary, not inside it.

**Avoid:** Dropping the subtitle after the colon | Using curly and straight quotation marks inconsistently

**Example:** A Critical Commentary on "Design Issues and Inference in Experimental L2 Research"

### [Authors] FIXED
**What to write:** List every author in the order printed on the paper, with the year. Affiliations are optional.

**Options:**
- Authors: X, Y and Z (Year)
- Author: X (Year)
- X and Y (Year)
- Authors: X (University of Y) and Z (University of W)

**How pros do it:** Use the same author order as the byline; order signals contribution.

**Avoid:** Listing only the first author with 'et al.' in the title block

**Example:** Authors: A. Author and B. Author (2015)

### [Bibliographic Information] FIXED
**What to write:** A complete APA 7 reference: authors, year, title in sentence case, journal in italics, volume(issue), page range, DOI.

**Options:**
- Bibliographic information: Surname, A. A., & Surname, B. B. (Year). Title of the article in sentence case. Journal Name, Volume(Issue), first page-last page. https://doi.org/xx.xxxx
- Reference: Surname, A. A. (Year). Title of the article. Journal Name, Volume(Issue), pages. https://doi.org/xx.xxxx
- Bibliographic information: Surname, A. A., Surname, B. B., & Surname, C. C. (Year). Title. Journal Name, Volume(Suppl. 1), pages. https://doi.org/xx.xxxx

**How pros do it:** Check the first-page header and footer for supplement numbers, page range and DOI. Cite the version of record, not the advance-access version, if both exist.

**Avoid:** Missing year or page range | An ISSN instead of a DOI | Mixing two citation styles

**Example:** Author, A., & Author, B. (2015). Title of the article. Language Learning, 65(S1), 76-96. https://doi.org/...

## Summary (about 40% · 350 to 450 words · 2 to 3 paragraphs · neutral)

### [Summary Opener] FIXED
**What to write:** One or two sentences of context: why the topic matters and what problem the paper responds to. Paraphrase the paper's own framing. No praise yet.

**Options:**
- X has long been a central concern in Y, yet Z remains poorly understood.
- Research in X often depends on Y, which makes Z a pressing issue.
- In response to growing interest in X, the authors turn their attention to Y.
- Over the past two decades, X has moved from the margins of Y to its centre.
- Few issues in X are as consequential, or as often neglected, as Y.
- Claims about X in applied linguistics rest heavily on Y.
- Although X is widely used in Y, its Z has received surprisingly little scrutiny.
- Recent calls for greater rigour in X form the backdrop to this article.
- Debates about X have intensified in recent years, particularly regarding Y.
- The quality of X research is only as strong as Y.
- X has become a recurring theme in discussions of Y.
- A persistent problem in X research is Y.

**How pros do it:** Take the problem from the paper's introduction, not from your own interests. Compress the background harder than anything else: one or two sentences.

**Avoid:** Opening with evaluation ('This insightful article...') | Copying the paper's first sentences | 'As we all know' openers

**Example:** Experimental designs are the main route to causal claims in L2 research, yet weak designs remain a frequent reason for unconvincing studies.

### [Purpose & Genre] FIXED
**What to write:** Name the genre correctly and state what the authors set out to do, in your own words.

**Options:**
- In this [methodological review / forum article / empirical study], the authors set out to X.
- The article argues that X.
- The authors' stated aim is to X.
- Against this background, the authors make the case that X.
- The article sets out to examine X, with a view to Y.
- The purpose of the article is twofold: to X and to Y.
- Drawing on X, the authors seek to Y.
- The authors address this gap by X.
- In this short position paper, the authors advocate X.
- Framed as a practical guide, the article aims to help researchers X.
- The central claim of the article is that X.
- The study investigates whether X, using Y with Z participants.

**How pros do it:** Purpose statements cluster in the abstract, the last paragraph of the introduction and the first paragraph of the conclusion. Look for: the purpose of, the goal of the current paper, we argue, we propose, it is hoped that. Note any success test the authors set themselves ('our goal is achieved if...'); you can use it later as a yardstick. Choose the reporting verb by the move: argue for a position, examine for an inquiry, describe for a neutral account.

**Avoid:** Calling a review or position paper 'this research' | Copying the purpose sentence instead of paraphrasing it

**Example:** In this methodological review, the authors set out to bring design issues to the forefront of experimental L2 research.

### [Roadmap] FIXED (optional)
**What to write:** Optional: one sentence previewing the paper's order. Useful for long papers; skip it for short forum pieces.

**Options:**
- To this end, they first X, then Y, and finally Z.
- The article is organised into X main parts.
- The discussion moves from X to Y before turning to Z.
- The argument unfolds in three stages.
- The article proceeds in X steps, each addressing Y.
- After a brief review of X, the authors turn to Y and close with Z.
- The paper begins with X, moves on to Y and concludes with Z.

**How pros do it:** Build it from the paper's own roadmap sentences ('This paper begins with...'), but in your own words.

**Avoid:** A roadmap that lists every subheading

**Example:** To this end, they move from research questions to validity threats, design types, statistical power and the language of causal claims.

### [Walk-through] VARIABLE
**What to write:** One or two sentences per major part of the paper, in the paper's own order. The paper's centre of gravity gets the most words.

**Options:**
- The authors begin by X.
- They then turn to X, distinguishing Y from Z.
- In the next section, they illustrate X through Y.
- Building on this, the authors X.
- The central part of the article is devoted to X.
- The authors go on to X.
- Turning to X, they argue that Y.
- A further section examines X.
- They catalogue X types of Y, from A to B, and stress that Z.
- To illustrate X, the authors draw on Y.
- This leads them to X.
- The authors also consider X.
- Particular attention is given to X.
- The discussion then shifts to X.
- Having established X, they move on to Y.
- In a worked example, the authors show how X.
- They identify X common problems in Y, grouped under Z headings.

**How pros do it:** Budget by importance, not by ease: the easy closing list must not eat the budget. Lists become category + count + two examples ('six threats, from attrition to testing effects'). Keep technical terms; change everything else into your own words. Use the present tense for what the paper says, consistently. Vary reporting verbs: describe, outline, distinguish, catalogue, illustrate, argue, concede, stress.

**Questions to ask:** What is the paper mostly about? Does my word budget show that? | Would the authors nod at every sentence?

**Avoid:** Patchwriting: copying sentences and swapping one word | Evaluation leaking in ('beautifully', 'fantastic') | Meaning flipping during compression; check every compressed claim against the paper | Leaving in-text citations from the paper in your summary

**Example:** They then catalogue six threats to internal validity, from attrition and history to selection and testing effects, illustrating each with an L2 scenario and stressing that a threat to internal validity also threatens generalizability.

### [Summary Wrapper] FIXED
**What to write:** How the paper ends: its conclusion, recommendation or practical output (checklist, tool, table).

**Options:**
- The authors conclude that X.
- The article closes with practical recommendations for X.
- In sum, the authors call for X.
- The article ends by urging researchers to X.
- Finally, the authors offer a checklist of X for Y.
- They close by reflecting on the implications of X for Y.
- The authors end with a call for X.
- Their concluding section summarises X and points to Y.
- In closing, the authors argue that X.
- The article concludes with a reminder that X.

**How pros do it:** Readers want to know what the paper leaves them with. Practical outputs (checklists, appendices, verb lists) are often the most useful part and are often forgotten. End where the authors end.

**Avoid:** Skipping the conclusion entirely | Adding your own verdict here

**Example:** They conclude that time spent on planning is well spent, a point captured in the carpenter's maxim 'Measure twice, cut once.'

## Strengths (about 20 to 25% · 180 to 230 words · 3 to 4 merits)

### [Strengths Opener] FIXED
**What to write:** One sentence of overall positive judgment that names the genre correctly and ties the judgment to the paper's purpose.

**Options:**
- Overall, the article makes a timely and valuable contribution to X.
- The article's strengths are considerable, and mostly substantive.
- Chief among the article's merits is X.
- The article succeeds on several fronts.
- This is a clear, well-argued and practically useful article.
- The article makes a persuasive case for X, and does so economically.
- Much in this article deserves praise.
- The article's most obvious strength is X.
- As a [methodological review / forum piece], the article does much of what it sets out to do.
- The article offers a great deal to researchers working on X.
- The article stands out for X.
- There is much to commend in this article.
- The article makes a welcome contribution to an under-researched area.
- Judged against its stated aim of X, the article largely succeeds.

**How pros do it:** Judge the paper against its own aim and its genre: an experiment as evidence, a methods paper as guidance, a forum piece as argument. Keep boosters modest: 'timely', 'valuable', 'clear' rather than 'outstanding', 'amazing'.

**Avoid:** 'To me, this paper was very inspiring' | Generic praise that would fit any paper

**Example:** Judged against its aim of making experimental L2 research more trustworthy, this methodological review largely succeeds.

### [Merit: Substance] VARIABLE
**What to write:** Your most important merit, about substance: contribution, framework, argument, design or accuracy. Use FAE: Feature + Anchor + Effect.

**Options:**
- The main strength of the article lies in X.
- The authors make a compelling case for X.
- Particularly valuable is X, which Y.
- The article's central contribution is to X.
- A key strength is the authors' decision to X.
- The article brings together X and Y in a way that Z.
- The framework the authors adopt allows them to X.
- By X, the authors move the discussion beyond Y.
- The argument is carefully built, moving from X to Y.
- The article fills a genuine gap by X.
- The authors' treatment of X is both thorough and balanced.
- Especially welcome is the attention given to X, a topic that is often overlooked.

**How pros do it:** Feature: name the quality precisely. Anchor: point to a section, figure, number or short quote. Effect: say what it does for the reader or the field. Lead with substance; presentation merits come later. Specific praise makes your later criticism more believable.

**Questions to ask:** What does this paper add that the field did not have? | Which choice by the authors made the paper work? | What would a researcher lose if this paper did not exist?

**Avoid:** Praise without an anchor ('detailed information', 'insightful') | Praise that is not true (check before calling references 'the most recent')

**Example:** The paper's most useful feature is the way it makes an abstract framework operational: Figure 2 sets out a single inference as claim, warrant, rebuttal and backing, which readers can transfer directly to their own instruments.

### [Merit: Illustration] VARIABLE
**What to write:** A merit about examples, figures, tables or worked numbers, anchored to a specific one.

**Options:**
- A further strength is the use of X to illustrate Y.
- Figure X, for instance, shows how Y.
- The worked example of X turns Y into Z.
- Abstract points are consistently grounded in concrete L2 scenarios, such as X.
- The argument is strongest where it shows rather than tells: X.
- The authors' use of real data from X lends weight to Y.
- Table X offers a useful at-a-glance summary of Y.
- Each X is illustrated with Y, which makes Z easy to grasp.
- The comparison of X and Y in Figure Z is particularly effective.

**How pros do it:** Name one example and state its function. 'Understandable examples' is a label; 'a worked calculation showing a 26% false-positive risk' is a merit. Figures are prime strength cues in methods papers because they turn theory into procedure.

**Avoid:** 'The figures were nice and neat'

**Example:** A worked calculation shows that six uncorrected comparisons raise the chance of a false positive to about 26%, which turns a general warning about multiple testing into a figure readers will remember.

### [Merit: Presentation & Sources] VARIABLE
**What to write:** A merit about structure, readability, practical tools or the quality of the references.

**Options:**
- The article is also clearly organised, moving from X to Y.
- The appendix offers a practical checklist that readers can apply to X.
- The discussion draws judiciously on both foundational and recent work.
- The prose is accessible without sacrificing precision.
- Technical terms are defined at first use, which suits X.
- The article is concise yet comprehensive in its coverage of X.
- The authors' list of X is likely to be of immediate practical use to Y.
- Memorable maxims, such as X, compress the paper's thesis into a form readers can retain.
- The structure mirrors the stages of X, which makes the article easy to use as a guide.

**How pros do it:** Practical outputs (checklists, verb lists, appendices) are often the most valuable feature and the one novices forget. Describe the reference list accurately: 'a judicious blend of foundational texts and recent syntheses' is better and truer than 'the most recent sources'.

**Avoid:** Making style your first or only merit

**Example:** The closing list of verbs suited to correlational versus causal claims gives readers a tool they can apply in their next report.

### [Strengths Closer] FIXED
**What to write:** One sentence of synthesis: who benefits from the paper and how.

**Options:**
- Taken together, these features make the article a valuable resource for X.
- These strengths make the article particularly useful for X.
- In sum, the article succeeds in X.
- For these reasons, the article deserves a place on the reading list of X.
- These qualities make the article an accessible entry point for X.
- As a result, the article is likely to be widely read and cited by X.
- In short, the article is a clear and practical guide to X.
- Combined, these merits make a strong case for X.

**How pros do it:** Name the audience: novice researchers, teachers, test developers, reviewers.

**Avoid:** Ending the section without a closing sentence (all three model commentaries did)

**Example:** Taken together, these features make the article a valuable primer for novice researchers planning their first experiment.

## Weaknesses (about 35% · 300 to 400 words · 3 to 5 flaws)

### [Weaknesses Opener] FIXED
**What to write:** A concessive pivot that keeps the balance, ideally with a preview of the flaws you will discuss.

**Options:**
- Despite these merits, the article has some limitations, concerning X and Y.
- Notwithstanding these strengths, a few shortcomings should be noted.
- Valuable as the article is, it is less convincing on X.
- Despite its many strengths, the article is not without limitations.
- While the article succeeds in X, it is less successful in Y.
- These merits notwithstanding, three aspects of the article call for comment.
- The article's contribution is somewhat weakened, however, by X.
- That said, the article raises some concerns, chiefly regarding X.
- However, a closer reading reveals some limitations, mainly in X and Y.
- Several issues, however, limit the article's usefulness for X.
- Against these strengths must be set a number of weaknesses.
- Some aspects of the article, however, would benefit from further development.
- The article is not, however, without its problems.
- At the same time, the article has three limitations worth noting.

**How pros do it:** 'Despite these merits, the article has three limitations, concerning X, Y and Z' tells the reader exactly what is coming. Keep the tone calm; the pivot is a hinge, not an attack.

**Avoid:** 'Like other papers, this paper had some flaws!!' | Starting the critique with your feelings ('Reading this article I found...')

**Example:** Despite these merits, the review has three limitations, concerning its coverage of designs, the link between threats and designs, and its treatment of ethics.

### [Flaw: Claim] VARIABLE
**What to write:** State the limitation precisely and calmly. Put the most consequential flaw first: substance before structure before style.

**Options:**
- First, the rationale for X remains largely implicit.
- The treatment of X is rather brief.
- A more serious concern is X.
- The most serious limitation is X.
- The coverage of X is uneven.
- The article stops short of X.
- X is asserted rather than demonstrated.
- The claim that X is not fully supported by Y.
- The link between X and Y is never made explicit.
- The article does not explain why X was preferred to Y.
- The argument rests heavily on a single X.
- The balance of the argument tilts towards X.
- The article's own logic calls for X, which is missing.
- X and Y are discussed in separate sections and never brought together.
- Some recommendations would benefit from qualification.

**How pros do it:** Run the fairness filter first: Is it really absent (search the paper for it and two synonyms)? Was it excluded on purpose? Does the genre require it? Would intended readers miss it? Is it about this paper, in this field? The strongest critique shows that the paper's own logic needs the missing piece (an internal-consistency argument). In a methods paper, test whether the advice itself is correct. Look at key features from both sides: a figure can be a strength and a flaw.

**Questions to ask:** Do the claims outrun the evidence (causal language from correlational data; a rule from one example)? | Is the framework or example choice justified, and what does that choice cost? | Do the authors have a stake (their own tool, test, framework)? Is it disclosed? | Did the authors make a promise the text does not keep? | Is the sample, task or setting narrow for the claims made? | Is every 'should' followed by a 'how'?

**Avoid:** 'The paper I wanted': demanding coverage the authors explicitly set aside | Claiming something is missing when the paper covers it | Rhetorical questions instead of claims | Reader reactions ('confusing', 'made me lose track') instead of properties of the text | Speculation ('maybe there are problems') | Criticising a whole research tradition instead of this paper

**Example:** The most serious limitation is the balance of the argument.

### [Flaw: Evidence] VARIABLE
**What to write:** Show where the flaw appears, and first acknowledge what the authors did say. Give a page or section.

**Options:**
- Although the authors note that X (p. n), they do not Y.
- While the article acknowledges X, it stops short of Y.
- The authors concede X, yet Y.
- The authors rightly warn that X, but Y.
- X is mentioned only in passing (p. n).
- The article raises X as a threat but never Y.
- Of the X, only Y receives sustained discussion.
- The authors state that X; however, Y.
- For example, X.
- This is evident in X, where Y.
- As the authors themselves acknowledge, X.
- Section X, for instance, Y.

**How pros do it:** Crediting concessions before pushing further is fairer and sharper: 'Although the authors acknowledge X, they...' Use the paper's own words or numbers as evidence where possible. Precision matters most when you criticise: 'one sentence of the abstract reappears almost word for word' beats 'a paragraph is exactly repeated'.

**Avoid:** Evidence-free assertions ('the whole text is biased') | Overstating the problem

**Example:** The authors state that they do not intend to 'sell' the package and concede its steep learning curve, yet they close by calling it 'the preferred package of researchers in the 21st century'.

### [Flaw: Consequence] FIXED
**What to write:** Why the flaw matters for the paper's own aim or its intended readers.

**Options:**
- As a result, readers may find it difficult to X.
- This leaves unclear whether X.
- This may limit the usefulness of the article for X.
- Consequently, the conclusions apply more readily to X than to Y.
- This matters because X.
- The effect is to X.
- This weakens the claim that X.
- Without X, readers cannot judge Y.
- This makes it hard to generalise the findings beyond X.
- The omission is significant, since X.
- This undercuts the authors' own aim of X.
- Readers new to X may therefore Y.

**How pros do it:** Tie the consequence to the authors' stated purpose or success test: that is what makes the critique fair and hard to dismiss. Use consequence connectors (as a result, therefore, this means); the model commentaries had none.

**Avoid:** Listing flaws without saying why they matter

**Example:** This undercuts the authors' own stated aim of helping readers make more informed decisions.

### [Flaw: Alternative] FIXED
**What to write:** One concrete remedy. A limitation plus a remedy is scholarship; a limitation alone is a complaint.

**Options:**
- It would have been helpful if the authors had X.
- A brief comparison with X would have clarified Y.
- The article would have benefited from X.
- A short section on X would have closed this gap.
- A table crossing X with Y would have let readers see Z at a glance.
- A second, contrasting example would have shown X.
- Making X explicit would strengthen Y.
- A sentence of disclosure about X would have made Y more credible.
- Readers would be better served by X.
- A more balanced account of X would make the case more persuasive, not less.
- Future work could usefully X.
- The authors could have drawn on X to Y.
- Including X would have allowed readers to Y.
- A clearer signal of X would help readers follow Y.
- Pointing readers to X would have addressed this concern.

**How pros do it:** If you cannot think of a remedy, the weakness is not ready: think harder or drop it. Keep the remedy small and realistic: a table, a paragraph, a second example, a disclosure.

**Avoid:** Remedies that ask for a different paper

**Example:** A table crossing the designs with the threats each leaves open would have let readers see the trade-offs at a glance.

### [Further Flaws] FIXED
**What to write:** Shorter blocks for the remaining flaws. Structure and genre conventions (abstract moves, keywords, repetition) and style come last.

**Options:**
- A further limitation concerns X.
- In addition, X is not always signalled.
- Finally, on a more minor point, X.
- Second, X.
- A related issue is X.
- Less serious, but still worth noting, is X.
- Some smaller presentational issues also deserve mention.
- A final, minor concern relates to X.
- Beyond these substantive issues, X.
- On a stylistic note, X.

**How pros do it:** Order: substance, then coverage and balance, then structure, then style. Group related minor points into one sentence.

**Avoid:** Opening the critique with missing keywords and reaching the serious bias fourth

**Example:** Finally, on a more minor point, the piece carries no keywords, which may reduce its discoverability if the venue's format allows them.

### [Final Verdict] FIXED
**What to write:** Weigh the limitations against the merits and tell readers how to use the paper.

**Options:**
- These limitations do not undermine X; rather, they suggest that Y.
- On balance, the article remains a useful starting point for X.
- Read alongside X, the article offers Y.
- On balance, the strengths of the article clearly outweigh its weaknesses.
- None of these reservations detracts from the article's central contribution to X.
- Despite these shortcomings, the article is recommended reading for X.
- In sum, the article is a valuable, if not definitive, guide to X.
- With these caveats in mind, researchers will find much of value in X.
- The article succeeds in X, though Y remains to be done.
- These are, however, minor concerns in an otherwise X article.
- The article is best read as X, ideally together with Y.
- Taken as a whole, the article makes a worthwhile contribution to X.

**How pros do it:** End on a fair, forward-looking note; the reader should know whether and how to use the paper. Match the verdict to the balance of your critique: do not praise lavishly after five serious flaws.

**Avoid:** Ending abruptly after the last flaw (all three model commentaries did)

**Example:** These limitations do not undermine the review's value as a primer; rather, they suggest it is best read alongside a fuller treatment of single-case and time-series designs.

## Helpers

- **Hedges (one per interpretive claim):** appears to | seems to | largely | arguably | may | might | to some extent | somewhat | in part | is likely to | perhaps | it could be argued that | tends to | in some respects
- **Contrast & Concession:** However, | Although X, Y. | While X, Y. | Nevertheless, | Nonetheless, | That said, | At the same time, | Even so, | By contrast, | Yet | Admittedly, X; still, Y. | Whereas X, Y.
- **Consequence:** As a result, | Therefore, | Consequently, | This means that | Thus, | For this reason, | Hence, | In turn,
- **Addition & Sequence:** First, | Second, | In addition, | Moreover, | A further X is | Similarly, | Likewise, | Finally,
- **Example:** For example, | For instance, | Take X, for instance. | A case in point is X. | This is evident in X. | as illustrated by X

## Reporting verbs by function

- **Neutral:** describe, outline, present, introduce, review, trace, survey, note, discuss, examine
- **Argument:** argue, contend, maintain, make the case that, propose, claim (signals doubt), assert (signals doubt)
- **Explanation:** explain, illustrate, demonstrate, show, exemplify
- **Listing:** identify, distinguish, categorise, catalogue, group, enumerate
- **Admission:** acknowledge, concede, recognise, admit
- **Emphasis:** stress, emphasise, highlight, underline
- **Closing:** conclude, recommend, urge, call for, close with
