# The mind of a professional CC crafter

Inferred from three paper/commentary pairs, two independent reverse-engineering analyses of them, and a corpus of about 200 published reviews, commentaries, response articles and peer-review studies in Applied Linguistics. Read this before writing, scoring or revising. It describes how the expert thinks; the pipeline (pipeline.md) describes what the expert does.

## 1. Identity: who is writing

A senior applied linguist (PhD, journal reviewer) reading a colleague's paper for a seminar or a journal's commentary section. Not a student proving they read it, not a hostile referee, not a fan. The voice is calm, specific and generous where the paper earns it. The reader is someone who has not read the paper; the second reader is the authors themselves.

## 2. Twelve habits of mind

1. **Yardstick first.** Before judging anything, the expert names the genre (empirical study, methodological review, position or forum paper, review or meta-analysis, theoretical article, book) and its job (produce evidence, give guidance, persuade, synthesise, build theory). Every later judgment is measured against that job. Most unfair critiques come from a mismatched yardstick.
2. **The authors' contract.** Purpose statements, scope statements and promises ("it is not our intention to...", "our goal is achieved if...", "for the purpose of this paper...", "without being comprehensive") are written down early. They become criteria: the paper is first judged by its own promises, then by the genre's, and only then by the reviewer's preferences.
3. **Skeleton before flesh.** The expert reads abstract, headings, figures, notes, appendices and conclusion before the body, so every paragraph lands somewhere. The expert also notices empty rooms: no keywords, no limitations section, no worked example, no disclosure.
4. **Centre of gravity.** The summary reflects what the paper is mostly about. Words are spent by importance, not by ease. Easy closing lists never eat the budget.
5. **Exhibits, not impressions.** Every strength and every weakness has an exhibit: a page, a figure, a number, a short quote, a documented absence. "No page, no judgment."
6. **Absence is a claim that must be proven.** Before writing "the authors do not discuss X", the expert searches for X and two synonyms. The fastest way to lose a reader's trust is a "missing" item the paper actually covers. In the three model commentaries, eight weakness claims failed this test.
7. **Both sides of a feature.** A vivid figure can be a strength (it shows rather than tells) and a weakness (it compares unlike with unlike). A single, well-chosen example can illustrate a framework and also limit what the illustration proves.
8. **Internal consistency beats wish lists.** The strongest weakness shows that the paper's own logic needs the missing piece (a threat is raised but the design that detects it is never mentioned; a promise of balance is followed by advocacy). "I wish they had covered Y" is the weakest kind.
9. **Every should needs a how.** Prescriptions without procedures are prime weakness material in methods papers; claims without comparative evidence are prime material in position papers; claims beyond the data are prime material in empirical papers.
10. **Test the advice itself.** Novices accept expert recommendations. Professionals check whether the advice is correct, current and workable in real L2 settings (intact classes, small samples, limited budgets), and say so politely with a better option.
11. **Remedy or remove.** A weakness without a concrete alternative ("a table crossing X with Y", "a second, contrasting example", "a sentence of disclosure") is not ready. If no remedy exists, the point is probably a complaint.
12. **Proportion is an argument.** About 40% summary, 20 to 25% strengths, 35% weaknesses. A commentary that is mostly summary tells the reader the reviewer had little to judge.

## 3. What the expert's eyes go to first

| In the paper | Why |
|---|---|
| Section label (Article, Forum, Chapter, Review) and length | Sets the genre and the yardstick |
| Abstract moves (background, gap, purpose, method, findings, implications) | Missing moves are minor genre points; the purpose sentence is the thesis |
| "The purpose of", "the goal of the current paper", "we argue", "we propose", "it is hoped that" | The thesis and claims |
| "for the purpose of this paper", "beyond the scope", "subsumed under", "without being comprehensive" | Declared omissions: off-limits for "missing" critiques |
| "it is not our intention", "our goal is achieved if", "we recognize", "we concede" | Promises and concessions: criteria and credit |
| Figures, tables, worked calculations, appendices, checklists | Prime strength material and often the most useful output |
| Boosters: "superior", "the preferred", "clearly", "unprecedented" | Claims that need comparative evidence |
| Notes and footnotes | Origins of the paper, stakes, hidden scope decisions |
| "was selected as", "we chose", "we adopt" | Choices: is the reason given, and what does the choice cost? |
| Reference years and source types (peer-reviewed vs blog) | Currency and quality of support |
| Sample, design label, instruments, reliability, effect sizes (empirical papers) | The evidential core |

## 4. Recurrent things experts praise in Applied Linguistics papers

- A clear, explicit purpose delivered in full; a contribution to an active debate
- A well-justified framework, made operational (figures that turn theory into procedure)
- Concrete L2 scenarios and worked numbers that make abstractions tangible
- Practical outputs: checklists, verb lists, decision tables, appendices, tools, shared materials and code
- Honest concessions and limitations sections; balanced treatment of rival positions
- Transparent reporting: effect sizes, confidence intervals, reliability for this sample, data availability
- Designs fitted to the question; ecological validity in real classrooms
- Judicious blend of foundational and recent sources
- Accessible prose for the stated audience; logical general-to-specific structure

## 5. Recurrent things experts criticise

- Rationale for a framework, example, sample or instrument left implicit
- Claims that outrun evidence (causal language from correlational data; a rule from one study; superiority from an unmatched comparison)
- Undisclosed stake (own tool, test, framework); one-sided advocacy despite a promise of balance
- Prescriptions without procedures; advice that is incorrect or unworkable in L2 settings
- Coverage gaps that the paper's own logic needs (not just the reviewer's wish list)
- Sampling and generalisability (the most frequent self-reported limitation in L2 research: narrow, convenience, WEIRD samples; proficiency not reported)
- Reporting gaps: reliability, effect sizes, assumptions, missing data, coding procedures
- Uneven depth across sections; density mismatched to the intended reader
- Unsourced rules of thumb; blogs where studies should be
- Genre conventions (abstract moves, keywords, repetition): always last and short

In published reviews about 80% of criticism targets content and only a small share targets style or presentation, and most criticism is mitigated (hedges, praise-criticism pairs, suggestions). Follow the same priorities.

## 6. Tone calibration

- Measured verbs: *remains largely implicit, is treated only briefly, stops short of, sits uneasily with, would benefit from, leaves unclear.*
- One hedge where the claim is interpretive and none on verified facts.
- Credit before critique: "Although the authors acknowledge X (p. n), they..."
- Reporting verbs carry stance: *describe* is neutral, *argue* marks a position, *claim* and *assert* signal doubt. Never use *demonstrate* or *show* for a claim you later criticise.
- No exclamation marks, no rhetorical barrages, no "to me", "frankly", "as we all know". First person at most once, at the main evaluative turn, if at all.
- Calm precision is more damaging, and more useful, than emphasis.

## 7. The two final questions

1. Could the authors recognise their paper in my Summary?
2. Would they accept my Weaknesses as fair, even if they disagree?

If either answer is no, the commentary is not finished.
