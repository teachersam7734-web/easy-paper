# Key works digest

How to read this file
- Bibliographic details (DOI/ISBN) were checked where possible; see `../sources/academic-education.md`.
- Book summaries are condensed from my general knowledge of these widely known books and from publisher descriptions seen this session. I did not read the full texts in this research round. Treat chapter-level details as "verify before quoting". Where I am unsure, it says so.
- Evidence level: STRONG / MODERATE / EMERGING / PRACTICE WISDOM, as in `higher-education-adhd.md`.

## A. Books

### 1. Barkley, R. A. (with Benton, C. M.). Taking Charge of Adult ADHD (2nd ed., 2021; 1st ed. 2010)
Argues: ADHD is a disorder of self-regulation and executive functioning, a problem of doing what you know rather than knowing what to do. Management means changing the environment and building external supports at the "point of performance", alongside treatment.
Gold nuggets
1. Performance, not knowledge: information about what to do is rarely the problem; being able to do it when and where it counts is.
2. Externalize: put time, steps and rewards outside your head (visible timers, checklists, notes where the action happens).
3. Make consequences immediate: delayed payoffs motivate weakly, so build small, near rewards and accountability.
4. Treat it like a chronic condition: get a proper evaluation, learn the facts, build a long-term toolkit.
5. The 2nd edition (publisher description) adds expanded sections on mindfulness, emotional self-control, time management.
Evidence: Barkley's theory (1997) is influential but debated; the practical advice aligns with CBT trials. MODERATE as theory, STRONG for externalizing/structure as a practice supported by CBT evidence.
Easy Paper use: cues and timers at the start of each task; short "next action" lines; visible progress; immediate micro-rewards in the study loop.

### 2. Hallowell, E. M., & Ratey, J. J. Driven to Distraction (1994) and ADHD 2.0 (2021)
Argues (as I recall; verify): ADHD is better understood as a trait with real costs and real strengths; shame is a major, avoidable harm; a strengths-based, relational approach plus lifestyle and treatment helps people thrive. ADHD 2.0 (publisher blurb: "new science and essential strategies for thriving") updates the framing for newer research.
Gold nuggets
1. Strengths and shame: reduce shame, name strengths, align work with interests.
2. Connection matters: people and community as part of the intervention (the authors' "Vitamin Connect" idea in ADHD 2.0, as I recall; verify).
3. Body matters: exercise, sleep, and nutrition are part of the plan (Ratey's wider exercise-and-brain work).
4. Coaching and structure alongside treatment.
Evidence: PRACTICE WISDOM plus some supporting science (exercise, sleep) of mixed strength. Be careful with claims about brain mechanisms from popular books.
Easy Paper use: tone (no shame), strength-first framing, optional movement breaks, social/body-doubling prompts.

### 3. Quinn, P. O. ADD and the College Student (Magination Press, 1994; later revised editions)
Argues: young adults with ADD/ADHD face specific transitions (independence, self-managed time, large open workloads) and can succeed with the right supports, disclosure and strategies.
Gold nuggets (verify against the text)
1. The transition shock: college removes external structure that high school gave.
2. Use support services early; documentation and disclosure processes matter.
3. Build routines: time management, study environment, organization.
4. Plan courses and schedules around your attention patterns.
5. Treat emotions and social life as part of academic success.
Evidence: PRACTICE WISDOM, consistent with DuPaul et al. (2021) that early support predicts outcomes (MODERATE).
Easy Paper use: reduce the early overwhelm of a new reading list: weekly plan, triage, "what to skip" guidance.

### 4. Tuckman, A. More Attention, Less Deficit: Success Strategies for Adults with ADHD (Specialty Press)
Argues: adults with ADHD can build workable systems by working with how their attention actually behaves, using practical, behavior-level tools.
Gold nuggets (as I recall; verify)
1. Make the right thing easy and the wrong thing hard (set up the environment).
2. Externalize reminders and use cues at the point of use.
3. Plan for variable performance rather than demanding consistency.
4. Treat emotional reactions and self-talk as part of the toolkit.
Evidence: PRACTICE WISDOM; the book is a clinician-written guide, not a trial. Publication year was not confirmed in this round (the ISBN listing was verified, the year was not).
Easy Paper use: friction design: pre-built templates, one-click starting points, reminders at the end of each session.

### 5. Solanto, M. V. Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Adult ADHD: Targeting Executive Dysfunction (Guilford, 2011)
Argues: adults with ADHD benefit from a structured CBT that targets executive dysfunction (time management, organization, planning, distractibility) and unhelpful thinking, rather than treating ADHD as primarily a mood problem.
Gold nuggets
1. Three skill domains: organization and planning, distractibility, adaptive thinking.
2. Core tools: calendar, task list, prioritizing, breaking tasks into steps, scheduled work blocks, rewards.
3. Make planning visual and portable.
4. Work with negative automatic thoughts such as "I always fail" or "I will do it later".
5. Based on a manualized program tested in an RCT (Solanto et al., 2010).
Evidence: STRONG for the program (RCT), mostly non-student adults.
Easy Paper use: the planning and task-breakdown structure; "calendar plus task list" habits; thought-reframing prompts in the prompt vault.

### 6. Safren, S. A., Perlman, C. A., Sprich, S., & Otto, M. W. Mastering Your Adult ADHD (Oxford University Press, 2005 workbook) and Safren et al. trials
Argues: a skills-based CBT (organization and planning, reducing distractibility, adaptive thinking) helps adults who still have symptoms despite medication.
Gold nuggets: the same three domains as Solanto, plus problem-solving and relapse prevention; use of a structured workbook; brief weekly sessions.
Evidence: STRONG (RCT: Safren et al., 2010 JAMA). Book ISBN not verified in this round.
Easy Paper use: informs the problem-solving structure and the "what got in the way?" reflection.

### 7. Dodson, W. (various articles and talks): the "interest-based nervous system"
Argues: people with ADHD are motivated by interest, novelty, challenge, passion and urgency (often summarized as INCUP) rather than importance or rewards.
Gold nuggets
1. Importance alone does not start a task; interest or urgency does.
2. Create urgency (deadlines, accountability, body doubling) and novelty for tasks that must be done.
3. Link tasks to personal interest or challenge.
Evidence: PRACTICE WISDOM. I found no controlled test of this model, and it is not a validated scientific construct. Related research on reward and delay processing exists (Sonuga-Barke, 2005; Volkow et al., 2009) but does not test Dodson's framework. Cite as a coaching heuristic only.
Easy Paper use: gamified progress, short deadlines, challenge framing, with an honest label.

### 8. Weyandt, L. L., & DuPaul, G. J. (Eds.). College Students with ADHD: Current Issues and Future Directions (Springer, 2013)
Argues: ADHD in higher education is a distinct clinical and educational topic with specific assessment, treatment and policy issues.
Gold nuggets: broad evidence overview for college students; academic functioning; assessment; interventions; accommodations.
Evidence: academic edited volume (MODERATE).
Easy Paper use: background for the evidence tables.

### 9. Anastopoulos, A. D., & King, K. A. (2015); Anastopoulos et al. (2021): the ACCESS program (see section C)

## B. Learning science landmarks

### 10. Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan, & Willingham (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques
Argues: of ten popular techniques, practice testing and distributed practice have the best evidence; summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonics, imagery for text and rereading rated low.
Nuggets: (1) test yourself; (2) space your study; (3) elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving are moderately useful; (4) feeling fluent is not learning; (5) the weakest strategies are the most popular.
Evidence: STRONG for general students; INFERRED for ADHD.
Easy Paper use: study loops built around retrieval and spacing; highlighting deprioritized.

### 11. Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Karpicke & Blunt (2011), Rowland (2014)
Argue: retrieval practice produces better long-term retention than restudying, even when it feels harder; it also beat concept mapping for learning from science texts; meta-analysis confirms a reliable testing effect.
Nuggets: (1) quiz after reading; (2) retrieval beats rereading; (3) low-stakes frequent quizzes; (4) feedback after retrieval; (5) short repeated retrieval beats one long session.
Evidence: STRONG.
Easy Paper use: end-of-section "close the page and answer" prompts; flashcard-style review.

### 12. Cepeda et al. (2006). Distributed practice
Argues: spacing study sessions improves retention compared with massing them; the best gap depends on how long you need to remember.
Nuggets: (1) several short sessions beat one long one; (2) plan the next review before you finish; (3) spacing works better than cramming for lasting memory.
Evidence: STRONG.
Easy Paper use: review schedule in every output.

### 13. Sweller (1988); Sweller, van Merrienboer, & Paas (1998); Kalyuga et al. (2003): cognitive load theory
Argues: working memory is limited, so instruction should minimize unnecessary load, manage intrinsic complexity, and encourage useful processing; what helps novices may not help experts.
Nuggets: (1) cut extraneous detail; (2) chunk complex material; (3) use worked examples early; (4) fade guidance as expertise grows; (5) do not split attention between sources that must be integrated.
Evidence: STRONG as a framework in educational research; ADHD-specific tests are limited (INFERRED).
Easy Paper use: layout and chunking rules; adaptive scaffolding.

### 14. Mayer (2009). Multimedia Learning (2nd ed.)
Argues: people learn better from words and pictures arranged to fit how the mind processes information.
Nuggets: coherence, signaling, redundancy, contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality.
Evidence: STRONG in general student experiments; INFERRED for ADHD.
Easy Paper use: PDF design rules: signal headings, cut decoration, keep figure and caption together, pre-teach terms. ISBN is from memory, verify.

## C. Landmark ADHD-in-college and adult-intervention papers

### 15. Anastopoulos et al. (2021), ACCESS RCT; Eddy et al. (2021); Langberg et al. (2023)
Argues: a two-semester CBT group plus mentoring program improves ADHD symptoms, executive functioning and use of campus accommodations, with moderate gains in self-reported study skills and time management.
Nuggets: (1) 250 students, randomized; (2) effects medium to large on symptoms/EF; (3) no GPA change in the trial window; (4) knowledge and behavioral strategies are key mechanisms; (5) adaptive thinking changes were small but strongly related to outcomes.
Evidence: STRONG (for stated outcomes).
Easy Paper use: skill-first, not just accommodation-first, framing; do not promise grades.

### 16. Safren et al. (2010, JAMA); Solanto et al. (2010, AJP); Knouse et al. (2017, meta-analysis)
Argue: CBT-type skills training reduces symptoms in adults, including medicated adults with persistent symptoms; meta-analysis supports CBT for adult ADHD overall.
Nuggets: (1) active-control comparison improves confidence in the effect; (2) structured modules; (3) emphasis on time management and organization; (4) the Knouse paper carries a published correction notice; (5) the effect sizes depend on rater type and control (check the paper).
Evidence: STRONG.

### 17. DuPaul et al. (2021). Academic trajectories of college students with and without ADHD
Argues: students with ADHD show lower GPAs, fewer study skills and lower persistence; support starting before college and addressing executive functioning and depression predicts better outcomes.
Evidence: MODERATE (longitudinal, observational).
Easy Paper use: justify study-skill focus and mood check-ins.

### 18. Extended time research (Miller 2013; Lovett & Leja 2013; Jansen 2018; Lewandowski 2007; Wadley & Liljequist 2013)
Argue: extra time is not a differential boost for ADHD; many students do not use it well.
Nuggets: see the table in `higher-education-adhd.md`, section 3.
Evidence: consistent across several small to moderate studies (MODERATE).

### 19. Brann et al. (2025). Metacognitive scaffolding for digital reading
Argues: staged guidance before, during and after reading narrows comprehension differences between adults with and without ADHD.
Nuggets: (1) 210 adults; (2) randomized; (3) mind-wandering mediated the gap; (4) scaffolding improved confidence calibration; (5) designed for digital reading.
Evidence: EMERGING (one RCT).
Easy Paper use: staged reading prompts in Deep Dive mode.
