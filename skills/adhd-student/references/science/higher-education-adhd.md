# Higher education and ADHD: what works, how strong the evidence is

Not medical advice. Medication is mentioned only in general terms; decisions about it belong to the student and a clinician.

Evidence labels used in this file
- STRONG: multiple randomized trials or meta-analyses in the relevant population (or very robust general learning science).
- MODERATE: one good RCT, several quasi-experiments, or strong evidence from general students that plausibly transfers.
- EMERGING: pilots, open trials, small RCTs, single studies.
- PRACTICE WISDOM: widely used by clinicians and coaches, little or no controlled testing.
- INFERRED: the research is on non-ADHD students; applying it to ADHD is a reasoned bet, not a proven result.

Source key: every citation here is listed with its DOI or ISBN in `../sources/academic-education.md`. Anything marked "(unverified)" could not be confirmed.

## 1. The problem in numbers (why this matters)

| Finding | Source | Note |
|---|---|---|
| College students with ADHD had lower GPA (Hedges g about -0.46 to -0.63) and reported much less use of study skills (g from -1.00 to -2.28) than peers, over four years | DuPaul et al. (2021) | Study skills gap is large: it is a trainable target |
| Four-year persistence: 59.1% of comparison students vs 49% of unmedicated ADHD students | DuPaul et al. (2021) | Association, not causation |
| Stronger predictors of success: better executive functioning, fewer depressive symptoms, high school Section 504 accommodations, college support services | DuPaul et al. (2021) | Support early, and treat mood as part of study support |
| Systematic review of 21 studies: academic regulation, academic self-efficacy, emotional regulation, ADHD symptoms, and academic/social integration predict college success | Stevens et al. (2021) | Suggests intervention targets: self-regulation, confidence, emotion, belonging |
| Working memory differences are linked with academic achievement in college students with ADHD (pilot) | Gropper & Tannock (2009) | Supports reducing working-memory load in study design |

Takeaway: the gap is more about self-regulation and study strategy than about intelligence or knowledge. That is good news, because strategies and structure can be taught and scaffolded.

## 2. Interventions for college students with ADHD

### 2.1 ACCESS (Anastopoulos and colleagues) - cognitive-behavioral group + mentoring
Evidence: STRONG for symptoms and executive functioning; NOT shown for GPA.

- Design: group CBT plus individual mentoring over two semesters (active phase plus maintenance boosters). Three presumed mechanisms: ADHD knowledge, behavioral strategies (organization, planning, study skills), and adaptive thinking.
- RCT (N = 250, two public universities, immediate vs 1-year-delayed treatment): significantly greater gains in ADHD symptoms, executive functioning, clinical change mechanisms and use of disability accommodations; effects medium to large (d about .39 to 1.21) (Anastopoulos et al., 2021).
- Functional outcomes (Eddy et al., 2021): moderate improvements in self-reported study skills, time management, daily functioning and well-being, maintained or increased after the second semester. No significant change in transcript outcomes (GPA, credits) in that analysis, and no improvement in interpersonal functioning.
- Mechanisms (Langberg et al., 2023): gains in behavioral strategies and ADHD knowledge were large; faster improvement in behavioral strategies went with big symptom and functioning gains; adaptive-thinking change was small but strongly tied to outcomes.
- Honest reading: it is the best-tested campus program. Skills and symptoms improve; objective grades did not move within the trial window. Do not promise grade gains.

### 2.2 CBT and metacognitive therapy for adults with ADHD (Safren, Solanto)
Evidence: STRONG that CBT-type skills training helps adults, including medicated adults with leftover symptoms; mostly non-student adult samples.

- Safren et al. (2005; 2010): CBT for medication-treated adults with persistent symptoms. The 2010 JAMA RCT compared CBT with relaxation plus educational support and found CBT superior on clinician-rated ADHD symptoms.
- Solanto et al. (2010): 12-week meta-cognitive therapy (organization/planning, time management, adaptive thinking) beat a supportive-therapy control on measures of time management, organization and planning, and ADHD symptoms.
- Knouse et al. (2017): meta-analysis of CBT for adult ADHD. I did not extract effect sizes for this file; read the paper for numbers and for how effects change with blinded raters and active controls. Note: the journal issued a correction notice (see source list).
- Knouse & Safren (2010): review of the status of CBT for adult ADHD.
- Common active ingredients: calendar and task-list systems, breaking tasks into steps, prioritizing, scheduling and rewarding, reducing distractibility, and challenging unhelpful thoughts ("I will do it later when I feel like it").

### 2.3 Other campus programs
- Organizational, time management and planning (OTMP) skills group: 6 group + 2 individual sessions; pilot with 30 students showed improvements in inattention, impairment and OTMP skill use; no control group (Hartung et al., 2022). EMERGING.
- DBT skills training pilot RCT in college students with ADHD (Fleming et al., 2014). EMERGING.
- Group executive self-management CBT (Solanto line of work): open trial replication in an urban public college (n = 41) showed feasibility and symptom/executive improvements without a control group (Solanto et al., 2026, journal article found via Consensus; DOI not retrieved). EMERGING.
- A 2024 systematic review of psychosocial interventions for university students with ADHD (9 studies) named CBT, interpersonal group therapy and coaching as the best-supported for well-being outcomes (Ali et al., 2024). MODERATE for well-being; thin for academics.

### 2.4 ADHD coaching
Evidence: EMERGING to MODERATE. Quasi-experimental and qualitative work, few large RCTs.

- Prevatt & Yelland (2015): coaching for college students was associated with improved learning/study strategies and self-regulation compared with a non-coached group (check paper for design details and limits).
- Parker et al. (2011): student perceptions of coaching and self-control (qualitative/perception data, not outcome proof).
- Takeaway: weekly accountability, goal-setting and planning conversations are plausible active ingredients; the AI "study buddy" in the skill can play a limited version of this, but it is not a clinician or a coach.

### 2.5 Academic skills and organization training vs accommodations alone
Evidence: MODERATE, from a younger sample.

- Harrison et al. (2020) randomized 64 middle-school students with ADHD to interventions (organization training, self-management, note-taking instruction) or accommodations (organization support, extended time, copies of teacher notes). The intervention group was more likely to keep an organized binder and to take complete, accurate notes. The principle (teach the skill, do not only remove the barrier) is the point, but the age group differs from university students.

### 2.6 Medication (general only)
Medication is a common treatment option for ADHD, and in the DuPaul et al. cohort medicated and unmedicated students differed in persistence. Whether and how to use medication is a clinical decision. The skill does not advise on it. Study systems work alongside medication, and CBT trials (Safren) show added benefit for adults who remain symptomatic on medication.

## 3. Accommodations: what extended time does and does not do

Evidence: STRONG-ish negative finding. The research is consistent that extra time is not a specific boost for ADHD.

| Study | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Miller et al. (2013) | 38 college students with ADHD, 38 controls; reading comprehension | Extra time helped both groups; the authors concluded extended time is not specific to ADHD and not necessarily needed for every diagnosed student |
| Lovett & Leja (2013) | College students | More ADHD and executive-function symptoms went with benefiting LESS from extra time; students' own sense of need did not predict benefit |
| Lewandowski et al. (2007) | Grades 5-7, math | No differential boost: ADHD group did not gain more than controls |
| Wadley & Liljequist (2013) | 61 ADHD, 68 controls; math | Extended time had no significant effect on scores or on time used |
| Jansen et al. (2018) | 30 ADHD, 30 controls; simulated exam at +0%, +33%, +50% | Students used the extra time but performance did not rise; helpful strategies (such as marking key words) were rarely used |
| Lee et al. (2010) | University students with ADHD | Computerized test format improved performance; amount of time did not |
| Gelbar et al. (2020) | 596 students with disabilities, 3,726 exams | Extended time used on about half of tests; use varies |

What to take from this
1. Extra time alone does not fix what actually costs ADHD students marks: losing the thread, rushing, skipping questions, poor time allocation, and anxiety.
2. It still can be useful. Individual students may benefit, institutions decide eligibility, and nothing here says "refuse accommodations". The skill should say: use what you are granted, and also train exam strategy (below).
3. Format and environment (computerized format, quiet room, breaks, clear layout) may matter as much as time (Lee et al., 2010); evidence is thin.
4. Teach "use the time well": a plan for the extra minutes (first pass, second pass, review of flagged items). Jansen et al. found students did not change strategies when given time.

Not covered: legal rights and process differ by country and institution. Direct students to their disability services office.

## 4. Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

Idea: plan materials and assessment with options for how people take in information, show what they know, and stay engaged (multiple means of representation, action/expression, engagement), so fewer students need special fixes.

Evidence: EMERGING to MODERATE, and honestly weak on outcomes.
- Capp (2017) meta-analysis of 18 studies (2013-2016): UDL appears effective for improving the learning process for all students, but impact on educational outcomes was not demonstrated.
- Almeqdad et al. (2023) systematic review and meta-analysis (13 studies): significant effects in some subgroups, but considerable heterogeneity and mostly one-group designs (DOI not retrieved).
- Cumming & Rose (2021, online; 2022 in print): rapid review of 52 articles in higher education: UDL well supported by theory, high satisfaction among students and instructors.
- Seok et al. (2018): 17 postsecondary studies, 15 reported effective outcomes (published in Open Journal of Social Sciences, DOI not retrieved).
- Recent higher-education reviews call out that causal links between specific UDL actions and outcomes are still lacking.

Use in Easy Paper: UDL is a design philosophy, not a proven treatment. It supports offering the same content in several forms (summary, outline, glossary, audio-friendly script, visual map) and letting the student choose. Say it plainly in the skill: "choice and flexibility are reasonable and low-risk; do not claim UDL has been proven to raise grades."

## 5. Note-taking and reading with ADHD

### Note-taking
- Taking notes beats not taking notes, especially for people with more ADHD symptoms: students with higher symptoms learned more with handwritten or typed notes than with no notes; slower handwriting and weaker sustained attention were linked to higher symptoms, so the best modality may depend on writing speed (Shimko et al., 2025). EMERGING.
- Teaching a note-taking procedure helped adolescents with ADHD improve comprehension and behavior (Evans et al., 1994) and take more complete notes (Harrison et al., 2020). MODERATE, young samples.
- Meta-analytic background: Kobayashi (2005) examined what limits the encoding benefit of note-taking. For general learners, note-taking is more useful when the person processes the material, and reviewing notes helps. INFERRED for ADHD.
- Laptop versus longhand: Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) argued longhand is better because it forces summarizing; the paper has a published correction notice and the finding's replication status is contested (not re-verified here). Do not rely on it; the choice should follow the student's transcription speed and preference.
- Note quality predicts test performance (Gleason, 2012 dissertation, adolescents: unpublished, treat as a pointer only).

### Reading comprehension
- A review of 34 studies concluded reading comprehension is impaired in ADHD overall, most clearly on retelling and central-idea tasks, and that results depend on the task; some studies found improvement when task demands were low (Parks et al., 2021). EMERGING.
- A 2025 RCT with 210 adults (about half with ADHD) reading a long digital text: staged metacognitive scaffolding (guidance before, during and after reading) removed the comprehension and confidence gap between ADHD and non-ADHD readers; mind-wandering mediated the gap in the control group (Brann et al., 2025). EMERGING but directly relevant: this is close to what a guided "Deep Dive" does.
- Reading interventions for school-aged children with ADHD (decoding-focused, 30+ hours) are effective in meta-analysis (Chan et al., 2022). Not applicable to adult academic reading; included to avoid over-generalizing.
- Mind wandering can be reduced by training in general student samples (Mrazek et al., 2013, mindfulness and GRE performance). INFERRED for ADHD.

## 6. General learning science that helps everyone (and is often tested least in ADHD)

### 6.1 Dunlosky et al. (2013): ten techniques rated
| Rating | Techniques |
|---|---|
| High utility | Practice testing; distributed practice |
| Moderate | Elaborative interrogation; self-explanation; interleaved practice |
| Low | Summarization; highlighting/underlining; keyword mnemonic; imagery for text; rereading |

Caveat: mostly non-ADHD samples, and the rating concerns general learners. Treat as STRONG for general students, INFERRED for ADHD. A key implication: the techniques that feel productive (rereading, highlighting) are the weakest, and for a person who easily drifts, passive rereading is especially risky.

### 6.2 Retrieval practice
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006): testing beats restudying for long-term retention, even though restudying feels better. Karpicke & Blunt (2011): retrieval practice beat concept mapping for learning from science texts. Rowland (2014) meta-analysis confirms a robust testing effect. Roediger et al. (2011) list ten benefits of testing.
- ADHD use: low-stakes self-quizzes give frequent feedback, short tasks and a built-in "level" (a mini game). Keep quizzes short and mixed difficulty.

### 6.3 Spacing
- Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis: spread practice beats massed practice for verbal recall. STRONG. ADHD use: plan 3 to 5 short passes rather than one marathon; because deadlines drive activation, build mini-deadlines (the next review date) into the calendar.

### 6.4 Cognitive load theory
- Sweller (1988) and Sweller et al. (1998): working memory is limited; design should reduce extraneous load, manage intrinsic load by chunking, and support germane processing. STRONG as a framework in general education research.
- Kalyuga et al. (2003): expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can hurt experts. ADHD use: heavier scaffolding at first reading, fading as the student masters the topic.
- Link to ADHD: working-memory limits are repeatedly reported in ADHD (Kofler et al., 2011 for children; Gropper & Tannock, 2009 for college students). INFERRED that external memory (checklists, outlines) will help.

### 6.5 Mayer's multimedia principles
Mayer (2009) summarizes principles: coherence (cut extras), signaling (highlight the structure), redundancy (do not duplicate text and narration of the same words), spatial and temporal contiguity (put related text and picture together in space and time), segmenting (let the learner pace chunks), pre-training (teach key terms first), modality (graphics with narration), multimedia (words plus pictures), personalization and voice.
- Evidence: STRONG in general student experiments; not tested specifically for ADHD adults (INFERRED). Use these as design rules for Easy Paper PDFs: signal headings, cut decoration that carries no meaning, put figure and caption together, pre-teach vocabulary, break content into short segments.

## 7. Interest, novelty and urgency (Dodson's idea) - evidence level
Dodson's "interest-based nervous system" (people with ADHD are driven by interest, challenge, novelty, passion and urgency rather than importance) is widely used by coaches and in patient education.
- Evidence level: PRACTICE WISDOM. It is not a peer-reviewed, validated model, and I found no direct test of it in this research round (unverified as a scientific construct).
- Compatible science: reward and delay processing differences are reported in ADHD research (Sonuga-Barke, 2005, delay aversion and dual-pathway models; Volkow et al., 2009, dopamine reward pathway findings). These support "immediacy and reward matter" in general, not Dodson's specific framework.
- Safe use in the skill: it is a useful lens for designing behavior (add novelty, make urgency visible, add challenge, tie to personal interest). Phrase as "a helpful way to think", never as "science shows".

## 8. Practical takeaways

### 8.1 Studying papers (articles)
1. Orient before reading (title, abstract, headings, figures, conclusion): this works as pre-training and signaling (Mayer). INFERRED.
2. Set a question to answer, then read in staged chunks with a pause after each: metacognitive scaffolding before, during and after reading narrowed the gap in Brann et al. (2025).
3. Write a one-sentence summary per section in your own words (active processing; avoid highlighting-only, Dunlosky).
4. Close the paper and retrieve: "What was the research question, method, key result, limitation?" Then check.
5. Space it: second pass the next day, third pass a week later, short.
6. Time-box (25 to 40 min blocks) and write the next action before stopping, to cut restart cost. PRACTICE WISDOM, consistent with CBT planning skills.

### 8.2 Studying textbook chapters
1. Preview, set page and time targets, convert headings into questions.
2. Read a section, then write 2 to 3 retrieval questions; answer them from memory after the chapter.
3. Build one-page maps or comparison tables (organizing); use them for self-quizzing, not as decoration.
4. Interleave topics across sessions when they are related.
5. Keep a "parking lot" for tangents so curiosity does not derail the session.

### 8.3 Exams
1. Expect extra time not to be enough on its own; plan how to spend it (first pass, flagged items, review).
2. Practice under exam conditions with timed retrieval sets (retrieval practice; spaced).
3. Use a written exam routine: read all questions, mark key words (Jansen et al. found this rarely used), allocate minutes per question, answer easy items first.
4. Manage anxiety and mood as part of preparation (DuPaul et al., 2021, depression/anxiety as predictors).
5. If the format is a choice, ask disability services about computerized formats and reduced-distraction rooms (Lee et al., 2010 evidence is limited).

### 8.4 Environment and AI use
- Design the session so the AI does the structuring (chunking, scheduling, quizzing) and the student does the thinking (explaining, retrieving). This keeps the benefits of effortful processing.
- Offer choices of format (UDL) but keep the default simple to avoid decision fatigue.
- Never present the AI as a replacement for clinical care, coaching, or disability services.

## 10. Gaps and cautions
- Most strategy-level evidence (Dunlosky, Mayer, Sweller) is from non-ADHD samples. Treat transfer as a reasoned bet and say so.
- No trial found in this round tests an AI study partner for ADHD students (unverified; absence from my searches only).
- Search tool limits: one literature search for the note-taking/reading query and one for UDL were returned by a semantic engine; no exhaustive systematic search was run.
- Books are covered in `key-works-digest.md`.

## 11. Turning the evidence into design rules (for Easy Paper modes and QC agents)

| Rule | Why (evidence) | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Open every output with a 30-second orientation: purpose, time needed, what to ignore | Pre-training and signaling (Mayer); reduces extraneous load | INFERRED |
| Keep one idea per chunk, with explicit headings and a clear next step | Cognitive load; segmenting; working-memory limits in ADHD | INFERRED/MODERATE |
| Put a retrieval question at the end of each section, answers hidden or at the end | Testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke; Rowland) | STRONG (general) |
| Suggest a review schedule (next day, one week, one month) | Spacing (Cepeda) | STRONG (general) |
| Provide staged reading prompts: before, during, after | Brann et al. (2025) | EMERGING |
| Offer a skim path and a deep path for the same content | UDL (flexibility); time efficiency | PRACTICE WISDOM |
| Make the first action tiny and concrete ("read the abstract and write one sentence") | CBT planning, task breakdown (Safren; Solanto) | STRONG (adult CBT) |
| Avoid decoration that carries no meaning; keep figure and caption together | Coherence and contiguity (Mayer) | STRONG (general) |
| Never rely on highlighting or rereading as the only study step | Dunlosky ratings | STRONG (general) |
| Include time boxes and breaks, and a "parking lot" for tangents | Executive-function support; mind-wandering evidence | PRACTICE WISDOM |
| Add a mood and stress check-in line when workload is high | Depression/anxiety predict outcomes (DuPaul 2021) | MODERATE |
| State uncertainty honestly; do not promise grade gains | ACCESS GPA result (Eddy 2021) | STRONG |

### QC checklist questions (yes/no)
1. Can a reader tell in 10 seconds what this page is for and how long it takes?
2. Is every section short enough to finish in one sitting without losing the thread?
3. Is there a retrieval prompt, not just a summary?
4. Is the next action explicit and small?
5. Are terms defined before they are needed?
6. Are claims about evidence labelled by strength?
7. Are optional extras clearly marked as optional (no guilt, no wall of text)?
8. Is anything stated as proven that only has practice-wisdom support?

### What a student can ask an AI study partner to do (and what not)
- Do: chunk a paper, set a timer plan, quiz from memory, ask for an explanation after the student has tried, schedule reviews, check understanding by asking the student to teach it back.
- Do not: replace reading entirely with summaries (summaries without retrieval give the feeling of knowing), write the assignment, or give medical, diagnostic or medication advice.
- Principle: let the AI hold the structure (plans, reminders, chunking), and keep the learning work (retrieving, explaining, deciding) with the student.
