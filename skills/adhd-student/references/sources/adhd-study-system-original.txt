Applied Linguistics and SLA Reading
The routine, every AI prompt, and a complete worked example built from your own PDF

About this guide
One routine covers every reading unit: a journal article, an empirical paper, or a chapter from a standard MA
Applied Linguistics and SLA text. You read in short sprints, each aimed at a question you wrote down. You write
or draw constantly, and the AI plays four roles: pre-reading guide, recall checker, sparring opponent, and
quizmaster. It never reads for you. Notes live in one dedicated notebook, one double-page mind map per unit.
The worked example uses the article at the start of your file, AI_in_language_teaching_learning.pdf: Weiming
Liu, “The Integration of ChatGPT in Language Education” (IGI Global, 2024, printed pp. 1–21). Every page number
in this guide is the printed number at the top of each page in your PDF. It is Unit 1 of a four-unit day: you see
this one unit worked in full, and Part 3 shows how the other three fit around it.

Part 1. The System
Core principles for a wandering mind
• Short sprints, never marathons. Read for 12 minutes, then take a 3-minute break. Use a visible timer, not
   your phone.
• Every sprint has a mission. Never read “the next section.” Read to answer a hunt question you wrote
   down, with the page range where it is likely answered.
• Constant output. Writing, drawing, speaking, and answering questions anchor the mind. Passive reading
   lets it drift.
• Novelty and stakes. Change the activity every 10 to 15 minutes. Turn the AI into an opponent with a score,
   so something is on the line.
• Lower the cost of starting. Begin every unit with the 2-minute ignition ritual below, so you never have to
   decide how to start.
• Catch distractions, do not fight them. Keep a Parking Lot box on every page. When a stray thought,
   errand, or tangent appears, write three words in it and go back.
• Page anchors. Write the page number next to every keyword (p.9). You can re-find anything in seconds, and
   it trains you to cite.
• Skim on purpose. Literature reviews, statistics tables, long example lists, and reference lists are skipped
   unless they are a hunt target.
• Move in breaks. Stand, stretch, walk, drink water. Screens hijack attention and make the return harder.
• Reward after, not during. Choose tonight’s reward before you begin.
• Never skip blind recall. If time runs short, shrink the sparring match or the connections check. Stage 4
   stays.

The notebook system
• One double page per unit. Centre: the unit’s central question in your own words.
• Branches: Method (if the unit reports one), the main content branches (research questions, concepts,
   studies, evidence), Limits & Critique, and Connections (links to other SLA theories).
• Limits & Critique has two columns: what the authors admit, and what you notice (type of evidence,
   sample size, claims versus data). This is where exam essays come from.
• Style: keywords, arrows, small symbols, colour. No full sentences.
• Corner boxes: Confusions (anything unclear, to resolve with the AI) and Parking Lot (stray thoughts).
• Colour code: one pen colour per unit for the main map. Corrections from blind recall and sparring always go
   in red, in every unit, so your weak spots show at a glance.
• Scoreboard strip along the bottom: sprints finished, blind-recall score, sparring rounds survived, confidence
   before sparring.

Daily setup (25 minutes, once per day)
1. Put your phone in another room or a drawer. If you need it as a timer, use airplane mode.
2. Clear the desk: PDFs, notebook, one pen per unit plus a red pen, water, timer.
3. Open one fresh AI chat per unit and name each chat with the unit’s mission name.
4. On each unit’s notebook page, write the mission name and draw the empty scoreboard strip.
5. Assign the AI a role for each unit (role cards below) and note it on the page.
6. Choose the order of the day: lightest unit first, the heaviest reading when your energy is highest, usually
   second or third. Rotate the order across days.
7. Choose tonight’s reward and write a sticky note with the day’s target: beat yesterday’s total score.
The 2-minute ignition ritual (before every unit): stand up, drink some water, write the mission name and date,
set the timer for the 8-minute preview, and press start before you feel ready. Starting is the whole job.

Role cards (paste one into the opener):
• Strict examiner: You are a strict but fair MA examiner. You are formal, precise, and unimpressed by vague
   answers.
• Curious beginner: You are a smart first-year student who has never heard of this topic. Ask me naive
   questions and make me explain things simply.
• Rival researcher: You are a researcher from a competing theoretical camp. You respectfully dispute my
   reading, question the method, and look for weaknesses.

Part 2. Worked Example, Unit 1: The Liu Article
Mission name: “Case File 1: Does ChatGPT Really Help Learners?” · Pen colour: blue · AI role: strict examiner
· Unit type: article (three sprints).
The article is a review of 12 empirical studies. Its structure is clean (research questions, method, four themes of
findings, implications), so it is a good first unit. Before you begin, do the ignition ritual, open a fresh chat named
with the mission, and paste the Stage 0 opener with the role card for the strict examiner. Then upload the PDF.

Stage 1: Preview (8 minutes, no AI)
Do this. Read only these parts, in this order:
• Title and abstract, p.1, and the start of the introduction, p.1–2 (stop at “Literature Review”).
• The four research questions, p.3–4.
• The headings only: Methodology (p.4), Results and Discussion (p.6), Implications (p.14), Conclusion (p.16).
• Table 4 in the appendix, p.20–21. It has one row per study, so it is the article’s whole evidence base on two
   pages.
• The Conclusion, p.16, and the Key Terms list, p.19. Ignore the reference list, pp.16–19.
Then in the notebook:
1. In the centre of a fresh double page, write the central question in your own words.
2. Draw seven empty branches: Method, RQ1 Contexts, RQ2 Benefits, RQ3 Challenges, RQ4 Implications, Limits
   & Critique, Connections. Add the Confusions and Parking Lot boxes.
3. Write a one-line prediction. Guessing first turns reading into a test of your guess.

  WORKED EXAMPLE · YOUR PAGE AFTER STAGE 1

  Centre: What do 12 empirical studies show about ChatGPT in language education: contexts, benefits, challenges,
  implications?
  Prediction: Mostly positive findings, but the evidence will be thin.
  Seven empty branches drawn from the headings; Parking Lot and Confusions boxes in the corners.



Stage 2: AI pre-reading guide (4 minutes)
Do this. Upload the PDF (or confirm it is attached), paste the prompt, and copy the three hunt questions into
your notebook. Add the key terms to the map as empty bubbles.

  COPY AND PASTE

  Give me a pre-reading guide for this PDF. Do not summarize the whole thing and do not reveal the
  full findings. Provide:

  1. The central question the unit addresses, in one sentence.
  2. The unit's structure, in one line (for an empirical paper: the research question, participants,
  and method in one line).
  3. 4 to 6 key terms with a plain one-line definition each, with the page where each appears.
  4. Exactly 3 hunt questions I should find answers to while reading: one conceptual, one about
  evidence or method, and one about limits or significance. Give the page range where each is most
  likely answered.
  5. A sprint plan: split the unit into reading sprints of about 12 minutes each, by page range, and
  say what to skim (for example literature review, heavy statistics, long examples) unless it is a
  hunt target.

  Keep it under 220 words. Then wait.


  WORKED EXAMPLE · WHAT THE AI RETURNS (WORDING WILL VARY)

  Central question. What do the 12 empirical journal articles on ChatGPT in language education report about where the
  research was done, what benefits and challenges appear, and what follows for teaching?

sentence you would read. Anything unclear goes in Confusions; stray thoughts go in the Parking Lot.

Do this. Close the PDF. Speak or type everything you remember, in your own words, as if teaching a friend. Do

  WORKED EXAMPLE · WHAT YOU TYPE (IMPERFECT ON PURPOSE)

  “This article is a review of 14 studies on ChatGPT in language teaching, found in Scopus and Web of Science. There are

  and report in this format:

  - Got right: the ideas I explained correctly (brief list)
  - Missed: important ideas I left out (brief list, with the page)
  - Misunderstood: anything I explained incorrectly, with the correction in one sentence and the

     Stage 5: sparring match. Act in your assigned role. Challenge me on the weakest points from my

     Rules:
     - Ask exactly ONE question at a time. Up to 5 rounds.
     - Mix question types: explain a concept, apply it to a concrete learner or classroom example,

     rating was too high, too low, or about right.

     Start round 1 now.

  Link (OUTSIDE THE PDF): the interaction hypothesis. It holds that learners progress through conversation in which

  restate the link in one sentence.

  Xiao and Zhi’s learners revised their own writing and rewrote prompts (p.10).

Part 4. The End-of-Day Quiz
On a full day, open a new chat and upload all four PDFs. If you cannot, upload photos of your four mind maps or
paste your three takeaways per unit. Then paste this prompt. Answer each question in your own words before
reading the reply. Questions 10 to 12 normally link different units, for example “How would the interaction
hypothesis explain the finding in paper 2?” or “Which two units disagree about feedback, and why?”

     COPY AND PASTE

     You are my quizmaster for an MA Applied Linguistics and SLA exam. I have ADHD, so ask ONE question
     at a time and keep replies short. Use the documents I uploaded (one per unit) and name which unit
     and page each question comes from. If I uploaded mind maps instead of PDFs, base questions on
     those and say so.

     Run a 12-question quiz in this order:
     - Questions 1 to 3: definitions and terminology
     - Questions 4 to 6: who argued what, or which study found what
     - Questions 7 to 9: application (apply a concept to a concrete learner, classroom, or study
     example)
     - Questions 10 to 12: cross-unit (for example: How would the interaction hypothesis explain the
     finding in unit 2?). If I uploaded only one unit, make these three questions link that unit to SLA
     theories or to the quality of its evidence instead, and label any outside knowledge OUTSIDE THE
     PDF.

Part 6. Keeping It Going
Making it captivating
• Give every unit a mission name and record the sprint count.
• Race the timer, and beat yesterday’s total.
• Study in the same place at the same time so the routine becomes the cue.
• Use a study partner or a silent video call as a body double if you can. Working beside someone helps many
   people start and stay on task.
• Stop mid-thought on purpose at each break and jot the next sentence you would read.
• Check your energy at every break. If you are fading, eat, drink, or walk outside before the next unit. If you
   take medication for ADHD, plan the densest reading inside its effective window.

Bad-day plan
• Light day: two units, then a 6-question quiz (about 3.5 hours).
• Very low day: one unit only, but all six stages, including blind recall. One complete unit beats four
   half-finished ones.
• Never cut: the preview, the hunt questions, blind recall, and the next-morning review.
• If you derail mid-unit, restart from the next sprint, not from the beginning. Write one line in the Parking Lot
   and keep going.
