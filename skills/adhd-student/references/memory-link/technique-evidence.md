# Evidence for Memory Link techniques

Evidence grades used here: **strong** (meta-analyses, many RCTs or replicated lab results), **moderate** (consistent but narrower or mostly lab or school studies), **emerging** (few studies, indirect), **practice wisdom** (expert consensus, no direct test). Reference status: "checked" means the title, authors, year and volume were matched to a bibliographic index during this research; "book/chapter" means a real book or chapter not indexed by article databases (publisher details given from standard citation, check before formal use).

Honest summary: mnemonics reliably help recall of lists and paired associates, and the effects are large in the lab. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated the keyword mnemonic and imagery for text as low utility for general student learning because of narrow scope, durability and transfer questions. Mnemonics should therefore be used for facts that must be recalled, paired with explanation and retrieval practice, not as the main study method.

---

## 1. Mnemonics: overview

- **Classification and criteria.** Bellezza (1981) classified mnemonic devices and proposed criteria for a good one (for example, the cue must be available at recall and must specify the target). Worthen & Hunt (2011) is the book-length treatment, *Mnemonology: Mnemonics for the 21st Century* (Psychology Press; book/chapter). Worthen and Hunt stress distinctiveness, organisation and the need for imagery and meaning to be at retrieval. Bellezza's review: checked.
- **Dunlosky et al. (2013).** Reviewed ten techniques. Keyword mnemonic: low utility (strong for foreign vocabulary pairs, but evidence for abstract material and long delays is weak). Mnemonic use of imagery for text: low utility. Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation: moderate. Practice testing and distributed practice: high. Summarisation, highlighting, rereading: low. Checked.
- **Educational use.** Levin (1993) reviewed twenty years of classroom mnemonic research and argued for large benefits when used for the right content. Checked (published in The Elementary School Journal, 94(2), 235-244).
- **Evidence grade overall:** strong for short-term and moderate-delay recall of lists and paired associates; moderate for classroom use; weak for deep comprehension and transfer.

## 2. Method of loci (memory palace)

- **What it is.** Place images at ordered locations on a familiar route, then walk the route to recall.
- **Evidence.** Roediger (1980) compared mnemonics for ordered recall and found structured systems (loci, pegs) support order better than unstructured ones (checked). Dresler et al. (2017) trained memory-naive adults for 6 weeks and reported large gains in word-list recall (from roughly 26 to roughly 62 words) with changes in brain network connectivity, compared with controls (checked, Neuron 93(5)). Wagner et al. (2021) followed up with durable memories after training with loci (checked, Science Advances 7(10), eabc7606). Qureshi et al. (2014) reported better performance in endocrinology students using loci to learn content (checked; small, non-randomised classroom context, so moderate at best).
- **Limits.** Takes setup time; best for ordered lists; the gain is for items to recall, not for relationships between ideas; people with weak mental imagery may prefer other devices. Interference when the same route is reused without gaps (practice wisdom).
- **Grade:** strong in lab for lists; moderate for authentic study.

## 3. Peg systems

- **What.** Learn a fixed list of peg words (rhyme, body, shape) and hook each item onto its peg.
- **Evidence.** Pegs and loci both give a stable "scaffold" that improves order and random access (Roediger, 1980; Bellezza, 1981). Direct tests of peg systems with learning-disabled or ADHD adults are scarce (none found for adult ADHD).
- **Limits.** Setup cost, a limited number of pegs, only useful for lists long enough to justify it.
- **Grade:** moderate (mostly older lab work).

## 4. Acronyms and acrostics (first-letter mnemonics)

- **Evidence.** Carlson, Zimmer & Glover (1981), "First-letter mnemonics: DAM (Don't Aid Memory)", found that first-letter cues help recall of the labels when the learner already knows the items, but do not by themselves supply the content (checked). Bellezza (1981) lists first-letter devices as retrieval-cue systems whose power depends on learning the items.
- **Limits.** The code provides only a cue. If the student does not understand the items, the acronym recalls empty labels. Good for criteria lists in methods and for taxonomies after understanding.
- **Grade:** moderate for label recall; practice wisdom for classroom effectiveness.

## 5. Story / link method

- **Evidence.** Bower & Clark (1969): participants who composed narrative stories linking each list's words recalled several-fold more words in order than controls (reported roughly 93 percent versus 13 percent across lists). Checked as a bibliographic record. Roediger (1980) found the link method helped serial recall but less than loci or pegs.
- **Limits.** Stories are fragile if long; one wrong turn derails the chain. Best for lists up to about 8 items per story.
- **Grade:** strong in lab for ordered recall of word lists; moderate for educational content.

## 6. Keyword method

- **Evidence.** Atkinson & Raugh (1975) found large gains for Russian vocabulary learning in the lab (checked). Pressley, Levin & Delaney (1982) summarised the method and its conditions of success (checked). Wang, Thomas & Ouellette (1992) found that keyword advantages on immediate tests could vanish or reverse after a delay (checked). Wang & Thomas (1995) examined long-term retention with similar caution (checked). Beaton, Gruneberg & Ellis (1995) reported a ten-year follow-up of keyword-learned vocabulary (checked; direction of results not independently verified here).
- **Limits.** Strongest for concrete nouns and experimenter-supplied keywords; weaker for abstract terms, for learner-generated keywords with poor imagery and after delays; costs preparation time. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate it low utility overall.
- **Use in Memory Link.** For heavy foreign-sounding terms, use it as a first handle, then test again after a day and a week and add an example or definition.
- **Grade:** strong for immediate vocabulary recall in lab; weak to moderate for durability and abstract content.

## 7. Rhymes and rhythm

- **Evidence.** Rhyme and rhythm add redundant cues and structure but formal studies are fewer; most support is practice wisdom plus general memory-for-verse work (not cited here).
- **Use.** Short spoken lists. **Grade:** practice wisdom / emerging.

## 8. Chunking

- **Evidence.** Miller (1956) popularised capacity limits; Cowan (2001) argued for about four chunks in working memory (both checked as records; the Cowan title includes the subtitle "A reconsideration of mental storage capacity"). Ericsson, Chase & Faloon (1980) showed a runner trained to remember about 79 digits by chunking digits into running times and organising them (checked). Gobet et al. (2001) reviewed chunking mechanisms (checked). Thalmann, Souza & Oberauer (2019) showed that chunking helps working memory by freeing capacity once items are bound into known units (checked).
- **Limits.** The chunk must be meaningful to the learner (prior knowledge); arbitrary chunks give little benefit.
- **Grade:** strong (core cognitive science).

## 9. Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation

- **Evidence.** Pressley et al. (1987): asking "why would this be true?" and generating the answer improved learning of facts compared with reading (checked). Chi et al. (1994): students prompted to self-explain text learned more (checked). Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated both moderate utility; benefits are stronger when learners have some prior knowledge and when the facts are not isolated.
- **Limits.** Needs prior knowledge, time and verbal ability; wrong self-explanations can reinforce errors without feedback.
- **Grade:** moderate.
- **Use.** Ask the student to explain why each item in the list belongs in the set, or why the analogy fits.

## 10. Dual coding and imagery

- **Evidence.** Paivio's dual coding theory (1971 book, *Imagery and verbal processes*, Holt, Rinehart & Winston; book/chapter; and the 1991 review in Canadian Journal of Psychology 45(3), 255-287, not found by the lookup tool but widely cited as a standard reference) holds that verbal and visual codes support each other. Mayer & Moreno (2003) tie this to multimedia learning and cognitive load (checked). Dunlosky et al. (2013) found imagery for text low utility in general.
- **Limits.** Pictures must be relevant; decorative images add load; imagery helps concrete material more than abstract. Redundant text plus identical speech can overload.
- **Grade:** strong for concrete words and lists; moderate for complex ideas.

## 11. Concreteness and concrete examples

- **Evidence.** Concrete words are recalled better than abstract words (Paivio's concreteness effect; Nelson & Schreiber, 1992, checked). Fyfe et al. (2014) reviewed concreteness fading: start concrete, then fade to abstract, helps learning and transfer (checked). Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler (2008) in Science found that abstract (generic) examples can support transfer better than concrete contexts in some maths tasks (the lookup tool could not find it; widely cited, check before formal use). Atkinson et al. (2000) reviewed worked-example principles (checked).
- **Take-away.** Start concrete for initial memory and understanding; then connect to the abstract form to enable transfer. A concrete example alone may stick but not generalise.
- **Grade:** strong for memory of words; moderate for learning transfer.

## 12. Analogies and metaphors

- **Evidence.** Gentner (1983, structure-mapping, Cognitive Science 7(2), 155-170; not found by the tool but a standard reference): good analogies map relations, not surface features. Gick & Holyoak (1980): people often fail to use a relevant analogy spontaneously unless prompted (checked). Alfieri et al. (2013): learning through case comparison has a moderate to large benefit over single-case study (checked).
- **Limits.** Surface-similar but structurally wrong analogies can produce misconceptions (practice wisdom, widely reported in science education). Always state where the analogy breaks.
- **Grade:** moderate.

## 13. Generation effect

- **Evidence.** Slamecka & Graf (1978): items generated by the learner are recalled better than items read (checked). Bertsch et al. (2007) meta-analysis found a reliable but modest benefit (about d = 0.4; recall the exact figure from the paper before quoting it) (checked).
- **Limits.** Benefits are smaller when generation is hard enough to fail, when feedback is absent, or when the learner is overloaded. For ADHD students with executive limits, generation should be scaffolded (give candidates, student picks or edits).
- **Grade:** strong.

## 14. Self-reference and personal links

- **Evidence.** Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker (1977): encoding words relative to the self gave better recall than semantic encoding (checked). Symons & Johnson (1997) meta-analysis confirmed a reliable self-reference advantage (checked). Craik & Lockhart (1972) levels of processing (not found by the tool; classic reference) and Craik & Tulving (1975) underpin the idea that deeper, meaningful processing helps recall.
- **Grade:** strong. **Use:** personal anchors, ask "which of your own experiences does this resemble?"

## 15. Prior-knowledge activation and schema theory

- **Evidence.** Bransford & Johnson (1972): comprehension and recall of an ambiguous passage were much better when learners had a context or title that activated the right schema (checked). Ausubel's advance organisers (1960s, book; not re-verified here) and general schema theory (Bartlett, 1932, book) support linking new material to existing structure.
- **Limits.** Poor or incorrect prior knowledge can mislead; the link must match the real structure.
- **Grade:** strong for comprehension; moderate for memory of arbitrary lists.

## 16. Bizarre vs vivid imagery

- **Evidence.** McDaniel & Einstein (1986) found that bizarre imagery helps mainly through distinctiveness and when the list has both bizarre and common items; it does not reliably beat plain interactive imagery (checked). Take-away: make images vivid, interactive and distinctive, not simply weird.
- **Grade:** moderate (mixed).

## 17. Retrieval practice and spacing (essential companions)

- **Evidence.** Roediger & Karpicke (2006) test-enhanced learning (checked) and Karpicke & Blunt (2011) retrieval practice beat concept mapping (checked). Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis of distributed practice (checked). Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate practice testing and distributed practice as high utility.
- **Use.** Every Memory Link should be followed by cover-and-recall at once, then at 1 day, 1 week, 1 month.
- **Grade:** strong.

## 18. What works for ADHD learners

What the evidence shows and does not show:

| Finding | Evidence | Strength | Memory Link implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working memory is impaired on average in ADHD, with large heterogeneity | Martinussen et al. (2005, children, checked): moderate to large deficits, larger for spatial than verbal; Kasper, Alderson & Hudec (2012, children, checked): moderators matter; Hervey, Epstein & Curry (2004, adults, checked) and Frazier, Demaree & Youngstrom (2004, checked): measurable executive and memory differences in adults and children | Strong (meta-analyses), but averages hide individuals | Keep chunks to 3 or 4, externalise (write it down), do not rely on holding multi-step codes in mind |
| Spatial working memory deficits are larger than verbal in children's data | Martinussen et al. (2005) | Moderate | Method of loci relies on spatial imagery; consider adding word labels at each stop (design inference, not tested in ADHD) |
| Strategic memory is a weak spot: spontaneous use of organisation strategies is limited | Cornoldi et al. (1999, children with ADHD, checked) | Emerging | Supply structure (categories, hooks) rather than expect the student to invent it; check recall of the structure |
| Working memory training does not transfer broadly | Melby-Lervag & Hulme (2013, checked) | Strong (general population meta-analysis) | Do not rely on "training memory"; reduce load and use external aids |
| Mnemonic instruction benefits students with learning and behaviour problems | Mastropieri & Scruggs (1989, checked; DOI 10.1007/BF01326638); Scruggs & Mastropieri (2000, checked) | Moderate; samples mostly learning disabilities and school-age | Mnemonics are a reasonable bet; direct trials in adult ADHD university students not found |
| Generation and self-reference remain beneficial | General memory science; no ADHD-specific test found | Practice wisdom for ADHD | Offer candidates, student chooses or edits (low-effort generation) |
| Interest, novelty and immediate feedback help engagement | Widely cited in ADHD clinical literature; not re-verified here | Practice wisdom | Humour, game-like recall, short sessions, instant check |

Gaps stated plainly: (1) no direct controlled trial of mnemonics for adult university students with ADHD was found; (2) effects for ADHD may be moderated by comorbidity, medication state and task interest; (3) recommendations here for ADHD are design inferences from general memory science plus the ADHD cognitive profile, and should be adjusted to the individual. This is not medical advice.
