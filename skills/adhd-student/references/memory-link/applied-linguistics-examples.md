## A. SLA theory

### 1. Krashen's five hypotheses (Krashen, 1982, 1985)
- **Item:** Acquisition-Learning, Monitor, Natural Order, Input (i+1), Affective Filter.
- **Code (kitchen story, order kept):** *A* kid **acquires** cooking by eating at home but **learns** it from a recipe book (Acquisition-Learning); a kitchen **timer/Monitor** beeps only when there is time, you care about the rule and you know it (Monitor); dishes always come out in the **same natural order**, soup before dessert (Natural Order); the next recipe is **just one step harder** (Input, i+1); and **steamed-up glasses** from stress block the view (Affective Filter). Letters: A-M-N-I-A.
- **Simple:** We pick up language mostly from understandable input, slightly above our level, when we feel relaxed. Study of rules only edits our output.
- **Anchor:** learning to cook by watching a parent versus reading a cookbook.
- **Check:** Krashen's hypotheses are influential but criticised as hard to test (for example, McLaughlin, 1978; Gregg, 1984, not re-verified here). Use for orientation, not as established mechanism.

### 2. Monitor conditions (Krashen)
- **Item:** The Monitor is used when the learner has time, focuses on form and knows the rule.
- **Code:** "Clock, Spotlight, Rulebook" (Time, Form, Knowledge).
- **Simple:** We only self-correct when we have time to think, attend to grammar and remember the rule.
- **Anchor:** Proofreading an email: you read it slowly, look for typos, and know the spelling.
- **Check:** Matches the three conditions in Krashen's Monitor Model. Over-users, under-users and optimal users are separate descriptive types.

### 3. Lyster & Ranta's six corrective feedback types (1997)
- **Item:** Explicit correction, recast, clarification request, metalinguistic feedback, elicitation, repetition (plus learner uptake and repair).
- **Code:** Two buckets. **"Fix it for them" (reformulations):** Explicit ("No, say *went*") and Recast (silently says it right). **"Make them fix it" (prompts):** Pardon? (clarification request), Hint (metalinguistic feedback), Half-sentence (elicitation, "He ...?"), Echo (repetition, with rising tone). Order of first letters: E-R-C-M-E-R ("Every Rainy Cat Meows Every Radio").
- **Simple:** The teacher either gives the right form or nudges the learner to find it. Uptake is what the learner does next.
- **Anchor:** a friend who either tells you the answer or says "try again, think about the past tense".
- **Check:** The six types follow Lyster & Ranta (1997). In their French immersion data recasts were the most frequent type and led least often to uptake, while elicitation, clarification requests and metalinguistic feedback led more often to learner-generated repair. The reformulation versus prompt grouping is from later work (Lyster & Ranta, 2013, not re-verified here).

### 4. Uptake versus repair
- **Item:** Uptake is the learner's immediate response after feedback; repair is correct reformulation of the error.
- **Code:** "Up-take = they take it up (respond); Re-pair = they put the pair back right."
- **Simple:** Uptake means a response; repair means a corrected form.
- **Anchor:** Someone points out a spelling slip: you reply (uptake), you fix it (repair).
- **Check:** Uptake can include non-repair (for example, acknowledgement). Repair is a subset.

### 5. Interaction Hypothesis (Long, 1981, 1983, 1996)
- **Item:** Comprehension is aided by interactional modifications such as negotiation for meaning; interaction provides input, feedback and attention to form.
- **Code (three moves):** "Do you mean...?" (confirmation check), "What?" (clarification request), "Got it?" (comprehension check). Remember "Confirm, Clarify, Check".
- **Simple:** When two people work to understand each other, the repairs make input clearer and show the learner where the language differs.
- **Anchor:** A bad phone line: "Sorry, you said Tuesday?" and you both slow down.
- **Check:** Long (1996) widened the hypothesis to include corrective feedback and learner attention. Negotiation moves are classic categories from Long and Pica's work.

### 6. Output Hypothesis (Swain, 1985, 1995)
- **Item:** Producing language pushes learners to notice gaps, test hypotheses and reflect on language (three functions).
- **Code:** "Notice, Try, Talk": noticing/triggering, hypothesis-testing, metalinguistic reflection.
- **Simple:** Speaking or writing forces you to find out what you cannot yet say.
- **Anchor:** Trying to give directions in a new language and discovering the word you lack.
- **Check:** Three functions are from Swain (1995), a book chapter (not indexed in article databases). Swain did not claim output replaces input.

### 7. Noticing Hypothesis (Schmidt, 1990)
- **Item:** Input becomes intake only if consciously noticed; noticing the gap compares own output with input.
- **Code:** "You can't learn what you didn't notice": a yellow highlighter lighting only part of the page.
- **Simple:** Learners must notice a feature in the input to start learning it.
- **Anchor:** Buy a red car and suddenly see red cars everywhere.
- **Check:** Schmidt (1990, checked) argued noticing is necessary; later work softened this to "more noticing, more learning". Noticing is attention with awareness, not full understanding of the rule.

### 8. Focus on Form versus Focus on Forms versus Focus on Meaning (Long, 1991)
- **Item:** Focus on forms: grammar as the syllabus. Focus on meaning: meaning only. Focus on form: brief attention to form during meaning-based tasks.
- **Code:** "Forms = a whole menu of grammar dishes; Form = one grain of salt dropped into the meaning soup."
- **Simple:** Do not teach a list of rules in sequence; zoom in on a form briefly when a communicative task needs it.
- **Anchor:** A satnav that lets you drive and only speaks up at the wrong turn.
- **Check:** Plural versus singular matters: Long (1991) distinguishes "focus on form" (singular) from "focus on forms".

### 9. Comprehensible input and i+1 (Krashen)
- **Item:** Learners progress when input is slightly beyond current competence (i+1) and understood through context.
- **Code:** "One stair up."
- **Simple:** Aim a little above your level, with enough help to understand.
- **Anchor:** A video game level just hard enough to feel fun.
- **Check:** i+1 is not operationalised precisely; linked to Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development only loosely.

### 10. Selinker's five processes of interlanguage (1972)
- **Item:** Language transfer, transfer of training, strategies of second language learning, strategies of second language communication, overgeneralisation of target language material.
- **Code (suitcase story):** Pack **L1 habits** (transfer), the **teacher's drills** (transfer of training), your **study tricks** (learning strategies), **talk-around shortcuts** (communication strategies) and a **too-big stretch** of a rule (overgeneralisation).
- **Simple:** Learners' own language system (interlanguage) is shaped by five forces.
- **Anchor:** Packing a suitcase for a trip: old clothes, school uniform, tools, shortcuts, extra layers.
- **Check:** Five processes listed in Selinker (1972, checked). Fossilisation is the related idea that some features stop developing.

### 11. Mistake versus error (Corder, 1967)
- **Item:** Mistakes are performance slips the learner can self-correct; errors reflect incomplete knowledge (competence).
- **Code:** "Mistake = slip of the finger; Error = gap in the map."
- **Simple:** If you can fix it yourself when told, it is a mistake; if you cannot, it is an error.
- **Anchor:** Mistyping a word you know versus never knowing the spelling.
- **Check:** Corder (1967, checked). The distinction is conceptual; in real data it is hard to apply.

### 12. Zone of Proximal Development and scaffolding (Vygotsky; Wood, Bruner & Ross, 1976)
- **Item:** ZPD is the gap between what a learner can do alone and with help; scaffolding is temporary, adjustable support.
- **Code:** "Training wheels: on, loose, off."
- **Simple:** Help just above what you can do alone, then gradually remove it.
- **Anchor:** Learning to ride a bike with a hand on the seat.
- **Check:** Vygotsky's term; "scaffolding" was introduced by Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976; the chapter version was matched in the index, the original article is in the Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 17(2), 89-100).

## B. Language and communication models

### 13. Canale & Swain's communicative competence (1980; Canale, 1983)
- **Item:** Grammatical, sociolinguistic and strategic competence (1980); discourse competence added by Canale (1983).
- **Code (road-trip):** **Grammar = road rules**, **Dress code = sociolinguistic** (what fits where), **Story of the trip = discourse** (cohesion and coherence), **Spare tyre = strategic** (repair when it breaks down). Letters: G-D-S-S.
- **Simple:** To communicate you need the code, the right style for the setting, the way sentences hang together and tricks for breakdowns.
- **Anchor:** Texting your boss versus texting your friend.
- **Check:** Three components in Canale & Swain (1980, checked); four in Canale (1983, book chapter). Bachman (1990) proposed a different model.

### 14. Hymes' SPEAKING grid (1972)
- **Item:** Setting/Scene, Participants, Ends, Act sequence, Key, Instrumentalities, Norms, Genre.
- **Code:** The acronym S-P-E-A-K-I-N-G is itself the code. Picture a **wedding speech**: venue (S), guests (P), purpose (E), order of events (A), tone (K), speech or toast (I), etiquette (N), genre of toast (G).
- **Simple:** A checklist of what shapes a speech event.
- **Anchor:** A wedding or a graduation ceremony.
- **Check:** Eight components per Hymes (1972; chapter, details from standard citation).

### 15. Grice's maxims (1975)
- **Item:** Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner under the cooperative principle.
- **Code:** "Portion, Label, Topic, Clarity": right-size portion (not too much or too little), true label, on-topic, clear.
- **Simple:** Cooperative talk gives the right amount, is true, relevant and clear.
- **Anchor:** Ordering coffee: a short, true, relevant, clear order.
- **Check:** Four categories (Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner) per Grice (1975, chapter). Flouting a maxim creates implicature.

### 16. Austin's three acts of speech (1962)
- **Item:** Locutionary (the saying), illocutionary (the intended force), perlocutionary (the effect).
- **Code:** "Say it, Mean it, Effect it."
- **Simple:** The words, what you do with them, and what happens next.
- **Anchor:** "It's cold in here" means "close the window", and someone does.
- **Check:** Austin (1962), *How to do things with words* (book).

### 17. Searle's five classes of illocutionary acts (1976)
- **Item:** Assertives (representatives), directives, commissives, expressives, declarations.
- **Code:** "Tell, Ask, Promise, Feel, Declare" (A-D-C-E-D). Judge with a hammer stands for declarations.
- **Simple:** What we do with speech: say how things are, get others to act, bind ourselves, show feelings, change the world by saying.
- **Anchor:** "I now pronounce you married" changes status; "Congratulations" shows feeling.
- **Check:** Five classes per Searle (1976, checked).

### 18. Halliday's register variables
- **Item:** Field, tenor, mode.
- **Code:** "What, Who, How."
- **Simple:** What is going on, who is involved and by which channel.
- **Anchor:** A WhatsApp voice note to a friend versus an email to a professor.
- **Check:** Systemic functional linguistics; widely used framework (Halliday, various).

### 19. CAF(L) in L2 performance (Skehan; Housen & Kuiken, 2009)
- **Item:** Complexity, accuracy, fluency (and lexis in some versions).
- **Code:** "CAF = a cafe": a complex menu, an accurate order, a fast kitchen.
- **Simple:** Measures of how elaborate, correct and smooth learner language is.
- **Anchor:** Judging a restaurant: menu range, correct order, speed.
- **Check:** Housen & Kuiken (2009, Applied Linguistics 30(4), 461-473; not found by tool; standard). Trade-offs among the three are debated.

### 20. Nation's "what is involved in knowing a word"
- **Item:** Form, meaning and use (each with receptive and productive knowledge).
- **Code:** "Face, Meaning, Habits": the word's face (spoken, written, parts), what it stands for, and where it hangs out (grammar, collocations, constraints).
- **Simple:** Knowing a word means more than its translation.
- **Anchor:** Knowing a person: face, role, habits.
- **Check:** Nation (2001), *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language* (book; details from standard citation).

## C. Research methods and design

### 21. Greene, Caracelli & Graham's five purposes of mixed methods (1989), TCDIE
- **Item:** Triangulation, Complementarity, Development, Initiation, Expansion.
- **Code (soup):** **Triangulation**: two cooks taste the same pot and agree. **Complementarity**: salt and pepper together make the flavour complete. **Development**: the first taste tells you what to add. **Initiation**: a surprising taste contradicts the recipe and makes you rethink. **Expansion**: a bigger menu with different dishes. Acronym T-C-D-I-E ("Tie-dye").
- **Simple:** Why combine methods: to confirm, to complete, to build one on the other, to uncover surprises, or to widen scope.
- **Anchor:** Cooking and tasting.
- **Check:** Five purposes per Greene et al. (1989, checked). Triangulation = convergence/corroboration; complementarity = elaboration/clarification; development = one method informs the other; initiation = paradox/new perspectives; expansion = extend breadth.

### 22. Denzin's four types of triangulation (1978)
- **Item:** Data, investigator, theory, methodological (within-method, between-method).
- **Code (detective):** more **witnesses** (data), more **detectives** (investigator), more **theories of the crime** (theory), more **tools** such as fingerprints plus CCTV (method). Letters: D-I-T-M.
- **Simple:** Cross-check by varying data sources, researchers, theories or methods.
- **Anchor:** A TV detective team.
- **Check:** Denzin (1978), *The Research Act* (book). Data triangulation subtypes: time, space, person.

### 23. Lincoln & Guba's trustworthiness criteria (1985)
- **Item:** Credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability.
- **Code:** "Can They Double-Check?" (C-T-D-C). Quant equivalents: internal validity, external validity, reliability, objectivity.
- **Simple:** Believable, reusable elsewhere, consistent and traceable, and grounded in the data not the researcher.
- **Anchor:** A restaurant review: believable, applies to your town, consistent over visits, based on what was served.
- **Check:** Four criteria per Lincoln & Guba (1985, checked). Authenticity added by Guba & Lincoln (1989, not re-verified here).

### 24. Techniques for credibility (Lincoln & Guba, 1985)
- **Item:** Prolonged engagement, persistent observation, triangulation, peer debriefing, negative case analysis, referential adequacy, member checks.
- **Code (four chunks):** **Spend time** (prolonged engagement, persistent observation), **Cross-check** (triangulation), **Ask others** (peer debriefing, member checks), **Hunt counterexamples** (negative case analysis); **Save raw material** (referential adequacy).
- **Simple:** Stay long, cross-check, ask people, look for what does not fit.
- **Anchor:** A fact-checker's routine.
- **Check:** Seven techniques named in Lincoln & Guba (1985, checked).

### 25. Research design decision tree
- **Item:** True experiment, quasi-experiment, non-experimental (correlational, survey, case study...).
- **Code:** Two questions: "Do I **manipulate**?" and "Do I **randomise**?" Yes+Yes = true experiment; Yes+No = quasi-experiment; No = non-experimental. Image: names drawn from a hat.
- **Simple:** True experiments assign people by chance; quasi-experiments use existing groups.
- **Anchor:** Lottery draw versus using two existing classes.
- **Check:** Campbell & Stanley (1963, book). Classification varies by textbook.

### 26. Mixed methods core designs (Creswell & Plano Clark)
- **Item:** Convergent, explanatory sequential, exploratory sequential (core); embedded, transformative, multiphase (advanced).
- **Code:** "Two rivers meet" (convergent); "numbers first, words explain" (explanatory: quan then qual, ex-PLAIN); "words first, then measure" (exploratory: explore the forest, then build a ruler: qual then quan).
- **Simple:** Same time: convergent. Numbers then words: explanatory. Words then numbers: exploratory.
- **Anchor:** A map: scout the trail, then measure; or measure first, then ask why.
- **Check:** Three core designs per Creswell & Plano Clark (2018; book, 3rd ed.). Terms differ slightly across editions.

### 27. Threats to internal validity: MR SMITH (Campbell & Stanley, 1963)
- **Item:** Mortality, Regression, Selection, Maturation, Instrumentation, Testing, History.
- **Code:** "MR SMITH" (M-R-S-M-I-T-H).
- **Simple:** Seven reasons the outcome may not be due to the treatment.
- **Anchor:** Mr Smith is the accused in a courtroom; list what else might have caused it.
- **Check:** Campbell and Stanley (1963) list eight, including the selection-maturation interaction; MR SMITH is a common teaching shortcut, not their wording.

### 28. Levels of measurement (Stevens, 1946)
- **Item:** Nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio.
- **Code:** NOIR ("black" in French). Jersey names (nominal), podium (ordinal), Celsius (interval), money (ratio, true zero).
- **Simple:** Categories, ranks, equal steps, equal steps with true zero.
- **Anchor:** Sport and wallet.
- **Check:** Stevens (1946, checked).

### 29. L2 effect-size benchmarks (Plonsky & Oswald, 2014)
- **Item:** Between-groups d: 0.40 small, 0.70 medium, 1.00 large. Within-group (pre/post): 0.60, 1.00, 1.40.
- **Code:** "Four, seven, ten" and "six, ten, fourteen"; Cohen's 0.2/0.5/0.8 are too lenient for L2.
- **Simple:** In L2 research, effects need to be bigger to be called small, medium or large.
- **Anchor:** A school grading curve shifted up.
- **Check:** Values from Plonsky & Oswald (2014, checked). Treat as field-specific rules of thumb, not laws.

### 30. Sampling types
- **Item:** Probability: simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster. Non-probability: convenience, purposive, snowball, quota.
- **Code (soup again):** Stir the pot and ladle (random); every fifth spoon (systematic); one spoon per layer (stratified); a whole bowl (cluster). Non-probability: whoever is nearest (convenience), hand-picked (purposive), friends of friends (snowball), fill the box (quota).
- **Simple:** Probability samples give everyone a known chance; non-probability samples do not.
- **Anchor:** Taste-testing soup.
- **Check:** Standard research methods classification.

### 31. Moderator versus mediator
- **Item:** A moderator changes the strength or direction of a relationship; a mediator explains how or why it happens.
- **Code:** Moderator = **volume dial**; Mediator = **middleman or bridge**.
- **Simple:** Moderator: "for whom or when?" Mediator: "through what?"
- **Anchor:** Gym: motivation changes the effect of training (dial); training builds muscle that improves performance (bridge).
- **Check:** Standard in statistics and SLA research; the mediator is on the causal path, the moderator is not.

## D. Motivation, proficiency, learning

### 32. Dornyei's L2 Motivational Self System (2005, 2009)
- **Item:** Ideal L2 Self, Ought-to L2 Self, L2 Learning Experience.
- **Code:** A wall of three photos: "**dream me**" (ideal), "**parents' version of me**" (ought-to), "**this class right now**" (learning experience).
- **Simple:** We learn when we picture the person we want to become, feel pressure from others' expectations or enjoy the process.
- **Anchor:** Vision board, family expectations, and how the classroom feels.
- **Check:** Three components per Dornyei (2009; chapter in a book that was matched in the index). Built on Higgins' self-discrepancy and Markus and Nurius' possible selves.

### 33. Gardner & Lambert: integrative versus instrumental motivation (1959)
- **Item:** Integrative: wish to join the L2 community. Instrumental: practical gain.
- **Code:** "Join the club" versus "use the tool."
- **Simple:** Learning to belong versus learning to benefit.
- **Anchor:** Learning Spanish to move to Spain versus to get a promotion.
- **Check:** From Gardner and Lambert (1959; not found by tool; standard). Later work questions the dichotomy.

### 34. Self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000)
- **Item:** Autonomy, competence, relatedness.
- **Code:** A-C-R, "I choose, I can, we belong."
- **Simple:** Motivation grows when people feel in control, capable and connected.
- **Anchor:** A good video game co-op.
- **Check:** Three basic psychological needs per Ryan and Deci (2000, checked).

### 35. Cummins: BICS and CALP
- **Item:** Basic interpersonal communicative skills (about two years) and cognitive academic language proficiency (about five to seven years).
- **Code:** "Lunch talk versus lab report."
- **Simple:** Everyday talk comes sooner than the language of school texts.
- **Anchor:** Chatting in the canteen versus writing a thesis.
- **Check:** Cummins (1979; working paper). Time estimates are approximate and the distinction has been criticised.
