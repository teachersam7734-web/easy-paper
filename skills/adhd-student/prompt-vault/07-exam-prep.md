# 07. Exam Prep

How to use this file: start with 7.1 (plan), then alternate 7.2 and 7.3 (retrieval) every day, use 7.4 for spaced review, and add 7.6 to 7.8 (oral and written practice) in the last third of the time. Use 7.9 on the night before.

Evidence summary, so you can trust what the prompts ask for:
- Retrieval practice (testing yourself) beats re-reading: strong (Roediger & Karpicke 2006, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x; Dunlosky et al. 2013, https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266).
- Spacing sessions beats massing them: strong (Cepeda et al. 2006, https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). Gaps work best when they grow toward your exam date.
- Highlighting and re-reading are rated low utility (Dunlosky et al. 2013), so these prompts avoid them.
- Interleaving (mixing topics) has moderate support, mostly for problem-type content; use it for comparing theories, not for first learning.
- All-night cramming helps short-term recall at the cost of retention and sleep. If you must cram, 7.1 gives the safest version.
- AI can be wrong about authors, dates and statistics. Check every fact against your readings.

Tools: any chat AI; a notebook for red corrections; a timer. Not medical advice; if exam anxiety is severe, use your university's counselling or disability service.

### 7.1 Exam plan builder (cram-aware)

**Use when:** you have a date and a pile of readings.
**Time:** 10 minutes.

```
Build an exam plan. Exam: [COURSE, FORMAT: essay / short answer / MCQ / oral]. Date: [DATE]. Days available: [NUMBER] with about [HOURS] hours per day. Topics/readings: [LIST]. My confidence per topic (1 to 5): [LIST]. My energy pattern: [DESCRIBE].
Make a day-by-day table with: topic, activity (retrieval quiz, mind map from memory, teach the novice, past-paper question, oral practice), minutes, and the output I should have (e.g. "10 flashcards", "one 300-word answer"). Rules: weakest topics get the most sessions; every topic appears at least 3 times, with growing gaps; sleep and one rest block per day; the last day is light review only; no new material the day before. If I have fewer than 3 days, give me a triage plan: must-know, should-know, skip.
Ask me one question to confirm the plan before you finalise.
```

**Why it works:** Distributed, repeated retrieval is the core of the strong-evidence techniques; triage handles the real-life cram case. Estimating confidence first reduces over-studying familiar topics.
**Variations:** "Use my 12-minute sprints", "add a Pomodoro-free version: 45-minute blocks".

### 7.2 Retrieval drill from your notes (cold recall)

**Use when:** daily, 10 to 15 minutes, after you have studied a topic.
**Time:** 10 to 15 minutes.

```
Run a retrieval drill on [TOPIC]. I will paste my notes after you start. Do NOT show them again. Ask me ONE question at a time, in this order: (1) free recall "Write everything you remember about [TOPIC] for 2 minutes", then compare with my notes and list the gaps; (2) five short questions on those gaps; (3) two "why/how" questions; (4) one application question with a new example.
After each answer say Correct / Partly / Not yet, give the missing point in one line, and add it to a list called MISSED. At the end, show MISSED and tell me which one to re-study first.
Use only my notes: [PASTE NOTES]
```

**Why it works:** Free recall followed by feedback is the testing effect in action; targeting the gaps makes review efficient.
**Variations:** "Voice only: I will dictate my answers", for students who talk more easily than they write.

### 7.3 Definition and distinction cards (SLA key terms)

**Use when:** you need exact terms and contrasts (for example interaction hypothesis vs output hypothesis, implicit vs explicit knowledge).
**Time:** 10 minutes.

```
Make 12 flashcards from my list of terms: [LIST OF TERMS]. Each card: front = a question that makes me produce the definition AND one distinction (e.g. "What is X, and how does it differ from Y?"); back = a model answer in under 40 words with the key term in bold. Use ONLY the definitions I give you; if I give none, mark each card "needs source". Then quiz me on six cards, one at a time, in random order, and tell me which two I should repeat tomorrow.
```

**Why it works:** Contrast questions train discrimination between similar constructs (a moderate-strength interleaving idea) while still being retrieval. Grounding in your sources limits invented definitions.
**Variations:** "Reverse cards: give a classroom example and I name the concept".

### 7.4 Spaced review schedule

**Use when:** you have already learned the material once and want it to last.
**Time:** 5 minutes to set up, then 10 minutes per review.

```
Create a spaced review schedule for these topics: [LIST, with the date I first studied each]. My exam is on [DATE]. Use growing gaps, with the last review 1 to 2 days before the exam. For each topic give review dates and what to do each time: round 1 = recall from memory; round 2 = answer 3 questions; round 3 = teach it aloud for 2 minutes; round 4 = apply to a new case. Output a calendar-style table I can copy into my planner. Mark any date that collides with my commitments: [LIST]. Make it lightweight; maximum [3] topics per day.
```

**Why it works:** Optimal gaps grow with the retention interval (Cepeda et al. 2006); varying the review activity adds novelty.
**Variations:** Ask for an `.ics` text block or a checklist list; add "a catch-up day each week for missed reviews".

### 7.5 Essay question practice (plan, don't write)

**Use when:** your exam has essay questions such as "Critically evaluate the role of input in SLA".
**Time:** 20 minutes.

```
Give me a practice essay question on [TOPIC] in the style of an MA exam, based on the themes: [LIST]. Wait for me to write a 5-minute plan (thesis, 3 points, evidence, counter-argument, conclusion). Then give feedback in this order: (1) does my thesis answer the question? (2) are points ordered and linked? (3) is evidence named (author, year, study) and is anything uncertain? Mark uncertain items "verify". (4) one stronger counter-argument I missed. Do NOT write the essay. Then ask me to rewrite my plan in 3 minutes.
```

**Why it works:** Planning under time pressure and receiving feedback builds exam skill; the AI does not write the answer, so the learning stays with you. Retrieval and elaboration both occur.
**Variations:** "Timed version: 20-minute response in my notebook, then paste for marking against this rubric: [RUBRIC]".

### 7.6 Mock oral exam

**Use when:** your exam is oral, or you present in class and face questions.
**Time:** 15 to 20 minutes.

```
Run a mock oral exam on [COURSE/TOPIC]. You are a calm examiner. Ask 8 questions, one at a time, from easy to hard, mixing: definition, compare, evidence, application to teaching, critique. After each answer, give a mark 0 to 3 and a one-line comment (content, structure, terms). If I pause or say "I don't know", give a hint and ask me to try again. At the end give a profile: strengths, three weaknesses, and two phrases or sentence starters to use when I'm unsure (e.g. "The way I understand it...", "I'd need to check the exact study, but the main claim is..."). I will answer in [full sentences / short bullet-like answers]; accept speech-to-text errors.
```

**Why it works:** Oral practice rehearses retrieval under pressure and builds fluency in spoken academic language. Uncertainty phrases reduce freezing. Practising aloud, not just silently, matches the real task.
**Variations:** "Run it in [LANGUAGE]" if the exam is not in English; "Use the oral exam rubric for my programme".

### 7.7 Viva or thesis defence practice

**Use when:** a proposal defence, thesis defence or viva is coming.
**Time:** 30 minutes.

```
You are an external examiner at my [PROPOSAL / THESIS] defence in applied linguistics. My project: [TITLE, RQs, METHOD, SAMPLE, MAIN FINDINGS or PLANNED ANALYSIS: 150 words]. Ask 10 questions, one at a time, in rounds: (1) opening: "Summarise your study in 2 minutes"; (2) rationale and gap; (3) theory and key terms; (4) design, validity, reliability, ethics; (5) results or expected results and alternative explanations; (6) limitations and contribution; (7) a tough, fair question. After each answer, say what a strong answer would contain in one line, then give me a 0 to 3 mark. At the end list the "danger questions" where I scored lowest and suggest how to prepare. Do not invent literature; if you refer to a source, mark it "check".
```

**Why it works:** Repeated, realistic questioning is retrieval plus rehearsal; rounds mirror how defences move from big picture to detail. Danger-question lists focus later practice.
**Variations:** Ask for a hostile examiner or a friendly one; "add a 90-second elevator version".

### 7.8 Exam-day short answer drill (timed)

**Use when:** two to three days before a written exam with short answers.
**Time:** 20 minutes.

```
Give me 6 short-answer questions on [TOPICS], each answerable in 80 to 120 words. I will write each answer in my notebook with a 6-minute timer, then paste it. After each, show me: a model answer (under 100 words), what I left out, and one phrase to make my answer more precise. Do not move on until I paste my answer. Keep a count of how many key points I hit (e.g. 3/4). Use only definitions from my notes: [PASTE]. If the notes lack something, say "not in notes".
```

**Why it works:** Written retrieval under time pressure trains the exact skill assessed; comparing against a model answer gives immediate feedback.
**Variations:** "Mix in two MCQs with plausible distractors".

### 7.9 Night-before and morning-of calm plan

**Use when:** the evening before the exam, when anxiety or the urge to cram peaks.
**Time:** 10 minutes.

```
Help me plan the night before and the morning of my exam on [DATE/TIME]. Ask me one question at a time: what I still feel unsure about (pick at most 3); how much time I have tonight; when I need to sleep and wake. Then give me a plan with: a 45-minute light review of those 3 topics using recall questions (no new material); a bag/ID/travel checklist; a bedtime; a short morning routine (water, food, movement, 5-minute warm-up recall of key terms); a 2-line pep-talk in plain words; and an if-then plan for a blank mind ("If I blank, then I will write the key terms I remember and sketch a plan"). Keep it under 200 words. This is not medical advice; if panic is severe, tell me to contact a counsellor.
```

**Why it works:** Limiting scope and fixing sleep protects performance; an if-then plan for blanking uses implementation intentions (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006). Practice wisdom for the checklist and pep-talk.
**Variations:** "Add a 3-minute breathing exercise" (general relaxation technique, modest evidence for short-term calm).
