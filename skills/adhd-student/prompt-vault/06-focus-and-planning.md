# 06. Focus and Planning

How to use this file: pick by problem. Overwhelmed? 6.1 then 6.2. Cannot start? 6.4 then 6.5. Planning the week? 6.7 and 6.9. All prompts keep answers short and ask one question at a time, because long lists are hard to hold in working memory.

Evidence notes: if-then planning and breaking goals into concrete first actions have strong general support (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 meta-analysis); ADHD-specific trials are thinner (moderate). Short timed work blocks, Pomodoro and body doubling are widely used but I found no ADHD-specific trial in this research: label them practice wisdom. Free tools that do similar jobs: Goblin Tools (https://goblin.tools/, Magic ToDo, Estimator, Compiler), simple visual timers. This is study support, not medical advice; talk to a clinician or your university disability service about diagnosis and treatment.

---

### 6.1 Brain dump to actions

**Use when:** your head is full of tasks, worries and deadlines.
**Time:** 5 minutes.

```
I will paste a messy brain dump. Turn it into a clear list. Steps:
1. Sort items into: STUDY (reading, writing, exam), LIFE (errands, admin), WORRIES (no action possible now), and PARK (ideas for later).
2. For STUDY and LIFE, rewrite each item as a verb phrase I can finish in one sitting (e.g. "Read pp. 3 to 8 of Ortega and write 3 keywords").
3. Mark any item with a deadline and the date.
4. Ask me ONE question: which item is most urgent or most dreaded?
Do not add tasks I did not write. Brain dump: [PASTE]
```

**Why it works:** Offloading to an external list frees working memory; verb-phrase tasks are concrete. Pattern mirrors the "braindump to actions" idea in Goblin Tools' Compiler.
**Variations:** Add "also tell me which two items I could drop or postpone".

### 6.2 Task breakdown (magic to-do style)

**Use when:** a task feels huge ("write the critical commentary").
**Time:** 3 to 5 minutes.

```
Break this task into small steps: "[TASK]". My deadline: [DATE]. My typical focused stretch: [10 / 15 / 25] minutes.
Give me [5 to 8] steps. For each step write: the verb, the exact output (what exists when I'm done), and a time estimate in minutes. The FIRST step must take 5 minutes or less and be a physical action (open file X, write the title, list 3 keywords). Tell me which steps I could skip if I am short on time.
Detail level: [LOW = few big steps / MEDIUM / HIGH = tiny steps]. Do not do the task for me.
```

**Why it works:** Concrete, small, time-estimated steps lower the cost of starting. Tools such as Goblin Tools' Magic ToDo and free ADHD task-breakdown tools use the same pattern (five micro-steps with time estimates in Joshckh/adhd-free-tools, MIT).
**Variations:** "Break step 3 down further", or "Make the steps spicier/smaller" (the detail slider above).

### 6.3 Time boxing the day

**Use when:** you have a free day or half-day and want a schedule you can keep.
**Time:** 5 minutes.

```
Build a time-boxed plan for today. Available hours: [START to END]. Fixed commitments: [LIST]. Tasks (with estimated minutes): [LIST]. Energy pattern: best focus at [TIME OF DAY], low at [TIME].
Rules: blocks of [15 / 25 / 45] minutes; a 5 to 10 minute break after each block with no screens; a longer break every 3 to 4 blocks; put the hardest task in my best-focus window; add buffer (about 25 percent extra time because I tend to underestimate); leave one empty "overflow" block.
Output a table: time | task | output expected. Then ask me: "What could derail this plan, and what is your if-then response?"
```

**Why it works:** Time boxing makes time visible. Estimating and adding buffer address common time-estimation errors; an if-then response uses implementation intentions (strong evidence in general).
**Variations:** "Use the 12-minute reading sprint + 3-minute break rhythm from my study system".

### 6.4 Start when stuck (the stuck ladder)

**Use when:** you are staring at the task and cannot begin.
**Time:** 2 to 5 minutes.

```
I'm stuck and can't start: "[TASK]". Run the stuck ladder with me. Ask ONE question at a time and stop as soon as I'm moving:
Rung 1: What is the very first physical action (under 2 minutes)? Offer 3 options if I cannot think of one.
Rung 2: If I still can't, what is blocking me: unclear task / too big / boring / scared of doing it badly / tired? (I'll pick one.)
Rung 3: Give me a fix for that block (clarify the output / shrink to 5 minutes / add novelty / permit a "bad first draft" / suggest rest).
Rung 4: Make an if-then plan: "If it is [TIME/PLACE], then I will [ACTION]."
Then say "Go. Set a 5-minute timer. Tell me when it ends."
```

**Why it works:** Naming the type of block avoids generic advice; shrinking to a physical first action reduces initiation cost; if-then plans improve follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006).
**Variations:** After the timer, ask "continue 5 more minutes?" (yes/no only).

### 6.5 Two-minute ignition ritual (checklist)

**Use when:** the start of each unit or session; automates the "how do I begin" decision.
**Time:** 2 minutes.

```
Guide me through a 2-minute ignition ritual before I read [ARTICLE/CHAPTER]. One step at a time, wait for "done" each time:
1. Phone away or on airplane mode.
2. Write today's mission in one line.
3. Write the central question of the text in my own words.
4. List 3 things I hope to find.
5. Set the timer for [12] minutes and name the page range.
Then say "Sprint 1. Go." and stay silent until I return.
```

**Why it works:** A fixed ritual removes decisions and sets a goal before reading, as in the original system; pre-questions focus attention on relevant material.
**Variations:** Add a sentence: "Tonight's reward is [REWARD]".

### 6.6 Procrastination rescue

**Use when:** you have been avoiding something for days and feel guilty.
**Time:** 10 minutes.

```
I have been avoiding "[TASK]" for [DAYS]. Be kind and practical; no lectures. Ask me one question at a time:
1. What exactly am I afraid will happen if I start? (examples: it will be bad, I won't understand, I'll find out how much work it is)
2. What is the worst realistic outcome of working on it for 10 minutes?
3. What is a "good enough" version due today (not the perfect version)?
4. Who or what could make it easier (a friend, a template, an earlier draft)?
Then give me a 10-minute starter plan with one micro-step, a timer, and a reward for after. If I say I'm too tired, offer a "tiny win" instead and note when to try again.
```

**Why it works:** Avoidance is often emotional (fear, perfectionism, uncertainty). Naming the feeling and shrinking the demand targets that. Evidence: general self-regulation research; this prompt format is practice wisdom.
**Variations:** "Use a lower-stakes version: write 3 bad sentences".

### 6.7 Energy-based planning

**Use when:** your energy swings and a fixed schedule keeps failing.
**Time:** 5 minutes.

```
Plan my study around energy. Today my energy is [LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH] and my mood is [WORD]. Tasks I could do: [LIST WITH ESTIMATES].
Match tasks to energy:
- HIGH: demanding work (new theory, writing arguments).
- MEDIUM: summarising, concept maps, quiz practice.
- LOW: filing, formatting references, easy flashcards, a walk, or rest.
Pick a realistic plan for the next [HOURS] hours with at most [3] tasks. Tell me one task to drop if energy falls. Ask: "How will you know when to stop?"
```

**Why it works:** Matching task demand to current capacity reduces failed plans and shame. Evidence is practice wisdom; it follows the original system's advice to put the heaviest reading when energy is highest.
**Variations:** Add a 1 to 5 scale and ask the AI to re-plan at the midpoint.

### 6.8 Hyperfocus guard (stay on target, stop on time)

**Use when:** you get absorbed and miss meals, sleep or deadlines, or you go down a tangent.
**Time:** 3 minutes setup.

```
Set up a guard for a deep-work session. Task: [TASK]. Hard stop time: [TIME]. Tell me to write these now: the one thing I must finish, the tangents I'm allowed to explore later (PARKING LOT), and the time I must eat or move.
When I come back and write "check", ask me three things in one message: Are you still on the task? What time is it? Have you eaten or moved? Then tell me whether to continue or stop. At the stop time, tell me to save my work and write the next step in one line.
```

**Why it works:** Absorption can be productive, but needs boundaries; external prompts replace the weak internal time sense (time-blindness is commonly reported in ADHD, though evidence on its mechanism is mixed). The AI only responds when you write to it, so also use a real alarm.
**Variations:** Add "no new sources after [TIME]".

### 6.9 Weekly review

**Use when:** Sunday evening or Friday afternoon, 20 minutes.
**Time:** 20 minutes.

```
Run my weekly review. Ask one question at a time:
1. What did I finish this week? (list)
2. What did I plan but not finish, and why? (choose: too big / unclear / low energy / unexpected event / avoided)
3. Which study techniques worked best (quizzing, mind map, teaching, sprints)? Which didn't?
4. What are next week's fixed dates (classes, deadlines, exams)?
5. What are my top 3 outcomes for next week?
6. What is my plan for the first 30 minutes on Monday?
Then give me a one-page summary: wins, lessons, next week's 3 outcomes, a time-boxed week table, and one small reward. Keep the plan realistic: leave 20 percent of the week unscheduled.
```

**Why it works:** Reviewing outcomes with a "why" categorises problems so fixes are specific. Planning the first 30 minutes of Monday is an if-then plan. The unscheduled 20 percent allows for the planning fallacy.
**Variations:** "Add a spaced-review slot for last week's material" (see file 07).

### 6.10 Interest hook (make the boring reading interesting)

**Use when:** a required reading feels dull and your attention will not stay.
**Time:** 5 minutes.

```
I must read "[TITLE/TOPIC]" and I find it dull. My interests: [LIST, e.g. film, football, linguistics of memes, a language I am learning].
Give me 5 hooks, each one sentence, linking this topic to my interests. Then suggest 3 ways to make my reading active: a game (e.g. find 3 claims and bet which one is weakest), a role (e.g. a film critic reviewing this paper), or a creative output (a 6-panel comic, a tweet thread). Ask me which one to try. Do not summarise the reading for me.
```

**Why it works:** Interest and novelty can raise engagement; the original system's principle is to change activities every 10 to 15 minutes and add stakes. Evidence that personalised hooks improve comprehension is moderate and mixed; treat it as a way to lower resistance, not a guarantee.
**Variations:** "Hook it to my research proposal topic".

### 6.11 Reward and shutdown routine

**Use when:** you finish a session and tend to stop abruptly or overwork.
**Time:** 5 minutes.

```
Help me shut down. Ask me one question at a time:
1. What did I complete? (celebrate in one line)
2. What is the next step for tomorrow, written as a verb phrase?
3. Where is the file or page I need tomorrow?
4. What is my reward now, and when do I stop?
Then write a 3-line "tomorrow note" I can paste at the top of my notebook page: NEXT STEP / FILE / FIRST 5 MINUTES. Tell me to close the laptop.
```

**Why it works:** Ending with the next step written down reduces the restart cost. A planned reward after work ("reward after, not during") is a principle in the original system; evidence that immediate rewards help people with ADHD is moderate in the wider literature, but I did not cite a specific study here.
**Variations:** Add "also schedule my first spaced-review question for tomorrow".
