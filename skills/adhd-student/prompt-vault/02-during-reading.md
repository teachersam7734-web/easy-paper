# 02 During reading

How to use this file
- These prompts are short on purpose. In a 12-minute sprint you should spend most time reading, not chatting. Rule of thumb: one AI exchange per sprint at most, and only if you are stuck or at the buzzer.
- Active output beats passive reading: write or draw something every sprint. The AI checks; it does not read for you.
- Evidence note: retrieval attempts (such as the cluster in prompt 2) and self-explanation are among the better-supported study techniques (high and moderate utility in the Dunlosky et al. 2013 review); re-reading and highlighting are rated low utility. Chunking and short replies respond to the working-memory difficulties documented in ADHD.
- Same rules as file 01: one question at a time, printed page numbers, OUTSIDE THE PDF label.

## 1. Sprint launcher

**Use when:** at the start of every sprint.
**Time:** 1 minute.

```
Sprint [NUMBER] of [TOTAL]. Remind me, in under 60 words:
- the hunt question for this sprint: [HUNT QUESTION, or "from the plan"]
- the page range: [PAGES]
- what to SKIM and what to SKIP in this range
- the one READING FOCUS line to hold in my head
Then say "Start the timer" and wait for me to write BACK when the sprint is over.
Do not ask anything else until then.
```

**Why it works:** re-externalising the goal at the start of each sprint keeps it in front of you, since working memory is a documented weak point. Silence during the sprint prevents the chat from becoming a distraction.
**Variations:** add "also tell me the sprint's end time if I start at [CLOCK TIME]" to help with time blindness; add "end with one sentence of encouragement, no more" if you like a nudge.

## 2. Sprint close: cluster check

**Use when:** at the buzzer. First close the PDF and write a keyword cluster from memory, then paste it.
**Time:** 3 minutes.

```
Sprint [NUMBER] check. I closed the PDF and wrote this keyword cluster from memory, with page
anchors where I remember them:

[PASTE CLUSTER]

Compare with the PDF pages [PAGES] and report in this format, under 120 words:
- Solid: what I got right
- Shaky: right idea but wrong detail (give the detail and the page)
- Blank: an important idea I left out (give the keyword and the page)
- Wrong: anything I stated incorrectly (give the correction and the page)
Then ask me ONE question about the single most important blank. Wait for my answer.
```

**Why it works:** a closed-book retrieval attempt after each short block beats immediate re-reading for later memory (strong evidence for the testing effect), and short checks give immediate feedback, which suits the sensitivity to delayed reward described in ADHD.
**Variations:** ask the AI to rate my cluster out of 5 for the scoreboard; ask it to say which hunt question my cluster supports; if I wrote under four items, ask it to tell me the three most important missing keywords only.

## 3. Rescue v2

**Use when:** you are stuck on a passage and cannot continue.
**Time:** 2 minutes (then return to the page).

```
I am stuck on this passage, page [PAGE]: [PASTE THE PASSAGE, OR DESCRIBE WHERE IT STARTS AND ENDS].
Do this, in under 120 words:
1. Say what the passage is trying to do (define, argue, give evidence, qualify) in ONE sentence.
2. Explain it in 3 simple sentences.
3. Give one concrete example from language learning or teaching.
4. Ask me ONE question to check that I understood.
Do not explain anything beyond this passage. Label anything not in the PDF as OUTSIDE THE PDF.
```

**Why it works:** limiting scope and ending with a check question stops a rescue from turning into a tangent, and the "what is this doing" line models how experts read.
**Variations:** add "in Persian first, then the English term" for a bilingual explanation; add "give me two different explanations" if the first did not help; add "keep my page position: tell me the next sentence to read."

## 4. Dense sentence untangler

**Use when:** one long academic sentence will not parse.
**Time:** 2 minutes.

```
Untangle this sentence from page [PAGE]:
"[PASTE THE SENTENCE]"
1. Split it into its main claim and its qualifiers as a short list (subject, verb, object; then
   each embedded clause with what it adds).
2. Rewrite it as 2 or 3 short sentences without changing the meaning.
3. Mark any hedges (may, suggests, tends to) and any nominalisations that hide who did what.
4. Ask me to say it back in one sentence of my own.
```

**Why it works:** breaking syntax into small parts reduces the number of items held in working memory at once; restating it yourself turns decoding into understanding.
**Variations:** ask for a "plain English version for a first-year student"; for a non-native reading of English, add "list the grammar structures used and one-line reasons."

## 5. Jargon buster

**Use when:** a term is unfamiliar or used in a specialised way.
**Time:** 2 minutes.

```
Explain the term "[TERM]" as used on page [PAGE] of this PDF. Give:
1. How THIS text defines or uses it (quote a short phrase and the page).
2. The plain meaning in one sentence.
3. How it differs from the closest everyday meaning, and from one related SLA term. Label this
   OUTSIDE THE PDF if the PDF does not say so.
4. An example in a language classroom.
5. One question for me, to confirm. Keep it under 100 words.
```

**Why it works:** contrasting the technical and everyday meanings prevents the common misreading of terms such as "input", "output", "interaction", "form", "task" or "transfer", and a confirm question stops you moving on with a fuzzy term.
**Variations:** for a cluster of related terms (for example input, intake, uptake) ask for a four-line comparison table; ask to add the term to a running glossary with the page.

## 6. Chunk explainer ladder

**Use when:** a paragraph or short section is too hard, and a simple rescue is not enough.
**Time:** 3 minutes.

```
Explain pages [PAGES] / the section "[HEADING]" in three levels. Give ONE level at a time and wait
for me to write NEXT between levels:
Level 1: the gist in one sentence.
Level 2: the argument in 3 to 4 sentences (claim, reason, example).
Level 3: the detail in 5 to 6 sentences, with the key terms and page numbers.
After Level 2, ask me to explain it back in one sentence before you show Level 3. Do not rewrite
the full text. Label anything outside the PDF as OUTSIDE THE PDF.
```

**Why it works:** a layered explanation offers just enough information at each step (cognitive load theory favours small steps), and the teach-back at Level 2 is a self-explanation that strengthens understanding.
**Variations:** stop at Level 2 if you are just checking; ask the AI to use my own earlier words from the chat to build on what I already said.

## 7. Figure and table decoder

**Use when:** a figure or table slows you down, and the main text does not make it clear.
**Time:** 3 minutes.

```
Help me read [FIGURE / TABLE NUMBER] on page [PAGE]. Do not interpret it for me first. Ask me,
ONE at a time:
1. What is on each axis (or what do rows and columns show)? Wait.
2. What is the main pattern you can see? Wait.
Then confirm or correct me. After that, give me, in under 120 words:
- The one-sentence message of the figure or table, in the authors' terms
- What it does NOT show (for example no information on learning gains, a small sample, only
  averages)
- Which hunt question it answers
- One thing the authors say about it in the text, with the page
```

**Why it works:** looking first and describing the pattern yourself is a retrieval and generation step; the "what it does not show" line builds the evidence-critique habit expected in critical commentary tasks.
**Variations:** for a table of studies in a review, ask for "a sorted version by sample size or design"; for a path diagram or model, ask "which arrow is the authors' main claim?"

## 8. Statistics in plain words

**Use when:** results use numbers you cannot read quickly (p values, effect sizes, confidence intervals, regression or mixed-effects output).
**Time:** 3 minutes.

```
Translate these results from page [PAGE] into plain words:
"[PASTE THE RESULTS SENTENCE OR TABLE ROW]"
1. What was compared or predicted?
2. What do the numbers say, in one sentence? (Explain any term such as p, d, r, R-squared, beta,
   CI, SE, odds ratio.)
3. How big is the effect in practice? Use the authors' own interpretation if given; if not, label
   your reading as OUTSIDE THE PDF and say it is a rough rule of thumb.
4. One thing this result cannot tell us (for example cause, generalisation, learning over time).
5. A one-question check for me.
Do not run new analyses and do not recalculate; explain only what is printed.
```

**Why it works:** most MA students need numbers translated into claims, not recomputed; the "cannot tell us" line keeps conclusions proportionate to the evidence.
**Variations:** ask for "what would the numbers look like if there were no effect?" to explain significance; ask for a "traffic light" (strong, medium, weak) with reasons and the label "my opinion".

## 9. Example and data extract decoder

**Use when:** the text gives learner language, transcripts, classroom extracts or coding examples.
**Time:** 3 minutes.

```
Look at this example on page [PAGE]: "[PASTE EXTRACT]".
1. Ask me first: what is the author using this example to show? Wait for my answer.
2. Then tell me what the author says it shows (with the page) and whether the example fits the
   claim, in two sentences.
3. Point out any feature a careful reader would notice (for example a single learner, a
   self-selected example, a transcript convention). Label it OUTSIDE THE PDF if it is not in the
   text.
4. Give me one short question to apply the same lens to a different example.
```

**Why it works:** examples are persuasive but often selective; asking for your interpretation first builds analytic skill, and the brief check keeps the sprint moving.
**Variations:** for conversation-analysis style transcripts add "decode the transcription symbols used"; for error analysis add "classify each error type, labelled as my suggestion."

## 10. Who said what

**Use when:** a section is dense with citations (literature review, theory background).
**Time:** 3 minutes.

```
For pages [PAGES], build a small table of who argued what, from the PDF only:
Author and year | Claim or finding | Type (theory, empirical, review) | Page
Limit to the 6 most important sources for the hunt question: [HUNT QUESTION].
Then ask me ONE question: which two of these sources disagree, or which one does the author rely
on most? Do not add outside sources. If the text gives no detail about a source, write "not
described here" in that cell.
```

**Why it works:** literature reviews overload working memory with names and years; a small table stores that load outside your head. Limiting to the sources that matter for the hunt question mirrors skimming on purpose.
**Variations:** ask for "who is cited for what, and which citations are secondary" (to find the originals); ask for a timeline of the argument.

## 11. Claim, evidence, hedge spotter

**Use when:** a discussion or conclusion section makes strong claims, or when you prepare a critical commentary.
**Time:** 4 minutes.

```
For pages [PAGES], list the 5 most important claims the authors make. For each give:
Claim (short quote or paraphrase) | Page | Type: STATED BY THE AUTHORS DIRECTLY, REASONABLE
INFERENCE, or SPECULATION | Evidence offered | Hedges used (may, suggests, tends to) | Strength: strong,
partial, or weak, with a one-line reason.
Base this only on the PDF. Then ask me ONE question: which claim would I challenge first, and
why?
```

**Why it works:** typing claims separates what was found from what was inferred, a core skill in critical reading, and the single challenge question moves you from reading to evaluating.
**Variations:** add "for each weak claim, suggest one study design that would test it, labelled OUTSIDE THE PDF"; compare the abstract's claims to the discussion's and list any drift.

## 12. Micro-check ping

**Use when:** your attention drifts mid-sprint, or you suspect you have read without absorbing.
**Time:** 1 minute.

```
Micro-check on pages [PAGES]. Ask me ONE question that I can answer in one sentence without looking
at the PDF, about the single most important idea on these pages. Wait. Then say solid, shaky, or
blank, give the correct answer in one sentence with the page, and say "Back to the page".
Do nothing else.
```

**Why it works:** a single retrieval attempt reorients attention and reveals whether you are truly processing the text; the instant feedback fits the preference for immediate reward over delayed reward documented in ADHD.
**Variations:** ask for a question every 4 pages automatically; ask for a "true or false" version if energy is very low.

## 13. Parking lot triage

**Use when:** at a break, you have a Parking Lot full of stray thoughts and tangents.
**Time:** 2 minutes.

```
Here is my Parking Lot from this sprint: [PASTE ITEMS].
Sort each item into one of four bins, with one line of advice each:
- ERRAND (do later, not now)
- TANGENT (a topic I may want to read about; add to a "later reading" list)
- IDEA FOR MY ASSIGNMENT (keep, move to my notes)
- WORRY (a feeling, not a task; suggest a 1-minute action or "let it go")
Do not expand any item. Finish by telling me to go back to the break (stand, drink water).
```

**Why it works:** capturing intrusive thoughts in a trusted place reduces the pull to act on them immediately; sorting them at the break keeps the sprint clean. It is practice wisdom, not a heavily tested technique.
**Variations:** for lots of ideas, ask for "the single idea worth 10 minutes of my day"; skip the AI and sort by hand when you want fewer screen minutes.

## 14. Break note and energy check

**Use when:** at every break, before standing up.
**Time:** 1 minute.

```
Break time. I write: "The next sentence I would read starts with: [WORDS]." Energy now (1 to 5):
[NUMBER].
Reply in under 40 words: confirm my restart point, and based on my energy advise ONE thing for
this break (water, food, walk, stretch, stand in daylight). If my energy is 1 or 2 after two
sprints, offer the bad-day plan: finish this unit's blind recall and stop. Say "Back in 3 minutes".
```

**Why it works:** stopping mid-thought and noting the restart point lowers the cost of returning; a quick energy check helps decide whether to continue, and physical movement and rest support return to focus (practice wisdom with general support).
**Variations:** add "do not use a screen during the break"; log energy ratings across the day so you can find your best hours.

## 15. Re-entry after derailing

**Use when:** you lost 10 minutes or more to a tangent, a notification or a mood dip.
**Time:** 2 minutes.

```
I derailed. Do not comment on it. Help me restart in under two minutes:
1. Ask me: what was the last thing I remember reading? (page or idea)
2. Give me a one-sentence recap of where the plan said I should be, using the sprint plan.
3. Tell me the next sprint (not the whole unit): the page range, the hunt question, and how long.
4. Remind me: restart from the next sprint, not the beginning. Then say "Start the timer".
```

**Why it works:** a derail often triggers shame and an all-or-nothing response ("the day is lost"); a short non-judgemental restart protocol is consistent with self-compassion approaches and the bad-day plan.
**Variations:** add "reduce the plan to what is essential" if you are more than 30 minutes behind; add a 5-minute body-double suggestion (study next to someone, or on a silent call).
