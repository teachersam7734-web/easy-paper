# The gold-nugget method

A gold nugget is an idea from the paper that (a) the authors would agree is central, (b) a professor would expect a student to know, and (c) fits in one sentence a tired reader can repeat. Most papers have 5 to 9. Easy Paper finds them first and builds everything else around them.

## Step 1. Find the message (what the authors are trying to say)

Write it at three zoom levels, in your own words:
- **1 sentence:** the thesis. "The authors argue that X, because Y."
- **3 sentences:** the problem, the move, and the payoff.
- **10 sentences:** one per major part of the paper, in the paper's order.

Where the message hides: the last paragraph of the introduction, the purpose statement ("the purpose of", "we argue", "we propose", "this paper examines"), the first paragraph of the conclusion, the abstract, and the figures or tables the authors chose to build. Authors repeat what they care about, so count repetitions.

**The message test:** could the authors read your 1-sentence version and nod? If they would say "that's not quite what we meant", rewrite it.

## Step 2. Score candidate items (the 80/20 filter)

List every candidate idea: constructs, findings, numbers, steps, distinctions, claims, critiques, people. Score each one 1 to 3 on four things:

| Criterion | 3 means |
|---|---|
| **Centrality** | The argument collapses without it |
| **Testability** | A professor could easily turn it into an exam or viva question (definitions, "the three types", "the main finding", "the key limitation") |
| **Transferability** | It connects to other papers or SLA theories, and it will come up again |
| **Confusability** | Students often mix it up with something else, so it needs a contrast |

Total = sum (4 to 12). Items scoring **9 or more** are nuggets. Items scoring 7 to 8 are chunk content. Items scoring 6 or less are cut, or kept as one line at most.

## Step 3. Phrase the nugget

- One sentence, 8 to 25 words, concrete subject, active verb.
- Lead with the idea, not the citation: "Mixed methods is defined by *combining* qual and quan elements for breadth and depth [p. 123]", not "Johnson et al. (2007) state on p. 123 that...".
- Include the number when there is one (19 definitions, 36 leaders, 5 themes).
- One anchor per nugget.

## Step 4. Decide verbatim or paraphrase

Quote **verbatim** (with a printed page, in a `definition` block) only when:
1. It is a **definition** the field reuses, or that the paper offers as its own definition.
2. It is a **named construct or framework** whose exact labels matter (the five purposes, the four triangulations, the three positions).
3. It is the **thesis sentence**, and quoting it lets the reader cite the authors in class.

Everything else is paraphrased as the authors' message. Never quote just because the original sounds good. Each verbatim quote is followed by an "In plain words" line. Every verbatim quote must pass `scripts/check_quotes.py`.

## Step 5. Attach a memory hook

For each nugget, add one: a contrast ("not X, but Y"), a number, an image or metaphor, an acronym the paper itself uses (TCDIE for Greene et al.'s five purposes), or a classroom example. Hooks go in the chunk, in the Review Guide's memory-hook cards, or in the say-it lines of the Critique.
