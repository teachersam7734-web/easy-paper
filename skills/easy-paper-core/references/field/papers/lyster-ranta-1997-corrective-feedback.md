# Corrective feedback and learner uptake (Lyster & Ranta, 1997)

**One-line message:** In communicative immersion classrooms teachers mostly recast errors, but recasts rarely lead students to repair them, while prompts that push learners to produce the form work better.

## Gold nuggets (3-5)
- **Six feedback types:** recast, explicit correction, clarification request, metalinguistic feedback, elicitation, repetition. This taxonomy is still the standard reference list.
- **Headline numbers:** about 18.3 hours of classroom data, 3,268 student turns, 686 error turns that got feedback. **Recasts were 55% of all feedback but led to repair only 18% of the time** (69% of recasts got no uptake at all).
- **Elicitation led to uptake every time (0% no uptake) and 46% repair.** Metalinguistic feedback gave 45% repair. These "prompts" let students self-repair.
- **Uptake** = the student's utterance that immediately follows teacher feedback and reacts to the correction. **Repair** = correct reformulation of the error (a subset of uptake).
- **Negotiation of form** (feedback that pushes learners to self-correct) is distinct from negotiation of meaning (Long). The authors argue immersion students get a lot of meaning negotiation but too little push on form.

## The problem it answered (context in that year)
- Communicative and immersion teaching assumed grammar would be absorbed through meaning-focused use. But immersion research (Swain, 1985; Harley & Swain) showed students develop strong comprehension and fluency yet persistent grammatical errors in production (French gender, verb forms, etc.).
- Earlier classroom feedback research (Chaudron, 1977, descriptive model of teacher feedback; Allwright; Fanselow) described how teachers react to errors, but did not show what students do next.
- Interactionist theory (Long, 1996 on negotiation and negative feedback; Schmidt, 1990 on noticing; Swain on output) predicted that feedback matters, but classroom data on what feedback does were scarce.
- Open question: which feedback types lead to learner uptake and repair in real communicative lessons?

## The argument / design in plain language
**Design:** descriptive, observational classroom study (corpus of teacher-student interaction), part of a larger project.

**Setting and participants (verified from the paper's text as retrieved):**
- Four primary-level French immersion classrooms in the Montreal area, from two school boards.
- Four Grade 4 teachers (one class was split Grade 4/5). Grade 6 classes were excluded from this analysis.
- The larger project had about 100 hours of audio from six classrooms; this analysis used **27 lessons totaling 1,100 minutes (18.3 hours)**: 13 French language arts lessons (7.8 hours) and 14 subject-matter lessons (10.5 hours).

**Coding:** All errors (and unsolicited L1 use or need for repair) were identified, then teacher feedback moves, then student uptake in the following turn. Feedback types were defined as below (paraphrased definitions).

**Counts (verified from the paper's text as retrieved):**
| Measure | Number |
|---|---|
| Student turns | 3,268 |
| Turns with an error, unsolicited L1 use, or need for repair | 1,104 (34%) |
| Error turns that received feedback | 686 (62%) |
| Feedback turns followed by uptake | 377 (55%) |
| Feedback turns followed by repair | 184 (27%) |
| All errors that led to repair | about 17% |

**Distribution of the 686 feedback turns:**
| Feedback type | Share |
|---|---|
| Recast | 55% |
| Elicitation | 14% |
| Clarification request | 11% |
| Metalinguistic feedback | 8% |
| Explicit correction | 7% |
| Repetition | 5% |

**Uptake outcomes by feedback type (repair / needs repair / no uptake):**
| Feedback type | Repair | Needs repair | No uptake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recast | 18% | 13% | 69% |
| Elicitation | 46% | 54% | 0% |
| Clarification request | 28% | 60% | 12% |
| Metalinguistic feedback | 45% | 41% | 14% |
| Explicit correction | 36% | 14% | 50% |
| Repetition | 31% | 47% | 22% |

**Student-generated repair (peer or self-repair, i.e., not just repeating the teacher's form):** elicitation 46%, metalinguistic feedback 45%, repetition 31%, clarification request 27%-28%, recast and explicit correction 0%. (Exact figures for clarification request differ by 1 point in the sources retrieved: 27% vs 28%.)

**Main conclusion (paraphrase):** the feedback type teachers use most (recasts) is the least likely to produce uptake and repair. Feedback types that withhold the correct form and push learners to self-correct (elicitation, metalinguistic feedback, clarification request, repetition) are labeled **negotiation of form** and produce student-generated repair.

**Why recasts fail (their argument):** recasts are ambiguous in a communicative classroom. Students may hear the teacher's reformulation as an alternative way of saying the same thing, or as confirmation or approval of content, not as correction. Teachers also use recasts to repeat or elaborate on correct utterances, so students cannot tell correction from rephrasing.

## Why it matters / impact (what it changed, citation influence)
- The single most cited paper on classroom corrective feedback. Its six-type taxonomy is used to code feedback in hundreds of studies.
- Sparked the **recasts vs prompts** debate that drives much of the corrective-feedback literature.
- Linked classroom data to theory: noticing (Schmidt), interaction (Long), output (Swain).
- Made **uptake** a standard measure, even though it is now treated as an indirect indicator of learning (see critiques).
- Influenced teacher education: shows that a teacher's common habit (recasting) may be less effective for prompting repair than other techniques.
- Led to follow-up classroom experiments in immersion and communicative contexts (Lyster, 2004; Ammar & Spada, 2006) and to meta-analyses.

## Known limitations and critiques (who, what)
- **Uptake is not learning:** Uptake and repair are immediate responses. They do not show durable acquisition (Mackey & Philp, 1998, found recasts helped development despite low immediate uptake; Long, 2007, and others argue recasts work through implicit processes).
- **Methodological:** descriptive, no pre/post-tests, no control, no measure of learning. Authors themselves frame it as describing what happens.
- **Context specific:** child immersion classes in Montreal, French, teacher-fronted interaction. Results may not transfer to adults, other languages, or one-to-one settings (Sheen, 2004, compared classroom settings and found different recast-uptake patterns).
- **Recast definition and variety:** Recasts vary (length, number of changes, declarative vs interrogative); Sheen (2006) and Loewen & Philp (2006) show that some recasts are more noticeable than others.
- **Confounds with error type and teacher style:** Lyster (1998a, 1998b) shows recasts are used more often for phonological and grammatical errors, while lexical errors get prompts; uptake rates depend on error type.
- **Ambiguity claim vs learners' perception:** Mackey, Gass & McDonough (2000) found learners perceive different feedback accurately depending on error type (morphosyntax recasts less accurately perceived).
- **Uptake as a measure of teachers' push:** the taxonomy focuses on teacher moves and might undervalue learner agency and sociocultural dimensions of feedback (see Aljaafreh & Lantolf, 1994).
- **Publication detail to note:** one retrieved copy showed the volume as 20 in a header; the correct reference is Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 19(1), 37-66 (1997).

## What came after (follow-up studies, revisions, meta-analyses)
- Lyster (1998a), SSLA, 20(1), 51-81: recasts, repetition and ambiguity in L2 classroom discourse. Lyster (1998b), Language Learning, 48(2), 183-218: negotiation of form, recasts and explicit correction by error type.
- Lyster & Mori (2006), SSLA, 28(2), 269-300: interactional feedback and instructional counterbalance (feedback that contrasts with the instructional orientation of the classroom).
- Ammar & Spada (2006), SSLA, 28(4), 543-574: prompts vs recasts and proficiency level in French-speaking learners of English.
- Li (2010), Language Learning, 60(2), 309-365 (meta-analysis of corrective feedback); Lyster & Saito (2010), SSLA, 32(2), 265-302 (meta-analysis of classroom oral CF: positive effect overall, prompts ahead of recasts); Brown (2016) and Kang & Han (2015) on written CF.
- Lyster, Saito & Sato (2013), Language Teaching, 46(1), 1-40: review of oral corrective feedback in classrooms. Lyster & Ranta (2013): counterpoint on variety in CF research.
- Mackey & Philp (1998) and Mackey (1999) show recasts can promote development in interaction even with limited immediate uptake.
- Nassaji (2015), The interactional feedback dimension in instructed SLA; Ellis (2009) typology of written CF.
- Ranta & Lyster (2007): awareness-practice-feedback sequence for immersion.

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> connection / critique angle)
- Paper codes teacher feedback -> check whether it uses the Lyster & Ranta six types and how it handles overlapping categories.
- Paper reports "uptake" or "repair" rates -> cite this paper for definitions; critique the validity of uptake as evidence of learning.
- Paper compares recasts to prompts -> classify under reformulations vs prompts; mention the ambiguity argument.
- Paper in immersion/CLIL -> the original context; ask whether the findings generalize.
- Paper on teacher beliefs or error treatment -> compare reported practices to this observed profile (55% recasts).
- Paper claiming recasts are ineffective -> counter with Mackey & Philp (1998), Long (2007): low uptake does not equal no learning; check outcome measures.
- Paper on peer feedback or online feedback -> note that the taxonomy was built for teacher-fronted face-to-face talk.

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8 with short model answers)
1. **Name the six feedback types.** Recast, explicit correction, clarification request, metalinguistic feedback, elicitation, repetition.
2. **Which type was most frequent and which was most effective for repair?** Recasts were most frequent (55%) but produced repair only 18% of the time. Elicitation (46% repair, no cases without uptake) and metalinguistic feedback (45%) were most effective for student-generated repair.
3. **Define uptake and repair.** Uptake: student utterance immediately after feedback that reacts to it. Repair: correct reformulation of the error in a single student turn.
4. **What is negotiation of form?** Feedback sequences in which the teacher withholds the answer and pushes the learner to self-correct (elicitation, metalinguistic feedback, clarification request, repetition).
5. **Why might recasts be ambiguous in the communicative classroom?** Students may interpret a recast as content confirmation or rephrasing, not as correction, since teachers also reformulate correct utterances.
6. **What are two limitations of the study?** Descriptive design with no learning measure (uptake is not acquisition); limited context (child French immersion, teacher-fronted).
7. **How do later studies qualify the findings?** Meta-analyses (Li, 2010; Lyster & Saito, 2010) find CF effective overall; prompts often outperform recasts in classrooms, but recasts can also support development (Mackey & Philp, 1998), and effects depend on context, error type and learner factors.
8. **How does the paper connect to Long (1996) and Schmidt (1990)?** It tests, in classrooms, whether feedback gets noticed and leads to learner modification, which interaction and noticing theories predict.
