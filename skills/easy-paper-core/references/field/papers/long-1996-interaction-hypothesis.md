# Updated Interaction Hypothesis (Long, 1996)

**One-line message:** Learning from conversation works mainly because negotiation for meaning (and the negative feedback it supplies) links input, the learner's attention and output at the moment the learner needs it.

## Gold nuggets (3-5)
- This is the **revised** interaction hypothesis. The 1981/1983 version said: conversational modification makes input comprehensible, and comprehensible input drives acquisition. The 1996 version adds **negative feedback**, **selective attention** and **output**.
- Verified key sentence (Long, 1996, p. 414, as quoted in a secondary review): "environmental contributions to acquisition are mediated by selective attention" and resources are brought together "most usefully, although not exclusively, during negotiation for meaning".
- Also verified there: "Negative feedback obtained during negotiation work or elsewhere may be facilitative of L2 development" (Long, 1996, p. 414). Note "or elsewhere": feedback outside negotiation counts too.
- Long himself said the hypothesis is not "anything like a complete theory of language learning" (as reported in the secondary review). It targets the role of the environment, not the whole of SLA.
- Key distinction: **positive evidence** (what is possible in the L2: models, input) vs **negative evidence** (what is not possible: corrections, feedback). Negative evidence can be **pre-emptive** or **reactive** and **explicit** or **implicit**; recasts are a prime implicit form.

## The problem it answered (context in that year)
- 1980s debate: Krashen's Input Hypothesis (comprehensible input is the sole causal variable) vs. the idea that interaction and output matter too. Long (1981, 1983) had argued modified interaction aids comprehension; Swain (1985) argued comprehensible output is also needed after seeing learners in French immersion with high comprehension but weaker production.
- Linguistic theory (Chomsky-inspired, Universal Grammar) claimed that negative evidence is unavailable or unnecessary in L1 and probably ineffective in L2, based on the "poverty of the stimulus". Applied researchers asked: do L2 learners get and use negative evidence?
- Cognitive psychology (Schmidt, 1990) put attention and noticing at the center of learning.
- Task-based research on native/non-native speaker conversation (Long, 1983; Pica, Young & Doughty, 1987; Gass & Varonis, 1985) had documented negotiation moves but with an incomplete theory of why they help acquisition.
- The 1996 chapter sits in the Handbook of Second Language Acquisition (Ritchie & Bhatia, Eds.), a major reference work, and so reviews and revises the field's evidence about the "linguistic environment".

## The argument / design in plain language
**Type:** a long theoretical and review chapter (pp. 413-468); no new experiment. Long reviews the evidence for the role of input, interaction and feedback and refines the hypothesis.

**Core claim (paraphrase):** Negotiation work that triggers interactional adjustments by a native speaker or more competent interlocutor facilitates acquisition because it connects input, internal learner capacities (especially selective attention) and output in productive ways. This is a standard summary of Long's proposal; the exact wording used in the chapter is not verified here, but the p. 414 quotes above are.

**Mechanism in steps (plain language):**
1. A conversation partner does not understand, or the learner does not understand.
2. They negotiate: clarification requests, confirmation checks, comprehension checks, recasts, repetitions.
3. The input is adjusted and made comprehensible in context, and the learner's attention is drawn to a mismatch (a "gap") between their form and the target form.
4. Because this happens in the middle of a meaningful exchange, the learner has the processing capacity and motivation to notice and may also produce modified output.
5. Result: the form is more likely to be noticed and later learned.

**Evidence types Long reviews (paraphrase):**
- Positive evidence is always available in input; negative evidence is episodic.
- Studies of L1 acquisition suggest children get some negative feedback, including recasts.
- L2 studies show learners receive corrective feedback in conversation and some can use it.
- Because negative evidence is available only in interaction, interaction offers learners something input alone cannot.

**Role of the learner's internal state:** Developmental readiness matters (consistent with the Teachability Hypothesis of Pienemann, 1984/1989 and with L1 influence); feedback is useful when the learner is ready and the form is within reach.

## Why it matters / impact (what it changed, citation influence)
- Cornerstone of **interactionist SLA**. It integrated input, interaction, attention and output into one account, reducing reliance on Krashen's input-only view.
- Launched a large body of **interaction research** (Mackey, Gass and colleagues): negotiation, feedback, recasts, task design, and individual differences.
- Provided the main rationale for **corrective feedback** research and for **recasts** as a research focus (Lyster & Ranta, 1997; Mackey & Philp, 1998; Mackey, 1999).
- Underpins **task-based language teaching** (Long, 1985, 2015): tasks that need negotiation, and **focus on form** (Long, 1991), where attention is drawn to form during meaning-focused activity.
- Summarized and extended in Gass & Mackey (2007) and Mackey (2012); the hypothesis is a standard chapter in SLA textbooks.

### Mini example (my illustration, not from the chapter)
- Learner: "He go to school yesterday."
- Teacher: "He went to school yesterday? Oh, which school?" (recast inside a meaningful exchange)
- Learner: "Yes, he went to the new school."
- What the hypothesis says: the learner's attention was briefly drawn to the mismatch while the focus stayed on meaning, which is when feedback is most usable.
- Caution: the recast could also be heard as a simple confirmation, which is the ambiguity Lyster & Ranta (1997) emphasize.

### Interaction Hypothesis timeline (one-glance)
- 1981: modified interaction aids comprehension (Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, 379).
- 1983: negotiation of comprehensible input in NS/NNS conversation (Applied Linguistics, 4(2)).
- 1985, 1991: tasks and focus on form added to the research agenda.
- 1996: negative feedback, selective attention and output integrated.
- 2015: restated within task-based language teaching.

## Known limitations and critiques (who, what)
- **Not a complete theory** (Long's own caution). It says little about what is acquired or how knowledge is represented.
- **Causality:** much early work showed interaction produces comprehension, not acquisition. Showing learning gains took longer (Mackey, 1999, for question formation).
- **Negative evidence effectiveness:** Some argue learners often fail to notice recasts as correction (Lyster, 1998; Lyster & Ranta, 1997), and that feedback effects depend on type and context.
- **Generalizability from lab to classroom:** Most studies used dyadic NS-NNS or NNS-NNS lab tasks. Classroom power relations and turn-taking differ (Foster & Ohta, 2005, on peer negotiation being less frequent than expected).
- **Sociocultural critique:** Interaction is treated as a delivery channel for input and feedback; Vygotskian and CA scholars see interaction as the site of learning itself (Aljaafreh & Lantolf, 1994; Firth & Wagner, 1997).
- **Underspecified mechanisms:** "attention" and "noticing" are hard to measure; critics (Truscott, 1998, on noticing; Gass & Selinker on priming) question the causal link.
- **Quality of the research:** Plonsky & Gass (2011) reviewed the methodology of interaction research and found problems (small samples, inconsistent reporting) while noting improvement over time (as recalled).
- **Learner readiness:** Benefits may depend on proficiency, working memory and developmental stage.

## What came after (follow-up studies, revisions, meta-analyses)
- Mackey & Philp (1998), Modern Language Journal: recasts and development of question forms; Mackey (1999), SSLA, 21(4), 557-587: interaction and question formation in ESL.
- Mackey & Goo (2007), "Interaction research in SLA: A meta-analysis and research synthesis", in A. Mackey (Ed.), Conversational interaction in second language acquisition, Oxford University Press: interaction had a medium to large effect on L2 learning in the studies reviewed (effect-size values not re-verified here).
- Keck, Iberri-Shea, Tracy-Ventura & Wa-Mbaleka (2006) meta-analysis of NNS-NNS interaction and native-speaker comparisons (as recalled).
- Li (2010), Language Learning, 60(2): meta-analysis of corrective feedback. Lyster & Saito (2010), SSLA, 32(2): meta-analysis in classrooms favoring prompts over recasts for some outcomes.
- Gass (1997), Input, interaction, and the second language learner; Gass & Mackey (2007) in VanPatten & Williams (Eds.), Theories in SLA.
- Long (2015), Second language acquisition and task-based language teaching, Wiley-Blackwell, restates and extends the proposal.
- Computer-mediated interaction and text chat research extended the paradigm (Smith, 2003, among others).

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> connection / critique angle)
- Paper on negotiation of meaning, information-gap tasks, pair work -> locate it in the interaction hypothesis; check whether it measures learning or only negotiation counts.
- Paper on recasts, prompts or corrective feedback -> Long (1996) explains why recasts were studied; critique with Lyster & Ranta (1997) on ambiguity and uptake, and with Lyster & Saito (2010).
- Paper on task design (two-way, closed, convergent tasks) -> Pica et al. (1993) and Long's link between task and negotiation.
- Paper claims "comprehensible input is enough" -> counter with Long (1996) and Swain (1985).
- Paper uses "noticing" as a mechanism -> connect Long's selective attention to Schmidt (1990).
- Paper is sociocultural or CA-based -> contrast: interaction as conduit (cognitive-interactionist) vs interaction as constitutive of learning.
- Critique angle: effects of immediate post-tests only, small samples, lab artificiality, developmental readiness.

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8 with short model answers)
1. **What changed between Long's 1983 and 1996 versions?** 1996 adds negative feedback, selective attention and output to the role of negotiation; the focus moves from comprehensible input to a link between input, attention and output.
2. **Distinguish positive and negative evidence.** Positive evidence shows what is possible in the L2 (input); negative evidence shows what is not (correction, feedback). Long argues negative evidence can be facilitative.
3. **Why are recasts central in interactionist research?** They are implicit, keep the focus on meaning, supply positive and negative evidence together, and are common in conversation.
4. **What is negotiation for meaning?** Conversational work (clarification requests, confirmation and comprehension checks, recasts) used to resolve communication breakdowns, which adjusts input and draws attention to form.
5. **Compare the interaction hypothesis with the input hypothesis.** Krashen: comprehensible input suffices. Long: interaction adds feedback and attention, so input alone is not enough.
6. **Name two criticisms.** Evidence for learning (not just comprehension) was slow to appear; sociocultural scholars reject the conduit view of interaction. Also lab-to-classroom gap and weak measures of noticing.
7. **How does it relate to Schmidt (1990)?** Negotiation helps learners notice. Long's selective attention connects directly to the noticing hypothesis.
8. **Did Long claim to offer a full theory?** No. He cautioned it is not a complete theory of language learning.
