# Communicative competence framework (Canale & Swain, 1980)

**One-line message:** Knowing a language means more than knowing grammar: it means being able to use it appropriately and to fix communication breakdowns, so teaching and testing should target several distinct competences, not one.

## Gold nuggets (3-5)
- This is THE reference model of "communicative competence" in language teaching and testing. If a paper says "communicative competence" and cites a model, it is very often this one.
- Original model has THREE components: **grammatical**, **sociolinguistic**, **strategic**. Canale (1983) later split out a fourth, **discourse competence**, giving the common "four-part" version. Do not credit the four-part version to the 1980 paper alone.
- It is a response to Chomsky's competence/performance split (too narrow, ignores use) and builds on Hymes (1972), who said speakers need knowledge of what is formally possible, feasible, appropriate and actually done.
- It is a pedagogical and testing framework first, not a theory of how acquisition happens. It says what to teach and assess, not how learners develop it.
- The paper is long (pages 1-47) and has two jobs: (1) define the construct, (2) draw out guidelines for communicative syllabus design, teaching and testing. Many citations only use job 1.

## The problem it answered (context in that year)
- Late 1970s: "communicative" approaches were spreading fast (notional-functional syllabuses, Wilkins 1976; Council of Europe work), but the term had no clear theoretical backbone.
- Audiolingual and grammar-translation practice had focused on form. Tests mostly measured discrete grammar and vocabulary items.
- Chomsky's (1965) competence (idealized grammatical knowledge) vs performance (actual use) was seen as too thin for describing real language ability. Hymes (1972) had argued for "communicative competence" in sociolinguistics, but it was not yet turned into a usable teaching and testing model.
- Authors were at the Ontario Institute for Studies in Education (OISE), working on second language (especially French as a second language) programs and testing in Canada.
- Aim: give teachers, syllabus designers and test makers a principled definition of what "communicative" means.

## The argument / design in plain language
This is a conceptual and theoretical paper, not an empirical study (no participants, no statistics).

**Step 1: Define communicative competence.** Authors say it includes knowledge and skill needed to communicate. Their model has three areas:
1. **Grammatical competence:** knowledge of the language code (vocabulary, word formation, sentence structure, pronunciation, spelling, literal meaning). Roughly "can I build correct sentences?"
2. **Sociolinguistic competence:** knowledge of sociocultural rules of use and rules of discourse, so that utterances are produced and understood appropriately in context. In the 1980 version, discourse rules sit here. Roughly "can I say the right thing to the right person in the right setting?"
3. **Strategic competence (1980: compensatory only, for repairing breakdowns; Canale, 1983, later added strategies that enhance the effectiveness of communication):** verbal and nonverbal strategies used to compensate for breakdowns caused by limited competence or performance limits (for example paraphrase, circumlocution, repetition, hesitation, changes of style). Roughly "can I repair the conversation when I get stuck?"

**Step 2: Separate competence from actual communication.** The authors keep a distinction between underlying knowledge and skill (competence) and actual use in real situations (actual communication), while rejecting Chomsky's idealization that strips out social context.

**Step 3: Derive principles for a communicative approach.** Stated in plain terms (paraphrase):
- Communicative competence consists of the integrated components above; no one component should dominate.
- A communicative program should aim to meet learners' communicative needs, with meaningful interaction as central.
- Learners need chances to take part in authentic communication early, with feedback.
- Native-like grammatical accuracy is not the only target; being able to communicate despite gaps matters.
- Testing should assess communicative ability, not only isolated grammar points, and should reflect the same components.

**Step 4: Apply to teaching and testing.** The paper discusses implications for syllabus design (content, sequencing), classroom practice and test construction, and argues that tests, like programs, should be informed by an explicit competence model.

## Why it matters / impact (what it changed, citation influence)
- Became the default definition of communicative competence in applied linguistics, teacher education and language testing for decades. The paper is among the most cited in the journal Applied Linguistics (exact count not verified).
- Gave Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) a theoretical justification and a vocabulary teachers could use (see Savignon, 1983).
- Spawned the major successor models: Canale (1983, adds discourse), Bachman (1990) and Bachman & Palmer (1996, "communicative language ability"), Celce-Murcia, Dornyei & Thurrell (1995; adds actional and sociocultural competence, with discourse at the center).
- Fed into curriculum frameworks and proficiency scales, including the Council of Europe's CEFR (2001), whose "communicative language competences" (linguistic, sociolinguistic, pragmatic) echo the structure.
- Made "strategic competence" a research topic (communication strategies research: Faerch & Kasper, 1983; Bialystok, 1990).

### Quick classroom picture (my illustration, not from the paper)
- A learner ordering food: knowing the verb forms and word order is grammatical competence.
- Choosing "Could I possibly have..." for a waiter vs "Gimme..." with a friend is sociolinguistic competence.
- Saying "the thing you cut bread with" when the word "knife" is missing is strategic competence.
- Linking the order, a complaint and an apology into a coherent exchange is discourse competence (Canale, 1983).
- Assessment consequence: a test that only checks verb forms measures one of the components, not the whole construct.

## Known limitations and critiques (who, what)
- **Not a theory of acquisition:** says what the end state includes, not how learners get there. Needs pairing with SLA theory.
- **Static list of knowledge components:** critics (for example Widdowson, 1983, on "capacity" vs "competence") say it underplays the ability to use knowledge creatively and the dynamic, negotiated nature of interaction.
- **Native-speaker and monolingual orientation:** the model assumes target-community norms. Later work (Leung, 2005; Firth & Wagner, 1997; Canagarajah; ELF research) challenges the idealized native speaker as the yardstick.
- **Vague boundaries:** how sociolinguistic and strategic competence differ from grammatical competence is hard to operationalize. Strategic competence in particular has been called a catch-all.
- **Weak empirical grounding:** no data; later testing researchers (Bachman, 1990) point out the model does not specify how components interact in performance, which matters for test validation.
- **Discourse competence missing in 1980:** fixed by Canale (1983) but often cited incorrectly.
- **Pragmatics under-specified:** Bachman (1990) separates organizational and pragmatic knowledge more cleanly; Celce-Murcia et al. (1995) add actional competence.

## What came after (follow-up studies, revisions, meta-analyses)
- Canale (1983): four components (grammatical, sociolinguistic, discourse, strategic).
- Bachman (1990), Fundamental considerations in language testing, Oxford University Press: Communicative Language Ability with language competence (organizational, pragmatic), strategic competence and psychophysiological mechanisms. Bachman & Palmer (1996) refine this for test design.
- Celce-Murcia, Dornyei & Thurrell (1995), Issues in Applied Linguistics, 6(2), 5-35: linguistic, strategic, sociocultural, actional and discourse competence.
- Council of Europe (2001) CEFR and the 2020 Companion Volume: can-do descriptors for communicative language competences.
- Critical re-readings: Leung (2005), "Convivial communication: Recontextualizing communicative competence", International Journal of Applied Linguistics, 15(2), 119-144; Hall, Cheng & Carlson (2006) on "competence" in a practice view; the Douglas Fir Group (2016) transdisciplinary framework.
- Related constructs in other traditions: interactional competence (Young, 2011; Kramsch, 1986), intercultural communicative competence (Byram, 1997), pragmatic competence (Kasper & Rose, 2002).

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> connection / critique angle)
- Paper defines or measures "communicative competence" -> check which version it uses (1980 three-part, 1983 four-part, Bachman, Celce-Murcia). Name it precisely.
- Paper designs a CLT syllabus or task -> link components to activities: grammar practice (grammatical), role plays (sociolinguistic), information gaps with breakdown (strategic), extended texts (discourse).
- Paper teaches or studies communication strategies -> this is the origin of "strategic competence".
- Paper tests speaking with a rubric (accuracy, fluency, appropriateness, strategies) -> rubric categories echo the model; ask whether construct validity evidence is offered.
- Paper adopts a sociocultural or CA view of "interactional competence" -> use as the foil: competence as individual knowledge (Canale & Swain) vs competence as co-constructed in interaction.
- Paper adopts ELF or translanguaging -> critique the native-speaker norm embedded in the model.
- Critique angle for any paper that only cites this model: no acquisition theory, no data behind the components, components overlap.

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8 with short model answers)
1. **What three competences did Canale and Swain (1980) propose?** Grammatical, sociolinguistic and strategic competence. Discourse competence was added by Canale (1983).
2. **How did they respond to Chomsky and Hymes?** They kept a competence/performance distinction but widened competence to include appropriateness and strategies, following Hymes's view that knowing a language includes knowing when and how to use it.
3. **What is strategic competence and why does it matter for learners?** Verbal and nonverbal strategies for compensating for gaps and repairing breakdowns. It lets learners with limited language keep communicating, so teaching it is justified.
4. **Is the model a theory of SLA?** No. It is a descriptive and pedagogical framework for what to teach and assess. It does not explain developmental processes.
5. **Compare Canale and Swain with Bachman (1990).** Bachman treats language ability as language competence (organizational and pragmatic) plus strategic competence and psychophysiological mechanisms, and models how they interact in test performance. It is designed for test validation.
6. **What are two main criticisms?** Static list of knowledge components that underplays dynamic, co-constructed interaction; native-speaker, monolingual norm. Also weak empirical basis.
7. **How did the model influence teaching and testing?** It legitimized CLT, pushed syllabuses toward use and function, and moved tests toward communicative tasks and multi-component rubrics.
8. **Why is it wrong to say the 1980 paper has four components?** Discourse competence was introduced in Canale (1983); the 1980 paper placed discourse rules within sociolinguistic competence.

### Lineage cheat sheet (for placing a paper fast)
- Chomsky (1965): competence vs performance (idealized grammar knowledge vs use).
- Hymes (1972): communicative competence as knowing what is formally possible, feasible, appropriate and done.
- Canale & Swain (1980): three components, for teaching and testing.
- Canale (1983): adds discourse competence, and four dimensions of the pedagogy.
- Bachman (1990), Bachman & Palmer (1996): communicative language ability, for test validation.
- Celce-Murcia et al. (1995): pedagogically motivated model with content specifications.
- Council of Europe (2001, 2020): CEFR communicative language competences and can-do descriptors.
- Critical successors: interactional competence (Young), intercultural communicative competence (Byram), ELF and multilingual competence.
