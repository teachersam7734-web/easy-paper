# Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition (Krashen, 1982)

**One-line message:** We acquire language subconsciously by understanding messages slightly above our level (comprehensible input) in a low-anxiety setting; conscious rule learning only plays a small editing role.

## Gold nuggets (the 3-5 things everyone must know)
1. **Five hypotheses:** (1) acquisition-learning, (2) natural order, (3) monitor, (4) input (i+1), (5) affective filter. Memorise them in this order, as the book presents them.
2. **Acquisition vs learning:** acquisition is subconscious, like child L1 picking up; learning is conscious knowledge of rules. Krashen claims **learning never turns into acquisition** (the "no-interface" position).
3. **Comprehensible input is the one causal variable.** We acquire by understanding input containing structures a little beyond our current level (**i+1**). Speaking is the result of acquisition, not its cause.
4. **Affective filter:** anxiety, low motivation and low self-confidence block input from reaching the language acquisition device. Teaching should lower the filter.
5. **It was hugely influential but heavily criticised:** the hypotheses are hard to falsify, "acquisition" and "learning" are not operationalised, and the claim that output does nothing was challenged by Swain (1985) and Schmidt (1990).

## The problem it answered (context in that year)
- Late 1970s to early 1980s: SLA was becoming a field. Behaviourist audiolingual teaching had lost credibility after Chomsky's critique (1959) and the rise of L1 acquisition research.
- Morpheme studies (Brown, 1973 in L1; Dulay & Burt, 1973-74 in L2) suggested a predictable order of acquisition irrespective of instruction.
- Teachers needed a theory-backed alternative to grammar-translation and drill methods. Krashen (with Tracy Terrell) offered the Natural Approach (Krashen & Terrell, 1983).
- The 1982 book is a consolidated statement of the "Monitor Model" he had built since the late 1970s (e.g., Krashen, 1977, 1981); it adds a practice section for teachers.

## The argument / design in plain language
This is a **theory and practice book**, not an empirical study (no participants or design of its own). Krashen reviews prior studies (morpheme order, adult vs child, immersion, formal instruction) and uses them as supporting evidence.

**The five hypotheses in plain language:**
1. **Acquisition-Learning:** two separate systems. Acquisition = picking up language by using it for communication (subconscious). Learning = knowing rules (conscious). Only acquisition drives fluency.
2. **Natural Order:** grammar structures are acquired in a predictable order (e.g., progressive -ing and plural -s early, third person -s late), regardless of teaching order. This order is not the same as the textbook order.
3. **Monitor:** conscious learning acts only as an editor ("Monitor") that checks output. Use of the Monitor needs three conditions: enough **time**, a focus on **form**, and knowledge of the **rule**. Krashen described over-users, under-users and "optimal" users of the Monitor.
4. **Input (Comprehension):** we acquire by understanding messages with i+1 (current level plus a bit more). Context, pictures, and extra-linguistic information help us understand i+1. If input is understood and plentiful, grammar is automatically provided. Speech emerges on its own.
5. **Affective Filter:** emotional variables (motivation, self-confidence, anxiety) act like a filter. Learners with a high filter get input but do not acquire it. (Roots: Dulay & Burt, 1977.)

**Practical implications (second half of the book):** classroom time should deliver comprehensible input in a low-anxiety atmosphere; grammar teaching has limited use (feeding the Monitor, for older learners at best); error correction has little effect on acquisition; use reading and listening heavily; delay forced speaking.

## Why it matters / impact (what it changed, citation influence)
- The most widely taught SLA theory of the 1980s; "comprehensible input" became part of teacher training vocabulary worldwide.
- Shaped the Natural Approach, sheltered content instruction, immersion design, extensive reading (Krashen's later "The Power of Reading", 1993), and the idea of lowering anxiety (Horwitz et al. 1986 on foreign language anxiety grew partly out of affective filter debates).
- Pushed researchers to theorise input: Long's Interaction Hypothesis (1981, 1996), Swain's Output Hypothesis (1985), Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis (1990), VanPatten's input processing, and Gass's model.
- Even critics treat Krashen as the necessary starting point for any literature review on input.

## Known limitations and critiques (who, what)
- **Untestable/circular:** acquisition vs learning is not independently measurable; "acquired" is defined by what happens without conscious knowledge. McLaughlin (1978, 1987) criticised the vagueness and unfalsifiability; Gregg (1984), "Krashen's Monitor and Occam's razor" in *Applied Linguistics*, argued the model lacks explanatory power.
- **Input alone is not enough:** Swain (1985) showed Canadian immersion students with vast input still had non-native grammar; output pushes accuracy.
- **Interface debates:** many (e.g., DeKeyser; N. Ellis; R. Ellis) argue explicit knowledge can become automatised or aid noticing; Norris & Ortega (2000) showed instruction works and explicit methods do well.
- **"i+1" is vague:** how do we measure it? How can the teacher find each learner's i? (Gass & Selinker and others critique.)
- **Natural order over-claimed:** later work shows variability and L1 influence (e.g., Larsen-Freeman, Zobl; details not verified here).
- **Affective filter has little direct operationalisation,** though anxiety research supports a link (MacIntyre & Gardner).
- **Neglected interaction and meaning negotiation:** Long (1996) argued conversational modification is key.
- **L1 acquisition analogy** for adult L2 learners is contested (Critical Period research, Birdsong).

## What came after (follow-up studies, revisions, meta-analyses)
- Krashen (1985) *The Input Hypothesis: Issues and Implications* (Longman) develops the input claim.
- Long (1981, 1983) on conversational adjustments; Long (1996) updated Interaction Hypothesis.
- Swain (1985, 1995): output hypothesis (see swain-1985-output-hypothesis.md).
- Schmidt (1990): noticing hypothesis; Schmidt & Frota (1986).
- VanPatten (1996): input processing; Skill Acquisition Theory (DeKeyser, 2007).
- Meta-analyses of instruction (Norris & Ortega, 2000; Spada & Tomita, 2010) show explicit instruction works.
- Krashen continues to defend the Comprehension Hypothesis (2003 *Explorations in Language Acquisition and Use*; later work on reading, free voluntary reading).

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> connection / critique angle)
- Paper mentions "comprehensible input", "exposure", "extensive reading" -> link to Krashen's input hypothesis, then add Long (interaction) or Swain (output) as limits.
- Paper deals with anxiety, motivation, willingness to communicate -> connect to the affective filter; for L2 anxiety use Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope (1986) and MacIntyre & Gardner.
- Paper compares explicit vs implicit teaching -> position against Krashen's no-interface claim; cite Norris & Ortega (2000), DeKeyser (2007).
- Paper uses developmental sequences (morpheme order) -> connect to natural order, add Pienemann's Processability Theory and variability.
- Paper uses error correction or feedback -> Krashen predicts little effect; contrast with Lyster & Ranta (1997) and meta-analyses.
- Paper advocates immersion/CLIL -> Krashen's theoretical justification; add Swain's output critique.
- Paper claims "theory X is untestable" -> parallel to McLaughlin's critique of the Monitor Model.

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8 with short model answers)
1. **List Krashen's five hypotheses.** Acquisition-learning, natural order, monitor, input (i+1), affective filter.
2. **Distinguish acquisition from learning.** Acquisition is subconscious, communication-driven; learning is conscious rule knowledge. Learning does not become acquisition (no-interface).
3. **Under what conditions can the Monitor be used?** Enough time, focus on form, knowledge of the rule.
4. **What does i+1 mean and why is it problematic?** Input one step above the learner's current level; it is hard to define or measure and varies by learner.
5. **How did Swain (1985) challenge Krashen?** Immersion students with massive comprehensible input still lacked accurate grammar; producing output (pushed output) is needed.
6. **Why is the Monitor Model criticised as unfalsifiable?** Acquisition and learning cannot be independently observed, so any result can be explained post hoc (McLaughlin, 1978; Gregg, 1984).
7. **What classroom practices follow from the model?** Maximise comprehensible input, lower anxiety, minimise error correction and grammar drills, delay forced speech (Natural Approach).
8. **What is the affective filter?** A motivational/emotional barrier (anxiety, low confidence) which stops input from being used for acquisition.

## Memory hooks and quick checks (for fast revision)
- **Mnemonic for the five:** "A N M I A" = Acquisition-learning, Natural order, Monitor, Input, Affective filter.
- **Monitor conditions mnemonic:** "T-F-R" = Time, Form focus, Rule known.
- **Three Monitor user types:** over-user (monitors everything, hesitant), under-user (relies on feel), optimal user (monitors when it will not disrupt communication).
- **Classroom example:** a teacher uses pictures, gestures and slow clear speech to tell a story (comprehensible input), accepts one-word answers (low anxiety, no forced output) and does not correct errors during the story. That is the Natural Approach in miniature.
- **One-line contrast with rivals:**
  - Krashen: input is enough.
  - Long: input made comprehensible through interaction.
  - Swain: you also need pushed output.
  - Schmidt: you need to notice the form.
  - DeKeyser: practice turns explicit knowledge into automatic skill.
- **Historical caution:** the 1982 book is a revised, updated version of ideas from Krashen (1981) *Second Language Acquisition and Second Language Learning* (Pergamon). When a paper cites "Krashen 1981" vs "1982", they are close in content but not identical.
- **Common error to avoid:** saying Krashen has "one hypothesis". It is a set of five, usually called the Monitor Model or Monitor Theory (the input hypothesis is the centrepiece).
