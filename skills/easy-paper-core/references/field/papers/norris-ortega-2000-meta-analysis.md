# Effectiveness of L2 instruction (Norris & Ortega, 2000)

**One-line message:** Pooling 49 studies shows that focused L2 instruction produces large gains over no instruction, explicit approaches beat implicit ones, and the effects last; but the study designs and outcome measures in the field limit how far we can trust this.

## Gold nuggets (the 3-5 things everyone must know)
1. **First major meta-analysis in instructed SLA.** It answered "does teaching work?" with numbers (effect sizes), not opinion, ending a long Krashen-era argument on the basic question.
2. **Headline results:** instruction is clearly effective (overall effect large, about d = 0.96 per secondary sources); explicit beats implicit; Focus on Form and Focus on FormS are about equally effective; effects are durable.
3. **Sample:** 77 studies were located and screened, and 49 unique samples had enough data for the meta-analysis (studies from 1980 to 1998).
4. **Big caveat:** the measures favoured explicit instruction (mostly constrained, controlled response tasks like grammar tests). Explicit instruction also tends to be tested in a way that matches how it was taught. So "explicit wins" partly reflects what was measured.
5. **Methodological legacy:** the paper pushed the field to report effect sizes, means, SDs and sample sizes, and to use a clear coding scheme. Later critics note it did not control quality or publication bias.

## The problem it answered (context in that year)
- By the 1990s the field argued about whether formal instruction helps at all (Krashen: acquisition not learning; Long, 1983, said yes, instruction helps; Ellis, 1990s reviews).
- Narrative reviews (Long 1983; Ellis 1994) reached mixed conclusions and could not compare studies with different designs, measures and outcomes.
- Meta-analysis (Glass, 1976; Hedges & Olkin, 1985) was well established in psychology and education, but little used in SLA. Norris and Ortega argued SLA needed it to cumulate evidence.
- The "focus on form" movement (Long, 1991; Long & Robinson, 1998) needed evidence for form-focused vs meaning-only teaching.

## The argument / design in plain language
**Design (a research synthesis plus a quantitative meta-analysis):**
- Search: published and unpublished studies on L2 instruction, 1980 to 1998, via databases, journals, and reference lists. 77 studies screened, **49 unique samples** coded as having enough statistics to compute effect sizes (Wikipedia-level and DeepDyve abstracts confirm 49; the "77" comes from a critique of the paper).
- Effect size: standardised mean differences (Cohen's d style). Pre-post and treatment-control contrasts were pooled; samples were weighted equally in the original analysis (a point critics later raised).
- Coding: type of instruction (explicit vs implicit; Focus on Form vs Focus on FormS), type of outcome measure (constrained vs free response), length of treatment, and delayed posttests for durability.

**Key results (numbers):**
- Overall effect of focused L2 instruction: about **d = 0.96** (large), reported in secondary sources; 56% of primary studies used experimental designs with control or comparison groups but only about 17% used true control groups (per a critique).
- **Explicit vs implicit:** explicit instruction produced larger effects. Commonly cited values are d = 1.13 for explicit and d = 0.54 for implicit (unverified in this session, taken from widely repeated summaries; check against Table in the paper).
- **Focus on Form vs Focus on FormS:** both effective and roughly equal (values often cited as about 0.93 and 1.09, unverified here). The authors cautioned that the "equal" result does not prove equivalence in real classrooms.
- **Durability:** gains held on delayed posttests, though the delayed tests often came only weeks later.
- **Measure type:** effects were larger on constrained-response tests (e.g., cloze, multiple choice, controlled production) than on free-response tests (spontaneous speaking or writing).
- **Length:** longer treatments did not clearly outperform very short ones in this sample (reported as an unresolved question; unverified detail).

## Key constructs introduced (term | plain gloss | canonical definition w/ citation if verified)
| Term | Plain gloss | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Effect size (d) | How big the difference between groups is, in SD units | Standardised mean difference (Cohen, 1988 convention: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large). |
| Research synthesis | Systematic, transparent review of all relevant studies | Authors' approach, as opposed to a narrative review (paraphrase). |
| Explicit instruction | Rules are explained or learner is told to attend to form | Norris & Ortega coded treatments as explicit if they involved rule explanation or directing learners' attention to the target form (paraphrase). |
| Implicit instruction | No rules given, no directions to notice | Learning conditions without rule presentation or attention direction (paraphrase). |
| Focus on Form (FonF) | Brief attention to form within meaning-based activity | Long (1991) distinction, used here as a coding category. |
| Focus on FormS (FonFS) | Teaching a pre-selected series of forms | Long (1991) distinction. |
| Constrained vs free response | Controlled tests vs open production | Outcome measure categories; constrained favours explicit knowledge. |
| Durability | Whether gains persist after treatment | Judged from delayed posttests. |

## Why it matters / impact (what it changed, citation influence)
- Among the most cited papers in *Language Learning* and in instructed SLA.
- Established the empirical case for instruction and for explicit, form-focused work, supporting a shift from pure input/communicative approaches.
- Spawned a meta-analysis tradition: Norris & Ortega (2001, in the Language Learning supplement, "Does type of instruction make a difference?"); Spada & Tomita (2010) on interface of explicit/implicit; Goo et al. (2015) on explicit and implicit instruction; Li (2010) on corrective feedback; Lyster & Saito (2010) on oral corrective feedback; Plonsky (2011) on strategy instruction; Mackey & Goo (2007) on interaction.
- Pushed the field toward reporting practices (APA-style effect sizes, confidence intervals). Plonsky & Oswald (2014) later proposed field-specific effect size benchmarks (small d about 0.40, medium about 0.70, large about 1.00 for between-group contrasts).

## Known limitations and critiques (who, what)
- **Measure bias:** most outcome tests were constrained, favouring explicit knowledge and explicit teaching (Doughty, 2003; Ellis, 2002 raised similar points; exact citations unverified here).
- **Design quality:** 47% of samples did not report random assignment; 28% had 10 or fewer participants and 88.5% had 30 or fewer; experimental and quasi-experimental designs were pooled; no quality screening (Shin, 2010, "Another look at Norris and Ortega (2000)", Studies in Applied Linguistics & TESOL, per Teachers College Columbia).
- **Statistics:** samples equally weighted, ignoring sampling error from different n; Shin suggests Hedges' g or hierarchical linear modelling. Possible publication bias (no systematic grey-literature search).
- **Coding simplifications:** moderators such as age, proficiency, target language not coded; categories possibly misclassified input processing and input enhancement.
- **Short treatments, short delayed tests:** "durable" may mean weeks, not years.
- **Possible inflation of overall d** noted by Shin.
- **Ecological validity:** many studies were laboratory-like, so generalising to full classrooms is risky.

## What came after (follow-up studies, revisions, meta-analyses)
- Norris & Ortega (2001), Language Learning, 51(Suppl. 1), 157-213: follow-up on type of instruction (DeepDyve confirms this as "Does type of instruction make a difference?").
- Spada & Tomita (2010), *Language Learning*, 60(2): explicit vs implicit, with attention to complex vs simple features.
- Goo, Granena, Yilmaz & Novella (2015) in Rebuschat (Ed.), *Implicit and Explicit Learning of Languages*: re-examination of explicit vs implicit with respect to measure type.
- Plonsky (2013, SSLA) on study quality and reporting in SLA; Plonsky & Oswald (2014, Language Learning) on interpreting effect sizes.
- Meta-analytic wave in the 2010s on feedback, task-based teaching, and vocabulary (not all verified here).

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> connection / critique angle)
- Paper claims "explicit instruction works" -> cite N&O for support, then ask what measure was used (constrained vs free response). Align with test-treatment mismatch.
- Paper tests Focus on Form vs Focus on FormS -> N&O found them about equal; ask whether the new study controls what N&O could not.
- Paper reports only p-values -> point to N&O's push for effect sizes, CIs and descriptive statistics.
- Paper uses a very small sample -> note N&O's finding that most primary studies were small (88.5% n of 30 or fewer, per critique).
- Paper claims lasting effects -> check delay length versus N&O's durability caveat.
- Literature review in a paper cites N&O -> check whether it cites the 2000 article or the 2001 supplement chapter correctly.
- Paper proposes a meta-analysis -> compare quality screening, publication bias handling, and weighting to N&O's choices.

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8 with short model answers)
1. **What question did N&O address and how?** Whether L2 instruction is effective, through a research synthesis and meta-analysis of 49 unique samples (1980-1998).
2. **What did they conclude about explicit versus implicit instruction?** Explicit instruction produced larger effects, but tests were mostly constrained measures, which favour explicit knowledge.
3. **Did Focus on Form beat Focus on FormS?** No; both were effective and similar in size, so the evidence did not favour one.
4. **What is an effect size and why is it better than a p-value?** It expresses magnitude (e.g., d in SD units) and is comparable across studies; p-values only indicate whether a difference is likely not zero.
5. **Name two limitations of the meta-analysis.** Reliance on constrained measures and lack of quality control or publication bias checks (also equal weighting, small samples, short delays).
6. **Why is durability a caveat?** Delayed posttests were often only weeks after treatment, so long-term retention is unproven.
7. **What was the impact on the field?** It legitimised instruction research with evidence, set standards for effect-size reporting and launched a meta-analysis tradition.
8. **How do Plonsky and Oswald (2014) relate?** They argue Cohen's benchmarks are too lenient for SLA and propose field-specific benchmarks based on typical effect sizes.

## Memory hooks and quick checks (for fast revision)
- **Numbers:** 49 unique samples, 77 studies screened, 1980-1998, overall d about 0.96. Citation: Language Learning 50(3), 417-528 (a very long article, about 110 pages).
- **One-sentence verdict:** instruction works, explicit beats implicit, FonF equals FonFS, effects last, but trust the measures with care.
- **Effect size benchmarks:** Cohen (1988): 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. Plonsky & Oswald (2014) say SLA between-group contrasts are better read at about 0.4, 0.7, 1.0.
- **Do not confuse:** the 2000 article (Language Learning 50(3)) and the 2001 chapter "Does type of instruction make a difference? Substantive findings from a meta-analytic review" (Language Learning 51, Suppl. 1, 157-213). Cite the right one.
- **Meta-analysis in one breath:** collect all studies, convert each to an effect size, average them (ideally weighted by sample size), then test what explains the differences (moderators such as instruction type or measure type).
- **Why equal weighting matters:** a study with 8 learners and a study with 80 should not count the same, since small studies give noisy estimates.
- **Relation to Krashen:** N&O is the empirical answer to the claim that formal instruction has little effect on acquisition (see krashen-1982-monitor-model.md).
- **Relation to Long (1991):** supplies the FonF vs FonFS coding categories (see long-1996-interaction-hypothesis.md for Long's later work).

## Reading guide for a hurried student
- Skip the 100+ pages of coding tables on a first pass.
- Read: abstract, the research questions, the results sections on overall effect, explicit vs implicit, and FonF vs FonFS.
- Then read the discussion of methodological problems: it is the most quotable part for critique.
- Use the effect sizes only after checking them in the original tables.
