# The call to reconceptualize SLA (Firth & Wagner, 1997)

**One-line message:** SLA has been too cognitive, too individual and too tied to the idea of the learner as a deficient non-native speaker; it should widen its scope to include social and interactional views, and rethink its basic concepts.

## Gold nuggets (3-5)
- This is the **founding text of the "social turn" debate** in SLA. It triggered a famous exchange in the Modern Language Journal (1997 responses, 1998 rejoinders) and is the usual starting point for arguments about cognitive vs social SLA.
- **Three proposed changes** (from the abstract): (1) increased awareness of the **contextual and interactional dimensions** of language use; (2) an increased **emic** (participant-relevant) sensitivity toward fundamental concepts; (3) a significant **broadening of the traditional SLA data base**.
- Target of critique: the **individual-cognitive** bias. Verified quotes (as reported in a secondary source): it leads to "a general methodological bias and theoretical imbalance in SLA studies" (p. 288).
- Concepts they attack: **"learner", "native speaker" / "non-native speaker", "interlanguage"**, "target language", "communication strategies". Terms like NS and NNS "are blanket terms, implying homogeneity throughout each group" (p. 291).
- Important nuance: they said they were **not** claiming the criticized approaches were erroneous, only that they produced bias and imbalance (p. 288). The paper is often misread as an attack on cognitive SLA as a whole.

## The problem it answered (context in that year)
- 1990s mainstream SLA: dominated by psycholinguistic and cognitive models (Universal Grammar research, information processing, Krashen, Long's interaction hypothesis, Schmidt's noticing). Researchers largely studied interlanguage as individual mental knowledge, measured against native-speaker norms.
- Long (1990) and others argued for "theory culling" and strict scientific standards; Firth & Wagner partly respond to this stance (pp. 285-286, as reported in a secondary source).
- Meanwhile, **conversation analysis (CA)**, ethnomethodology, sociolinguistics, discourse analysis, and Vygotskian sociocultural theory were developing outside SLA proper. Firth had worked on "lingua franca" business interactions with CA (Firth, 1996); Wagner on workplace and foreign-language communication.
- Observation: the same data (for example, non-native speaker talk) can look like "deficient communication" or "competent, locally-managed interaction" depending on the lens.

## The argument / design in plain language
**Type:** a position paper with data illustration (not a conventional experiment). It uses short extracts of naturally occurring non-native/lingua-franca interaction, examined with conversation-analytic tools, to show how the same talk can be read differently (the sort of workplace and telephone interactions Firth and Wagner studied; specific extract details not re-verified here).

**Argument in steps (paraphrase):**
1. **Diagnosis:** SLA treats cognition and the individual as default, relegating social context to a background variable. This gives SLA a narrow view of discourse and communication.
2. **Deficit view:** Foreign-language speakers are framed as deficient communicators who are always on their way to an idealized native speaker target. Their abilities are measured by distance from that target.
3. **Alternative view:** Communication problems are better seen as **intersubjective, interactional phenomena** that participants manage together, not as "things possessed by individuals" (p. 291, as reported). Participants orient to identities beyond "learner" or "NNS" and use resources flexibly.
4. **Reconceptualization:** The field should take an **emic** perspective (what is relevant to the participants), examine categories like "learner" and "native" critically (p. 296), and include data beyond classrooms and experiments.
5. **Holistic aim:** Question the binary between social and individual/cognitive, and between **use and learning** (p. 296, as reported). Learning and use are intertwined.

**Style of claim:** It is not "social instead of cognitive". It calls for balance and for enlarging the "ontological and empirical parameters of the field" (p. 285, as reported).

## Why it matters / impact (what it changed, citation influence)
- Opened a long **methodological and ontological debate** in SLA; the paper is one of the most cited and discussed in the Modern Language Journal.
- Helped legitimize **CA for SLA** (Markee, 2000; Seedhouse, 2004; Kasper & Wagner, 2011), **sociocultural theory** (Lantolf), identity and investment research (Norton), **language socialization**, and **complexity/dynamic systems** approaches.
- Pushed **English as a lingua franca (ELF)** and multilingual turns, and wider talk of "learners" as multilingual users (Cook, 1999 on multi-competence; Ortega, 2013; May, 2014).
- Influenced the **Douglas Fir Group (2016)** transdisciplinary framework in the MLJ, which explicitly integrates micro (cognitive), meso (sociocultural) and macro (ideological) levels.
- Firth & Wagner (2007) "Second/foreign language learning as a social accomplishment" (MLJ, 91 Supplement s1, 800-819) elaborated and responded to ten years of discussion.

### Plain-language example (my illustration, not from the paper)
- A cognitive reading: a learner pauses and uses the wrong article in a phone call, so we note an interlanguage error and a lack of proficiency.
- Firth and Wagner's reading: both speakers treat the trouble as ordinary, fix it together in a turn or two, and get the business done. The talk is competent interaction, not just deficiency.
- Same data, different ontology: individual knowledge gap vs jointly managed interactional work.

### What the paper is NOT
- Not an anti-science manifesto: it asks for balance, not for dropping cognitive work.
- Not a CA methods paper: CA is a resource, and the larger point is about concepts and data.
- Not the origin of the phrase "social turn": that label is popularized later (Block, 2003).

## Known limitations and critiques (who, what)
Responses in the same MLJ issue (81(3), 1997) from Kasper, Long, Gass (1998), Poulisse, Liddicoat, Hall and others (see list below; titles recalled, details not all re-verified):
- **Long (1997), "Construct validity in SLA research: A response to Firth and Wagner":** argues SLA is about acquisition, a cognitive process, and that the authors' own categories lack construct validity; calls for theory-driven, testable claims.
- **Poulisse (1997), "Some words in defense of the psycholinguistic approach":** psycholinguistic research aims to explain and predict, not just describe (the quote about describing, explaining and predicting is reported in Firth & Wagner 2007, p. 802, via a secondary source).
- **Gass (1998):** "Apples and oranges" response: cognitive and social approaches address different questions; both are valid.
- **Kasper (1997):** "A" stands for acquisition: arguing that CA could contribute but that SLA is about acquisition and change over time, which CA of single interactions does not capture well. Kasper later became a leading advocate of CA-for-SLA (Kasper & Wagner, 2011).
- **Liddicoat (1997) and Hall (1997):** more sympathetic, develop social and practice-based views of language use and learning.
- **Common criticisms:** (1) straw man of mainstream SLA; (2) CA is silent on **learning over time**, and so cannot replace cognitive accounts; (3) shortage of evidence in the paper about how its alternative explains development; (4) claims of "bias" lack a systematic review of the literature.
- **Firth & Wagner's reply (1998), "SLA property: No trespassing!":** argues their arguments were staged "from outside SLA proper" and that responses treated SLA as private property.
- **Later critics of the social turn:** worries about relativism, loss of cumulative knowledge, and weak operationalization of "social" constructs; Zuengler & Miller (2006) argue the cognitive and sociocultural camps form "two parallel SLA worlds".

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> connection / critique angle)
- Paper uses CA, discourse analysis or ethnography on L2 talk -> place in the lineage of Firth & Wagner (1997); check whether it addresses learning or only use.
- Paper uses "native speaker" as benchmark, or "non-native" as a category -> raise the blanket-term and deficit critique.
- Paper focuses on identity, agency or investment -> social turn lineage (Norton; Block, 2003).
- Paper on ELF, translanguaging or multilingual users -> the deficit-view critique continues in multi-competence and the multilingual turn.
- Paper is cognitive and experimental -> respond fairly: Firth & Wagner call for balance; use Long (1997), Poulisse (1997) and Gass (1998) for the defense of cognitive SLA.
- Paper claims to integrate cognitive and social -> cite the Douglas Fir Group (2016) and check how learning is operationalized.
- Critique angle: does the study show change over time, or only moment-by-moment use? Is "social" operationalized or only invoked?

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8 with short model answers)
1. **What are Firth and Wagner's three proposals?** More attention to contextual and interactional dimensions of language use; emic sensitivity toward basic concepts; broader range of data.
2. **What do they criticize in mainstream SLA?** Individual-cognitive bias, a deficit view of the non-native speaker against an idealized native norm, and unexamined concepts such as learner, NS/NNS and interlanguage.
3. **Did they reject cognitive SLA?** No. They said it produced bias and imbalance, not that it was erroneous, and called for balance and broader scope.
4. **What is meant by an emic perspective?** Understanding categories and meanings as participants themselves orient to them, rather than imposing researcher-defined categories.
5. **How did Long and others respond?** Long: construct validity and the need for testable theory about acquisition; Poulisse: psycholinguistics explains and predicts; Gass: apples and oranges, both valid; Kasper: acquisition requires attention to change over time.
6. **What is the main weakness of the social-turn critique?** It had little to say about learning over time, which is the core of SLA; CA of single interactions shows use, not development (a point later work addresses with longitudinal CA).
7. **How did the paper change the field?** It legitimized CA, sociocultural, identity and ELF research within SLA and led to integrative frameworks such as the Douglas Fir Group (2016).
8. **Which later works elaborate or respond?** Firth & Wagner (1998, 2007), Block (2003), Zuengler & Miller (2006), Atkinson (2011), Douglas Fir Group (2016).
