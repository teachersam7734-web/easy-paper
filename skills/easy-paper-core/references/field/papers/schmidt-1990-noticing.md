# The Noticing Hypothesis (Schmidt, 1990)

**One-line message:** Input only becomes learnable intake when the learner consciously notices it, so attention to form is not optional.

## Gold nuggets (3-5)
- **Noticing = conscious registration of a feature in the input.** Schmidt's strong claim: "subliminal language learning is impossible" and noticing is the necessary (and, in the 1990 wording, sufficient) condition for turning input into intake (from the article abstract).
- **Intake** (for Schmidt) is "that part of the input that the learner notices" (paraphrase of the 1990 definition). This differs from Krashen's "comprehensible input = intake" idea.
- It is a **conceptual review**, not an experiment. Schmidt pulls together cognitive psychology of attention and consciousness and applies it to SLA, with illustrations from his and Frota's diary study of learning Portuguese (Schmidt & Frota, 1986).
- He distinguishes **noticing** (awareness at the level of surface forms) from **understanding** (awareness of a rule or principle). Noticing is what he claims is required.
- Strong version was softened later: Schmidt (1994, 2001) and Hulstijn & Schmidt (1994) concede that noticing helps but may not be strictly required for all learning; the "sufficient" part was dropped.

## The problem it answered (context in that year)
- 1980s SLA was dominated by input-based theory. Krashen (1985) said acquisition is unconscious and driven by comprehensible input (i+1), with conscious learning playing a limited "monitor" role.
- Open puzzle: learners are exposed to lots of input, yet do not acquire everything in it. Why do some forms get picked up and others do not?
- Cognitive psychology was producing work on attention, awareness, implicit learning (Reber, 1976, 1989) and subliminal perception, but SLA had not integrated it. Questions: can we learn without awareness? Is paying attention necessary?
- Schmidt wanted to clarify the fuzzy word "consciousness" and tie it to specific, testable claims about input processing.

## The argument / design in plain language
**Type:** theoretical and literature-based argument with case evidence. No participants or statistics in the main argument.

**Part 1: Untangling "consciousness".** Schmidt separates three senses (paraphrase):
1. **Awareness:** includes noticing (registering something) and understanding (grasping a rule or generalization).
2. **Intention:** learning on purpose vs incidentally.
3. **Knowledge:** implicit vs explicit knowledge (what the learner can verbalize).
He argues SLA debates had mixed these up.

**Part 2: Three questions about input processing.**
- **Subliminal learning (without any awareness)?** His conclusion: no. Noticing is needed for input to become intake.
- **Incidental learning (without intention)?** Yes, possible. People pick up language while doing meaning-focused tasks, if the task directs attention to the relevant material. Attention still seems needed.
- **Implicit learning (of rules/abstractions without awareness of the rule)?** The hardest one. He finds evidence both for implicit learning and for a benefit from conscious understanding, and suggests explanations may need to drop the idea of unconscious "rules" in the usual applied linguistics sense.

**Part 3: Evidence.**
- Diary study: Schmidt himself learned Portuguese in Brazil (about five weeks of classes plus interaction), kept a diary and made monthly recordings; with Sylvia Frota he analyzed them (Schmidt & Frota, 1986). Pattern: forms appeared in his speech only after he consciously noticed them in input (for example when a native speaker's use stood out or a teacher pointed it out). Hearing a form repeatedly without noticing did not lead to use.
- The earlier case of "Wes" (a learner with strong communicative ability but weak grammar; Schmidt, 1983) is used to illustrate that exposure alone does not guarantee grammatical development (recalled from the literature, not re-checked).
- Supporting ideas from psychology of attention (selective attention, limited capacity; dichotic listening/shadowing research).

**Part 4: Conditions that affect what is noticed** (paraphrase): frequency, perceptual salience, skill level, task demands, and instruction. Instruction can direct attention to forms.

## Why it matters / impact (what it changed, citation influence)
- One of the most cited papers in SLA. It put attention and awareness at the center of cognitive SLA.
- Direct basis for **focus on form** (Long, 1991; Long & Robinson, 1998), **consciousness-raising**, **input enhancement** (Sharwood Smith, 1993), **textual enhancement**, and **processing instruction** debates.
- Provided the theoretical justification for **corrective feedback** research: feedback works partly by making learners notice gaps (see Long, 1996; Lyster & Ranta, 1997).
- Shaped output research (Swain, 1995: output prompts noticing the gap) and tasks that elicit attention to form.
- Stimulated measurement research: think-aloud, stimulated recall, eye-tracking and reaction-time methods to measure noticing and awareness (Leow, 2015, for a review; eye-tracking studies such as Godfroid, Boers & Housen, 2013).
- Schmidt's later work (Schmidt, 2001, "Attention", in Robinson, Ed., Cognition and second language instruction, Cambridge University Press, 3-32; Schmidt, 2010, 2012) developed the role of attention and individual differences.

### Classroom picture (my illustration, not from the paper)
- A student hears "She goes to school" many times in a lesson but never registers the -s. Under Schmidt, that -s is not intake yet.
- The teacher writes the sentence on the board and circles -s, or recasts a mistake. The student notices the -s: now it can enter learning.
- Noticing is not mastery: it is the entry ticket. Practice, repeated exposure and consolidation still follow.
- Contrast: "understanding" would be explaining the rule for third-person singular.

### Strong vs weak versions (handy for critique)
- Strong (1990): no noticing, no intake; noticing is necessary and sufficient.
- Weak (Schmidt, 1994, 2001): noticing is helpful and much of learning requires attention, but some learning may occur with less awareness.
- Most critics target the strong version; most empirical work supports the weak version.

## Known limitations and critiques (who, what)
- **Truscott (1998), Second Language Research, 14(2), 103-135:** critical review, argues the hypothesis is vague, empirically weak, and that its cognitive psychology basis is shaky; evidence for noticing as necessary is thin.
- **Tomlin & Villa (1994), SSLA, 16(2), 183-203:** propose a finer model of attention (alertness, orientation, detection) and argue that detection can occur without awareness; they agree attention matters but challenge awareness as required.
- **Robinson (1995):** reconceptualized noticing as detection plus rehearsal in short-term memory (Language Learning, 45(2), 283-331).
- **Gass (1997), Carroll (2001) and others:** noticing alone does not explain acquisition of abstract properties of grammar; input processing is richer than noticing.
- **N. Ellis (2005):** the hypothesis oversimplifies implicit learning; much learning is implicit and statistical.
- **Methodology:** how do you measure noticing without making it happen (reactivity)? Diary data is retrospective and subject to memory bias, and based on one learner (the author). Concurrent think-alouds may change processing (Bowles, 2010, meta-analysis of reactivity, as recalled).
- **Strong vs weak version:** "necessary and sufficient" is too strong. "Sufficient" has been widely abandoned: noticing something does not guarantee it is learned.
- **Recent work:** studies of formulaic sequences suggest retention can occur without focal awareness (as reported in a 2024 Acta Psychologica article, Szczesniak; summarized secondhand).

## What came after (follow-up studies, revisions, meta-analyses)
- Hulstijn & Schmidt (1994), AILA Review, 11, 5-10 (guest editors' introduction on consciousness): clarifies and softens positions.
- Schmidt (1995, Ed.), Attention and awareness in foreign language learning, University of Hawai'i Press.
- Schmidt (2001) "Attention": restates the hypothesis in a weaker form: people learn about things they attend to and do not learn much about things they do not attend to.
- Schmidt (2012) "Attention, awareness, and individual differences in language learning", in Chan et al. (Eds.), De Gruyter, 27-50.
- Williams (2005), SSLA: awareness and implicit learning in an artificial language; Leow (2015), Explicit learning in the L2 classroom, Routledge.
- Meta-analytic and review work on form-focused instruction (Norris & Ortega, 2000; Spada & Tomita, 2010) and on textual enhancement (Lee & Huang, 2008) tests related claims; results are mixed for enhancement alone and stronger for explicit instruction.

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> connection / critique angle)
- Paper on corrective feedback, recasts or prompts -> noticing is the mechanism usually invoked. Critique: did the study measure whether learners noticed the correction (uptake is not noticing).
- Paper on textual enhancement, input flood or processing instruction -> noticing hypothesis as rationale. Critique: salience may raise noticing without leading to acquisition.
- Paper uses think-aloud, stimulated recall, eye-tracking -> noticing measurement. Critique: reactivity and validity.
- Paper on task-based learning/focus on form -> link to Long (1991, 1996) and Schmidt's claim that attention to form is needed.
- Paper on output -> Swain's noticing function of output (noticing the gap).
- Paper with an implicit/statistical learning stance (usage-based, N. Ellis) -> contrast: learning without awareness.
- Paper using diaries/journals -> cite Schmidt & Frota (1986) and discuss strengths (insider data) and limits (one learner, retrospection).

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8 with short model answers)
1. **State the noticing hypothesis.** Input becomes intake only if learners consciously notice the relevant feature. "Subliminal language learning is impossible" (Schmidt, 1990).
2. **How is Schmidt's "intake" different from Krashen's?** Schmidt: the part of input that is noticed. Krashen: roughly comprehensible input that is processed unconsciously and drives acquisition.
3. **Distinguish noticing from understanding.** Noticing is registering a surface form; understanding is grasping the underlying rule or generalization. Only noticing is claimed to be required.
4. **What evidence did Schmidt use?** Cognitive psychology of attention and consciousness, plus his and Frota's diary study of learning Portuguese: forms entered his output after being noticed in input.
5. **Name two criticisms.** Truscott (1998): vague and weakly supported; Tomlin & Villa (1994): attention can operate without awareness; also retrospective, single-case data and measurement problems.
6. **How did Schmidt later revise the claim?** He weakened it: noticing facilitates learning, with the strong "necessary and sufficient" wording dropped (Hulstijn & Schmidt, 1994; Schmidt, 2001).
7. **How does the hypothesis support focus on form and feedback?** If attention is needed, instruction and feedback should draw attention to form while meaning is processed, which is what focus on form and corrective feedback aim to do.
8. **Is the paper empirical?** No, mainly a theoretical review with illustrative case data, so the claim is argued, not tested directly.
