# Communicative competence: roles of comprehensible input and output (Swain, 1985)

**One-line message:** Comprehensible input is not enough; learners also need to be pushed to produce language that others can understand, because producing forces them to process grammar.

## Gold nuggets (the 3-5 things everyone must know)
1. **Origin:** observation of Canadian French immersion students who, after years of massive comprehensible input, still produced non-native French, especially in grammar and sociolinguistic skills, although their comprehension was strong.
2. **Core claim (Comprehensible Output Hypothesis):** producing language pushes learners from easy "semantic" processing (getting the gist) to deeper "syntactic" processing (working out grammar), so output plays a role in acquisition beyond practice.
3. **"Pushed output"** is output that stretches the learner to be precise, coherent and appropriate, typically triggered by a listener's non-understanding.
4. **Direct challenge to Krashen (1982):** Krashen said speaking is the result of acquisition, not a cause; Swain said production contributes causally to accuracy.
5. **Later refinement (Swain, 1993, 1995, 2005):** output has three functions, noticing, hypothesis testing, and metalinguistic reflection. Output is not the replacement of input, it complements it.

## The problem it answered (context in that year)
- Krashen's Input Hypothesis (see krashen-1982-monitor-model.md) dominated 1980s SLA and claimed comprehensible input is the sole causal variable.
- French immersion programs in Canada (since the 1960s) were praised as proof of input-rich learning, yet evaluation research (Swain & Lapkin, 1982, *Evaluating Bilingual Education*; Harley & Swain, 1984) found that after years in immersion, learners had near-native listening and reading comprehension but persistent grammatical errors in speaking and writing and weaker sociolinguistic competence.
- The paper was written for a volume on input (Gass & Madden, 1985), arguing that a theory built only on input could not explain this plateau.

## The argument / design in plain language
**Nature of the paper:** a theoretical-argument paper drawing on data from Canadian immersion research (it is not a stand-alone experiment). The classroom evidence is from a Grade 6 early French immersion study in Ontario run by the Swain team (detail of sample size and instruments not verified here; check the chapter).

**Argument steps (paraphrase):**
1. Immersion classrooms give rich comprehensible input; students become strong in comprehension.
2. Yet their productive skills in French lag, with persistent morphosyntactic errors. So input alone is not sufficient.
3. Why? In immersion classes students often have limited opportunity to speak at length; teacher talk dominates; student answers are short, and the teacher understands "good enough" messages.
4. In comprehension, learners can succeed through context and vocabulary without analysing grammar (semantic processing).
5. In production, they must choose words, structure and morphology, so they are forced to use syntax (syntactic processing).
6. Therefore learners need opportunities to produce extended output and to be "pushed" to improve it (via clarification requests and feedback).

**Original formulation of three early functions of output (Swain, 1985, as developed in 1993-95):**
- Testing hypotheses about the language.
- Developing automaticity and fluency.
- Moving from semantic to syntactic processing.

**The three functions as usually cited (Swain, 1995; Swain & Lapkin, 1995):**
- **Noticing/triggering:** learners notice gaps between what they want to say and what they can say.
- **Hypothesis testing:** utterances test hypotheses about grammar; feedback confirms or disconfirms.
- **Metalinguistic (reflective):** talking about language helps learners control and internalise knowledge.

## Why it matters / impact (what it changed, citation influence)
- Created the **output hypothesis**, one of the three pillars of the input-interaction-output framework (Krashen input; Long interaction; Swain output).
- Rehabilitated speaking and writing as learning, not just display of what is already known, supporting task-based language teaching and collaborative tasks.
- Influenced classroom design (dictogloss, jigsaw, information gap tasks; Swain & Lapkin 1995; Swain, 1998 on focus on form via collaborative dialogue).
- A staple citation in studies on corrective feedback, peer interaction, and L2 writing.
- Swain's later turn to sociocultural theory (collaborative dialogue, languaging) shows how the output idea evolved.

## Known limitations and critiques (who, what)
- **Evidence base:** the 1985 paper rested on immersion observations; causal evidence needed experiments (Izumi, 2003; Izumi & Bigelow, 2000; details not verified here). Results are mixed: some find output helps noticing and accuracy, others find little added benefit over input.
- **Not a full theory:** it does not specify exactly how output changes the interlanguage system or which output helps.
- **Input processing alternatives:** VanPatten argues the primary mechanism is input processing; Krashen (1998, "Comprehensible output?" in *System*) argued the evidence for comprehensible output was weak and that reading and listening explain gains better; Truscott (1998) similar.
- **Output vs interaction:** hard to separate the effects of output from feedback/negotiation in interaction (Long, 1996; Mackey).
- **Task effects:** benefit depends on task type, learner proficiency, and quality of feedback; individual and cognitive differences matter.
- **Comprehensibility vs accuracy:** a learner can be understood with incorrect grammar, so non-understanding does not always trigger syntactic processing.

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> connection / critique angle)
- Paper claims "students need to speak/write to learn" -> cite Swain (1985, 1995); ask whether it tests noticing, hypothesis testing, or reflection.
- Paper uses collaborative or pair tasks -> connect to collaborative dialogue and languaging; ask how the dialogues are coded (language-related episodes).
- Paper contrasts input-only vs input + output groups -> position as test of the output hypothesis; look for control of time on task.
- Paper discusses immersion/CLIL outcomes -> use Swain's grammar-accuracy gap as background.
- Paper uses dictogloss or reconstruction -> relevant to pushed output.
- Paper critiques Krashen -> use Swain as the key empirical and conceptual counterpoint.
- Paper measures only speaking fluency -> remind that Swain's point concerns accuracy and syntactic processing.

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8 with short model answers)
1. **State the output hypothesis.** Producing language, especially when pushed to be precise and comprehensible, promotes SLA by forcing syntactic processing.
2. **What observation motivated Swain?** Canadian French immersion students had strong comprehension but non-native grammatical accuracy and weak sociolinguistic skills despite abundant input.
3. **How did Swain challenge Krashen?** She argued comprehensible input is not sufficient and output has a causal role, whereas Krashen saw output as a result of acquisition.
4. **Define pushed output.** Output that stretches learners beyond comfortable, gist-level production to be accurate, coherent and appropriate.
5. **What are the three functions of output?** Noticing/triggering, hypothesis testing, and metalinguistic (reflective).
6. **What is the difference between semantic and syntactic processing?** Semantic: understanding meaning using context. Syntactic: working out grammatical structure, needed in production.
7. **Give one critique.** The original evidence was observational; later experiments show mixed results and the effect is hard to separate from feedback (Krashen 1998; Long 1996).
8. **How did Swain's thinking evolve?** From cognitive processing in 1985 to collaborative dialogue and languaging within sociocultural theory (2000, 2006).

## Memory hooks and quick checks (for fast revision)
- **Trio to remember:** Krashen = input, Long = interaction, Swain = output. Together they form the input-interaction-output framework.
- **Memory image:** Canadian immersion kids can understand everything but still say "elle a allé". Their comprehension outran their grammar because understanding does not force you to process grammar.
- **Three functions mnemonic:** "N-H-M" = Noticing, Hypothesis testing, Metalinguistic.
- **Classroom example:** a dictogloss. Learners listen to a short text, take notes, then reconstruct it in pairs. When they cannot find the right form, they notice the gap, test options and talk about grammar. All three functions appear.
- **Key distinction:** "comprehensible output" is not just any talk. It is output that is understood and that pushes the speaker to use better language.
- **Do not overclaim:** Swain did not say output is more important than input. She said input is necessary but not sufficient.
- **Date caution:** Swain (1985) is the origin; the three-function version is mostly Swain (1995) and Swain & Lapkin (1995). Cite the right source for the right idea.
