# Rod Ellis
**One-line identity:** British-born applied linguist (long at Temple University Japan and the University of Auckland; later Curtin University, Perth) who is the main synthesizer of research on instructed SLA, with major work on explicit vs implicit knowledge, corrective feedback, and task-based teaching.

## Gold nuggets (the 3-5 things everyone must know)
1. **The bridge between SLA research and teaching.** His textbooks (*Understanding Second Language Acquisition*, *The Study of Second Language Acquisition*) and research reviews turn SLA findings into usable classroom principles.
2. **Implicit and explicit knowledge are different and both matter.** Implicit = intuitive, automatic knowledge used in fluent speech. Explicit = conscious, analyzable rules. He developed valid tests to measure each (Ellis, 2005, *SSLA*).
3. **Weak interface position.** Explicit knowledge can help implicit knowledge develop indirectly (e.g., by drawing attention), but conversion is not automatic, which places him between Krashen (no interface) and strong interface views.
4. **Ten principles of instructed language learning (Ellis, 2005, *System*).** A balanced list: formulaic expressions plus rules, meaning-focused input and output, focus on form, implicit and explicit knowledge, the learner's built-in syllabus, input, output, interaction, individual differences, free and controlled production assessment.
5. **TBLT with realism.** Distinguishes task-based language teaching (tasks are the unit) from task-supported language teaching (tasks add to a structural syllabus), and defends a task definition with clear criteria.

## Core ideas in plain language (idea -> what it means -> classroom example)
- **Instructed SLA** -> The study of how teaching and classrooms affect learning. -> Does explicit grammar teaching help learners more than meaning-only exposure?
- **Explicit vs implicit knowledge** -> Know the rule (explicit) vs use the form automatically (implicit). -> A learner can state the past perfect rule but still avoid it in chat.
- **Weak interface** -> Explicit knowledge can prime noticing and later proceduralization. -> Learner studies a rule, then notices it in a listening text.
- **Corrective feedback (CF)** -> Responses to learner errors: recasts, prompts, explicit correction, metalinguistic explanation. -> Teacher gives an explanation after "He go" vs repeating "He went."
- **Implicit vs explicit CF** -> Ellis, Loewen & Erlam (2006) compared recasts and metalinguistic explanation for past -ed. Explicit feedback tended to help more on measures of explicit knowledge.
- **Focus on form** -> Attention to form in meaning-based work. -> Brief review of question forms during a role-play.
- **Task** -> Activity with a gap, meaning focus, learner resources, and a non-linguistic outcome. -> Plan a trip given a budget.
- **Task-based vs task-supported** -> Tasks as the syllabus vs tasks to practice a grammar item. -> Strong vs weak forms; Ellis often supports the weak form for many contexts.
- **Formulaic sequences** -> Chunks learned whole ("How are you?"). -> Teach routines alongside rules.

## Evolution of their thinking (how positions changed over time)
- **1980s:** Classroom-based SLA research and textbook syntheses (1984, 1985). Early focus on whether instruction changes the route of acquisition (largely no) and on interaction.
- **1990s:** *Instructed SLA* (1990) and the argument that formal instruction can speed rate and increase attainment; expands attention to interaction and the role of tasks.
- **2000s:** Systematic work on implicit/explicit knowledge, with measurement (2005), and on form-focused instruction (2001 *Language Learning* special issue he edited, per memory, unverified). Publishes the ten principles (2005) and a TBLT book (2003).
- **2009 onward:** Book on implicit and explicit knowledge with colleagues (2009). Pushes for corrective feedback that is explicit and metalinguistic in certain conditions, with attention to learner difference and target structure.
- **2010s:** Defends TBLT against critics (Swan; Widdowson; Seedhouse) using a distinction between tasks and activities; supports both task-based and task-supported forms. Publishes *Reflections on task-based language teaching* (2018) and collaborates on a multi-author TBLT text (Ellis, Skehan, Li, Shintani, & Lambert; details unverified).
- **Continuing:** Prolific on written CF and teacher education; emphasizes that research cannot be directly prescriptive for teachers.

## Main critiques and debates (who pushed back, on what grounds)
- **Interface:** Strong interface proponents (DeKeyser, skill acquisition) say explicit knowledge can become automatic with practice; Krashen (no interface) says it cannot. Ellis sits in between, arguing the effect is mostly indirect.
- **Measuring implicit knowledge:** Critics question whether his tests (timed grammaticality judgement, oral imitation, elicited oral production) truly tap implicit knowledge; construct validity is still debated (Suzuki & DeKeyser; Vafaee et al., details unverified).
- **TBLT:** Swan argues it lacks grammatical structure; Seedhouse raises concern about pidginized language; Littlewood says it is hard for beginners; Block calls it largely theoretical with unspecified conditions (per Wikipedia).
- **Corrective feedback evidence:** Lyster & Ranta (1997) and Lyster & Saito favored prompts over recasts. Truscott (1996) argued against grammar correction in writing; Ellis supports focused written CF, arguing that meta-analyses show benefits.
- **Meta-analytic base:** Norris & Ortega (2000) and Spada & Tomita (2010) support explicit instruction, which Ellis uses, but critics note short-term, controlled-test limits.
- **Principles as prescriptions:** Critics argue general principles cannot account for context; Ellis acknowledges contexts differ.

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> what to say / connect / critique)
- Paper on grammar teaching, FFI, "explicit vs implicit instruction" -> Ellis (2006, 2009), Norris & Ortega (2000), Spada & Tomita (2010).
- Paper on feedback type (recasts vs prompts vs metalinguistic) -> Ellis, Loewen & Erlam (2006); Lyster & Ranta (1997); check what test measured (explicit vs implicit knowledge).
- Paper claims to test "implicit knowledge" -> ask which measure; cite Ellis (2005) and note ongoing validity debate.
- Paper proposes tasks or TBLT curriculum -> use Ellis's task features; ask whether it is task-based or task-supported.
- Paper offers pedagogical implications -> check against Ellis's ten principles (2005).
- Paper on formulaic language -> principle 1 (formulaic expressions plus rule-based competence).
- Critique angle: over-reliance on lab or short-term outcomes, mismatch between measures and constructs, lack of delayed post-tests.

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8, with 1-2 line model answers)
1. **Define implicit and explicit knowledge and give a test of each.** Implicit is automatic, tested by timed judgement or oral production; explicit is conscious, tested by untimed judgement with metalinguistic explanation.
2. **Where does Ellis stand on the interface?** Weak interface: explicit knowledge helps indirectly, mainly by prompting noticing.
3. **Name three of Ellis's principles of instructed language learning.** Focus on meaning and form, balance implicit and explicit knowledge, extensive input and output opportunities.
4. **What did Ellis, Loewen & Erlam (2006) find?** Explicit (metalinguistic) feedback tended to outperform recasts on measures of explicit knowledge; delayed differences were small (per Wikipedia summary; verify details).
5. **Task-based vs task-supported teaching?** TBLT builds the syllabus from tasks; task-supported teaching uses tasks to practice pre-selected structures.
6. **How does Ellis's view compare with Krashen's?** Ellis accepts a role for explicit knowledge and instruction; Krashen's no-interface position denies direct benefit.
7. **What criticism faces TBLT?** Grammar neglect, beginner difficulty, pidginization risk, thin curriculum-level evidence.
8. **Why is Ellis considered important?** He synthesizes and applies SLA research to pedagogy, and developed methods to measure implicit and explicit knowledge.
