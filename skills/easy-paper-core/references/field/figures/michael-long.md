# Michael H. Long
**One-line identity:** British-born applied linguist (c. 1945-2021; Hawaii, then Maryland) who made interaction and negative feedback central to SLA theory, coined "focus on form," and built the case for task-based language teaching grounded in needs analysis.

## Gold nuggets (the 3-5 things everyone must know)
1. **Interaction Hypothesis.** Conversation in which partners negotiate meaning (checking, clarifying, rephrasing) makes input understandable and also draws learner attention to language. It is where input and attention meet.
2. **Revised 1996 version.** Long added negative feedback (including recasts) as a key part of interaction, because it supplies the information about what is wrong that input alone lacks.
3. **Focus on form vs focus on forms.** Focus on form = brief attention to language inside a meaning-focused lesson, when a problem arises. Focus on forms = teaching a syllabus of isolated structures (PPP, drills). Long rejected the second and defended the first.
4. **TBLT is the approach he built.** Start from learners' real-world target tasks (via needs analysis), teach through pedagogic tasks, and use focus on form as a design feature, not a grammar syllabus.
5. **Not a lone input theorist.** He accepted that input matters (like Krashen) but argued interaction and negative evidence are needed.

## Core ideas in plain language (idea -> what it means -> classroom example)
- **Negotiation of meaning** -> When communication breaks down, speakers use clarification requests, confirmation checks, and comprehension checks. This modifies the interaction so input becomes understandable. -> Two learners doing a spot-the-difference task ask "You mean the red car?"
- **Interactional modifications beat linguistic simplification** -> Long (1983) argued that adjusting the conversation helps comprehension more than simplifying the language beforehand. -> Native speaker repeats and checks, rather than only using short words.
- **Negative evidence** -> Information that something is not possible in the target language. Recasts give it implicitly in context. -> Learner: "He go yesterday." Teacher: "He went yesterday?" and conversation continues.
- **Focus on form** -> Draw attention to language reactively while the main goal is meaning. -> During a group task, the teacher stops briefly to model the past form after repeated errors.
- **Task as the unit of syllabus** -> Syllabus built from target tasks (e.g., "book a hotel by phone"), not from grammar points. -> Course for hotel staff built on observed work tasks.
- **Needs analysis** -> Find out what learners need to do in the language before designing the course. -> Interviewing nurses and observing ward handovers.
- **Methodological principles (MPs)** -> Ten principles (e.g., use tasks, elaborate input, provide rich input, promote cooperative learning, individualize instruction). Verify exact list in Long (2015, ch. 5) before citing.

## Evolution of their thinking (how positions changed over time)
- **Late 1970s-early 1980s:** Doctoral work (UCLA) and 1980s papers show that native speakers adjust conversation with learners, and that these adjustments (not simplified grammar) relate to comprehension. This became the first Interaction Hypothesis, building on Krashen's input but stressing interaction.
- **Late 1980s:** Argues that purely input-based or purely formal teaching are both insufficient; introduces focus on form (1988 talk, 1991 chapter) as a third option.
- **1996:** Revises the Interaction Hypothesis: negative feedback, especially recasts, linked to learner attention is a central mechanism. Moves from "comprehensible input" language to "negative evidence."
- **1990s-2000s:** Develops TBLT with Crookes and others; stresses needs analysis and rejects the synthetic (grammar-item) syllabus (Long & Crookes). Wrote on the sensitive period (his 1990 SSLA paper is widely cited, unverified here).
- **2015:** Book-length synthesis of SLA research and TBLT, with an explicit "analytic" stance: the learner's internal syllabus matters; teaching should not try to override it. Continued advocacy of recasts and rich input.

## Main critiques and debates (who pushed back, on what grounds)
- **Is negotiation necessary?** Critics (e.g., Foster, 1998; Gass) note that learners often do not negotiate, and comprehension does not guarantee acquisition. Long's claim is facilitative, not necessary.
- **Recast ambiguity:** Learners may not notice a recast as correction (Lyster & Ranta, 1997 found few recasts led to repair). Lyster argues prompts (elicitation, clarification) are more effective; Long defends recasts as implicit and appropriately unobtrusive.
- **Mackey & Goo (2007)** meta-analysis supports interaction benefits (effect sizes reported as medium to large; exact numbers not verified here).
- **Sociocultural critique:** Researchers like Lantolf and Swain treat learning as co-construction in dialogue, not information exchange. Long's view is cognitive-interactionist and treats cognition as individual.
- **TBLT critiques:** Swan (2005) argued against exclusion of grammar teaching and weak evidence base; Seedhouse (1999) raised concerns about pidgin-like language; Littlewood challenged beginners' suitability. Ellis (2018) defends a weaker TBLT. Long's strong version (no grammar syllabus) is the most contested.
- **Early critique of operationalization:** "focus on form" in research is applied inconsistently (reactive vs planned); see Ellis (2016) for a review (reference not verified).

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> what to say / connect / critique)
- "Negotiation for meaning," "clarification request," "task-based interaction" -> Interaction Hypothesis (Long, 1983, 1996). Ask whether the study measures learning or only interactional moves.
- Recast / feedback study -> Long (1996, 2007) supports recasts; compare Lyster & Ranta (1997) and Ellis, Loewen & Erlam (2006). Check learner noticing.
- Paper says "focus on form" without definition -> clarify reactive vs proactive; differentiate from "focus on forms" (Long, 1991).
- TBLT curriculum study -> check needs analysis and whether tasks are target or pedagogic.
- Paper treats input as sufficient -> contrast with Long's emphasis on negative evidence.
- Critique angle: ecological validity of lab tasks, short delayed-post-test windows, individual differences (working memory, aptitude).
- Sociocultural paper -> frame difference: Long (information processing) vs Swain/Lantolf (mediation).

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8, with 1-2 line model answers)
1. **State the Interaction Hypothesis and its 1996 revision.** Interaction with negotiation makes input comprehensible and focuses attention; 1996 stresses negative feedback.
2. **Difference between focus on form and focus on forms?** Form attention inside meaning-focused work vs isolated structure teaching.
3. **How does Long relate to Krashen?** Both value input; Long adds interaction and negative evidence and rejects the "input only" claim.
4. **Why are recasts debated?** They are implicit, so learners may treat them as alternative utterances and not as corrections; evidence is mixed.
5. **What are the steps of TBLT?** Needs analysis, target tasks, pedagogic tasks, syllabus of tasks, focus on form, task-based assessment.
6. **What criticisms face TBLT?** Under-attention to grammar, difficulty for beginners, scant long-term curriculum evidence.
7. **What is negative evidence?** Information that a form is not allowed, as opposed to positive evidence of what is allowed.
8. **Where does Long stand versus Swain?** Both accept interaction; Long stresses input and feedback, Swain stresses learner output and dialogue as thinking.
