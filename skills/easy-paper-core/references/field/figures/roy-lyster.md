# Roy Lyster
**One-line identity:** McGill-based researcher who mapped how teachers correct errors in immersion classrooms (with Leila Ranta), showed that "prompts" push learners to self-repair more than recasts, and built the counterbalanced approach to teaching language through content.

Affiliation: McGill University (Montreal), Department of Integrated Studies in Education (recalled; not re-checked).

## Gold nuggets (the 3-5 things everyone must know)
1. **Lyster and Ranta (1997) coded six feedback types** in four primary French immersion classrooms: explicit correction, recast, clarification request, metalinguistic feedback, elicitation, repetition.
2. **Teachers recasted a lot (55% of feedback moves) but recasts led to little learner repair (about 18%).** Elicitation, metalinguistic feedback, clarification requests, and repetition led more often to student-generated repair ("negotiation of form").
3. **Recasts versus prompts is the big distinction.** Recasts give the correct form; prompts (elicitation, metalinguistic clue, clarification request, repetition) push the learner to produce it. Lyster and Saito's (2010) meta-analysis found prompts generally gave larger effects than recasts.
4. **Recasts are ambiguous in classrooms.** Learners often hear a recast as just another way to say it, so they do not notice the correction (Lyster, 1998).
5. **Counterbalanced approach:** In content-based/immersion teaching, alternate between meaning-focused work and form-focused work so that learners shift attention back and forth.

## Core ideas in plain language (idea -> meaning -> classroom example)
- **Uptake** -> A learner's immediate response after feedback. -> Teacher: "Il a mangé les pomme?" Student: "Les pommes."
- **Repair** -> A type of uptake in which the error is actually corrected. Distinguished from "needs repair" uptake when the error remains.
- **Negotiation of form** -> Feedback that pushes the learner to correct the form without giving the answer. -> "How do we say that in the past?"
- **Recast** -> Teacher reformulates the error without the error, usually without signaling it is a correction. -> Student: "He go to school." Teacher: "He goes to school."
- **Prompt** -> Cues that withhold the correct form and push self-repair (elicitation, metalinguistic clues, clarification requests, repetition). -> "Is that verb correct?"
- **Counterbalanced instruction** -> Teach so that learners' attention is shifted to the opposite of where it naturally falls. Content-focused classes need form attention; form-focused classes need meaning. -> A science lesson on weather gets a mini-lesson on conditional verbs.
- **Awareness-Practice-Feedback (APF) sequence** -> Noticing, then practice, then feedback for oral proficiency (Ranta & Lyster, 2007). -> Students notice past-tense forms in a text, rehearse them in role plays, and receive prompts.
- **Immersion** -> A program that teaches subject matter in the L2. -> Math in French in Anglophone Canada.

## Evolution of their thinking (how positions changed over time)
- **1997:** Descriptive work: documents how teachers actually correct and how learners respond, concluding recasts dominate but are least effective at eliciting repair.
- **1998:** Argues recasts are ambiguous in classrooms because learners may hear them as confirmation or as another way of saying something.
- **2004:** Quasi-experimental study in immersion shows prompts outperform recasts for gender-marking in French (recalled, check before citing).
- **2007:** Presents the counterbalanced approach as a pedagogical framework tying feedback to content-based instruction.
- **2010-2013:** Meta-analysis (Lyster & Saito, 2010) shows CF works and prompts beat recasts; the 2013 review with Saito and Sato then covers many studies. Lyster and Ranta (2013) answer critics by arguing for variety: different CF types serve different learners and contexts, rather than "prompts always win."
- **Later:** Integrates skill acquisition theory and practice into the counterbalanced approach (recalled).

## Main critiques and debates (who pushed back, on what grounds)
- **Recasts defenders.** Long and colleagues, and Goo and Mackey (2013, "The case against the case against recasts," *SSLA*), argue that recasts are effective under many conditions and that classroom data underestimate them. Goo and Mackey also argue that "uptake" and "repair" are poor measures of learning. (Recalled.)
- **Uptake is not learning.** Immediate repair may reflect short-term repetition, not acquisition (Mackey & Philp, 1998; Ellis & Sheen, 2006; recalled).
- **Measurement matters.** Prompts may look better because free-production tests match the type of response prompts require. Lyster and Saito (2010) themselves reported the advantage was largest on free constructed-response measures.
- **Context.** Immersion, foreign language, and adult labs differ. Nicholas, Lightbown, and Spada (2001) argued recasts' success depends on context and form-focus (recalled).
- **Explicit versus implicit.** Ellis, Loewen, and Erlam (2006) found metalinguistic explicit feedback more effective than recasts in a lab-like design (recalled).

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> what to say / connect / critique)
- Paper counts **feedback types** -> Map them to Lyster and Ranta's six types and to the recast/prompt split.
- Paper reports **"uptake" or "repair" rates** -> Note that uptake is not acquisition; ask whether there is a delayed post-test.
- Paper compares **recasts and prompts** -> Cite Lyster (2004) and Lyster and Saito (2010) for prompts' edge; cite Goo and Mackey (2013) for the pro-recast side; check the outcome measure type.
- Paper is set in an **immersion/CLIL/EMI** class -> Use the counterbalanced approach: is attention to form planned or only reactive?
- Paper is a **meta-analysis of CF** -> Ask about domain definition and search criteria (see Plonsky & Brown, 2015, on why meta-analyses of feedback differ).
- Paper on **age or context** -> Lyster and Saito (2010) found younger learners benefited more and setting (SL vs FL) did not matter.
- Critique angle: was feedback isolated in the design, or mixed with other instruction? Were test measures free-response (favoring prompts) or constrained?

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8, with 1-2 line model answers)
1. **Name the six feedback types and give one example.** Explicit correction, recast, clarification request, metalinguistic feedback, elicitation, repetition (see core ideas above for examples).
2. **What did Lyster and Ranta (1997) find about recasts?** They were the most frequent type (about 55%) but least likely to lead to learner repair (about 18%).
3. **Why are recasts ambiguous in classrooms?** Learners may take them as paraphrases that continue the conversation, especially when teachers also recast to confirm content.
4. **What is the difference between recasts and prompts?** Recasts supply the correct form; prompts withhold it and push self-repair.
5. **What did Lyster and Saito (2010) conclude?** CF has significant and durable effects; prompts beat recasts, especially on free-production measures; younger learners benefited more; second versus foreign setting did not matter.
6. **What is the counterbalanced approach?** Alternating meaning- and form-focused instruction so that attention is directed away from where it naturally sits.
7. **What are the limits of uptake as evidence?** It captures an immediate response, not long-term learning; it can be rote repetition.
8. **Why do meta-analyses of CF disagree?** Different domain definitions, search strategies, and outcome measures (Plonsky & Brown, 2015).
