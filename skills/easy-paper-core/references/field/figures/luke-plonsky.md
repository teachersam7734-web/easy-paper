# Luke Plonsky
**One-line identity:** Methodologist of second language research who audits how the field does quantitative work (meta-analysis, effect sizes, study quality, replication, open science) and pushes it to be more transparent and rigorous.

Affiliation: Northern Arizona University (Applied Linguistics). Roles listed on his SLRF 2019 profile include Associate Editor of *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, Managing Editor of *Foreign Language Annals*, and Co-Director of the IRIS repository (iris-database.org). Roles may have changed; check before citing.

## Gold nuggets (the 3-5 things everyone must know)
1. **Interpret effect sizes within L2 research, not with Cohen's generic labels.** Plonsky and Oswald (2014) analyzed effects from 346 primary studies and 91 meta-analyses (over 604,000 participants) and found Cohen's benchmarks generally underestimate L2 effects. They proposed field-specific benchmarks.
2. **The field has a study-quality problem.** Plonsky (2013, 2014) coded designs, analyses, and reporting in hundreds of studies and found common gaps: low power, little reporting of effect sizes and reliability, heavy reliance on p-values, and weak transparency. He called for reform.
3. **Meta-analysis is the tool for cumulative knowledge.** Rather than read single studies, pool them (e.g., Norris and Ortega, 2000; Plonsky's own strategy-instruction meta-analysis, 2011).
4. **Replication is rare.** Marsden, Morgan-Short, Thompson, and Abugaber (2018), in the same community, found about 1 published replication per 400 articles and recommended 16 changes. Plonsky is an active voice in this push.
5. **Open science is the fix.** Share materials (IRIS), preregister, use Registered Reports, report full statistics, and share data.

## Core ideas in plain language (idea -> meaning -> classroom example)
- **Effect size** -> How big a difference or relationship is, not just whether it is "significant." -> A grammar course raised scores by d = 0.80; that tells you more than p < .05.
- **Field-specific benchmarks** -> Calibrate "small, medium, large" against what L2 studies typically find. -> A d of 0.35 may be a typical effect for between-group classroom comparisons, not "small" in a trivial sense.
- **Statistical power** -> Chance of detecting a real effect. Small samples give low power and unstable results. -> A 12-per-group experiment might miss a real effect.
- **Methodological synthesis** -> A review that codes how studies were conducted, not what they found. -> Counting how many studies report reliability or effect sizes.
- **Meta-analysis** -> Statistical pooling of effects across studies. -> Pooling 50 experiments on corrective feedback to estimate the average effect.
- **Replication** -> Re-running a study (exact, approximate, or conceptual) to test whether findings hold. -> A teacher tries a published vocabulary technique with a new class.
- **Open science** -> Making materials, data, and plans public. -> Posting a questionnaire in IRIS so others can reuse and audit it.
- **Domain definition problem** -> Different meta-analyses on the same topic reach different results because they define the topic and search differently. -> "Why 18 meta-analyses of feedback have different results" (Plonsky & Brown, 2015).

## Evolution of their thinking (how positions changed over time)
- **2011:** First meta-analyses (strategy instruction) and a study of quality in interaction research show that methodological features relate to outcomes.
- **2013-2014:** Broad methodological syntheses diagnose the field's practices and call for reform: more power, effect sizes, reliability, and transparency.
- **2014:** Effect-size benchmarks specific to L2 research arrive, shifting interpretation from generic labels to field norms.
- **2015:** Edited volume on advanced quantitative methods turns critique into training (mixed models, robust methods, meta-analysis).
- **2016 onward:** Open science and IRIS: sharing materials, preregistration, Registered Reports, and later arguments that study quality is an ethical as well as technical matter (recalled, not re-checked).
- **2020s:** Methods forums and journals encourage transparency (SSLA Methods Forum; see Cambridge link).

## Main critiques and debates (who pushed back, on what grounds)
- **Benchmarks as new rules of thumb.** Even field-specific labels can be misused; Plonsky and Oswald themselves urge judging practical significance by context (theoretical maturity, manipulation strength, publication bias) rather than by label alone.
- **Meta-analysis limits.** "Garbage in, garbage out"; heterogeneity; publication bias; and domain definition choices lead to conflicting meta-analyses (Plonsky & Brown, 2015).
- **Quality checklists may be too narrow.** Not every good study is quantitative; critics from qualitative and sociocultural traditions note quality frameworks center experimental designs.
- **Preregistration fit.** Some argue it suits confirmatory experiments better than exploratory classroom or qualitative work.
- **Replication difficulty.** Marsden et al. (2018) found no exact replications and many design changes, making it hard to interpret failed replications.
- **Incentives.** Open science relies on journals and hiring committees valuing it.

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> what to say / connect / critique)
- Paper reports only **p-values** -> Ask for effect sizes with confidence intervals and cite Plonsky (2013, 2014).
- Paper calls an effect **"small/medium/large"** -> Check whether it uses Cohen's labels or L2-specific ones (Plonsky & Oswald, 2014).
- Paper has **small samples** -> Raise power and instability; ask for a power analysis or sensitivity analysis.
- Paper reports **no reliability** for tests or coders -> Flag it as a common quality gap.
- Paper is a **meta-analysis** -> Check domain definition, search strategy, inclusion criteria, handling of publication bias and moderator analysis.
- Paper is a **replication** -> Which type (exact, approximate, conceptual)? Was original material accessible? Marsden et al. (2018) found material access and author overlap related to successful replication.
- Paper shares **no materials or data** -> Suggest IRIS, OSF, preregistration or Registered Report formats.
- Critique angle: do the conclusions go beyond the effect size, sample, and design?

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8, with 1-2 line model answers)
1. **Why did Plonsky and Oswald (2014) propose new effect size benchmarks?** Because Cohen's generic labels underestimate typical L2 effects; interpretation should be anchored in the field's own distribution.
2. **What does a methodological synthesis do?** It codes research practices (designs, analyses, reporting) across many studies to show strengths and weaknesses, rather than synthesizing findings.
3. **What were Plonsky's main findings about study quality?** Frequent reporting gaps: limited effect sizes, reliability, power, and transparency, along with overreliance on p-values.
4. **Why do two meta-analyses on the same topic differ?** Different domain definitions, search methods, inclusion criteria, and coding choices (Plonsky & Brown, 2015).
5. **How common are replications in L2 research?** Rare: about one per 400 articles in Marsden et al.'s (2018) review of 67 replications from 26 journals.
6. **Name three open science practices.** Sharing materials (IRIS), preregistration, and Registered Reports; also data and code sharing.
7. **Why is statistical power important?** Low power makes real effects hard to detect and exaggerates the effects that are detected.
8. **Is a significant p-value enough?** No; it says nothing about the magnitude or practical importance of an effect, which requires effect sizes and context.
