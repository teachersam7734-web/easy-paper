# Stephen Krashen
**One-line identity:** American linguist (b. 1941, Chicago; PhD UCLA 1972; later professor emeritus at USC) whose five-hypothesis "Monitor Model" made comprehensible input the central idea of 1980s SLA and language teaching, and who still champions free voluntary reading.

## Gold nuggets (the 3-5 things everyone must know)
1. **Acquisition is not learning.** Acquisition = picking up language subconsciously through meaningful communication (like a child). Learning = conscious knowledge of rules. Krashen says only acquisition produces fluent speech.
2. **Comprehensible input is the only cause of acquisition.** Learners move forward when they understand messages slightly above their current level, called i+1. Speaking and grammar drills are not the cause; they are results or side effects.
3. **The affective filter** is an emotional "gate": anxiety, low motivation, low self-confidence raise the filter and block input from reaching the acquisition system.
4. **The no-interface position.** Learned knowledge never turns into acquired knowledge. Learned rules only work as a Monitor (an editor) when the learner has time, cares about form, and knows the rule.
5. **Most-cited, most-criticized.** The hypotheses are famous for being hard to test (circular) and for ignoring output and interaction. Krashen's influence on teaching (Natural Approach, extensive reading) stays large even though researchers rejected the strong claims.

## Core ideas in plain language (idea -> what it means -> classroom example)
- **Acquisition-Learning Hypothesis** -> Two separate systems. Acquired system initiates and drives speech; learned system only edits. -> A student who "knows" the past-tense rule from a worksheet still says "I goed" in free conversation: knowledge is learned, not acquired.
- **Natural Order Hypothesis** -> Grammar structures are acquired in a predictable order, regardless of teaching order (he drew on morpheme-order studies, e.g. Dulay & Burt, Bailey, Madden & Krashen). -> Third-person -s is taught early but acquired late.
- **Monitor Hypothesis** -> Conscious learning acts as an editor that checks output. It needs three conditions: time, focus on form, knowing the rule. -> A learner proofreading an email can use the Monitor; in fast chat it is unavailable.
- **Input Hypothesis** -> We acquire by understanding input containing structure a little beyond our level (i+1), using context and world knowledge. Speaking emerges on its own. -> Teacher uses pictures, gestures, and familiar topics so beginners follow a story told in the L2.
- **Affective Filter Hypothesis** -> Affective variables (motivation, self-confidence, anxiety) do not cause acquisition directly; they decide how much input gets in. -> Low-anxiety, friendly classroom with no forced early speaking (Natural Approach "silent period").
- **Comprehension Hypothesis (later name)** -> Krashen's later umbrella: we acquire language and develop literacy by understanding messages. Reading for pleasure is the strongest source. -> Free voluntary reading programs, graded readers, narrow reading/listening.

## Evolution of their thinking (how positions changed over time)
- **1970s:** Monitor Model emerges from morpheme-order studies and Dulay & Burt's work; Krashen's early papers set acquisition vs learning and the Monitor.
- **1981-1985:** Five hypotheses packaged in book form (1981, 1982, 1985). Input Hypothesis becomes the center. The Natural Approach (1983, with Tracy Terrell) turns theory into a method: comprehension first, speech emerges, low anxiety.
- **Late 1980s-1990s:** Shifts to reading research. Argues free voluntary reading explains vocabulary, spelling, and writing development (1989, 1993). Also promotes narrow reading and narrow listening (1996, *System*).
- **2000s:** Renames the central claim the Comprehension Hypothesis (2002 book, *The Comprehension Hypothesis and its rivals*), contrasting it with Skill-Building and Output Hypotheses. Cites studies of sustained silent reading and Beniko Mason's "story listening."
- **2010s onward:** Applies the view to technology (Krashen & Jarvis, 2014, "Is CALL obsolete?") and keeps defending the no-interface position against skill-acquisition theory.

## Main critiques and debates (who pushed back, on what grounds)
- **Untestable / circular (McLaughlin, 1978; Gregg, 1984):** acquisition vs learning cannot be independently measured, so "acquired" just means "unmonitored." Gregg (1984) argued the Monitor is vague and violates Occam's razor.
- **Input alone is not enough (Swain, 1985; White, 1987):** Swain's French immersion students received huge input but kept grammar errors, so output and form attention matter. White (1987) argued that comprehensible input does not give learners negative evidence about what is ungrammatical.
- **No-interface vs interface:** Skill-acquisition and cognitive approaches (DeKeyser; R. Ellis on explicit knowledge aiding implicit; Schmidt's noticing) say conscious knowledge can help acquisition. Krashen's strong no-interface stance is largely rejected, a weaker interface view is common.
- **Interaction (Long):** negotiation for meaning makes input comprehensible and so the Input Hypothesis is extended, but Long argues for more than input.
- **Instruction research:** Norris & Ortega (2000) meta-analysis found explicit instruction effective, against Krashen's claim of minimal teaching impact.
- **Schwartz (1993)** argued that explicit and negative data affect linguistic behavior rather than competence, a Chomskyan-side critique parallel to Krashen's in some ways but not the same.
- **Defense:** Krashen still argues that evidence for free reading is large and that critics test learning, not acquisition.

## Use this when analyzing a paper (trigger -> what to say / connect / critique)
- Paper says "comprehensible input," "i+1," "input-rich," or "extensive reading" -> name Krashen (1982, 1985); note the paper may not accept the strong claim that input alone suffices.
- Paper studies anxiety, motivation, or classroom climate -> connect to the Affective Filter; point out that later work (MacIntyre; Dörnyei) treats affect with measurable constructs rather than a "filter."
- Paper compares explicit vs implicit instruction -> Krashen is the "no interface" extreme; contrast with Ellis (2009) and DeKeyser.
- Paper adds output tasks or feedback -> position it against Krashen; cite Swain (1985) and Long (1996).
- Paper claims "natural order" -> check evidence base (morpheme studies, mostly cross-sectional); note later work on developmental sequences (Pienemann's Processability Theory) refined this.
- Critique angle: unfalsifiable key terms, over-reliance on adult/child analogy, limited empirical operationalization of i+1.
- Use carefully: do not cite Krashen as the current consensus; cite as a historical foundation and as an advocate of reading.

## Likely professor / exam questions (5-8, with 1-2 line model answers)
1. **List Krashen's five hypotheses.** Acquisition-learning, natural order, monitor, input, affective filter.
2. **What is i+1 and why is it problematic?** Input slightly above current level that drives acquisition; problem: i and +1 cannot be measured independently, so it is hard to test.
3. **What does "no interface" mean?** Learned knowledge cannot convert into acquired knowledge; it can only edit output.
4. **How does Swain's work challenge Krashen?** Immersion students got plenty of comprehensible input yet stayed inaccurate, so pushed output is also needed.
5. **Why did Gregg (1984) criticize the Monitor?** Terms are vague, claims not falsifiable, and the model adds unnecessary distinctions.
6. **How does the Affective Filter relate to classroom practice?** Reduce anxiety, avoid forced early speech, make input interesting; the Natural Approach follows this.
7. **What is Krashen's current emphasis?** Free voluntary reading and the Comprehension Hypothesis.
8. **Is Krashen's theory still used?** Teachers use ideas (input, low anxiety, reading); researchers treat the model as historically important but empirically weak in its strong form.
